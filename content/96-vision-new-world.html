The short version

The last chapter, and the one where its author concedes the most. Trying to describe what a new arrangement of the Web would be like, he remembers trying to describe the first one, when there were five websites and he was persuading somebody to make the sixth.

He could not imagine then what would be built. He says so, and then argues that this is a reason to build openly rather than a reason to wait, which is the thread running through all ninety-six chapters.

The question

The last chapter. Written in December 2024, and its subject is a difficulty the author has met before:

There has been a common problem in all this, though, that it is really hard for people to imagine what this new world will be like. The Vision of a new World, 2024

He then says where he has met it, and the passage is the best thing in the note because it is an argument against his own authority.

96.1 The admission

It was really hard to explain to people what it would be like once the web had taken off, when there are only a few websites and I was persuading them to make the sixth. I could say "imagine a world with all the online documents linked together and instantly available on the net", but even then I could not imagine Google search, Wikipedia, Github, and XKCD. The Vision of a new World, 2024

The person who designed the Web could not imagine what would be built on it. Then the conclusion he draws about what he was actually doing:

So I had to persuade a person to get involved with the web, even though neither of us could really imagine how exciting and powerful it would become. The Vision of a new World, 2024

This is the last word on a theme the collection has carried since Chapter 1. Modularity, evolvability, the test of independent invention and the principle of least power are all ways of building for people whose plans you cannot know. Here the author says plainly that he never knew them, including at the moment he was arguing for the thing.

96.2 What cannot be imagined this time

The note is honest about the state of deployment: the software is stable and exists in several compatible versions, some open and some commercial, and almost nobody is offering it to the public. One regional government provides stores to its citizens for a limited number of purposes.

96.3 The value of connection

The economic argument is made by borrowing a law about networks and applying it to something else:

By applying Metcalfe's law to interconnected Solid Apps, the article highlights the extra value generated. The Vision of a new World, 2024

The original claim is that a network’s value grows with the square of its members, because each can reach every other. Applied to applications over a shared store, the members are not people but programs, and what each gains is access to what the others produced. Two applications that both see your calendar can do something neither could do alone, and every application added is a new pairing with all the existing ones.

The framing of what follows from that is careful about ends and means:

The article underscores the economic potential of Solid-driven innovations, not as an end goal but as a byproduct of enhanced productivity and creativity, collaboration and compassion. The Vision of a new World, 2024

96.4 Communities of every size

The closing vision names the unit it cares about, and it is not the individual:

It envisions a future where empowered individuals and communities of all sizes work together to tackle global challenges, marking a paradigm shift in how we use technology to shape the world. The Vision of a new World, 2024

Communities of all sizes is Chapter 87’s requirement, stated as an outcome rather than as a design constraint. The whole architecture of Volume VI exists to make groups of every scale expressible, from a household sharing a list to a profession sharing a vocabulary, which is what a two-setting system of Chapter 74 cannot do.

96.5 How the book ends

Ninety-six chapters, written across twenty-eight years, ending with an admission that the author could not imagine what his own work would produce, and an argument that this is a reason to proceed rather than to wait.

That is the through line. Chapter 1 asks designers to choose the least powerful means and to assume someone else will independently invent the same thing. Chapter 16 asks them to design so that version four can read version five. Chapter 19 asks them to build interfaces for systems that have not been imagined. Every one of those is advice about acting under ignorance of what comes next.

The notes were never a plan. They are a record of decisions made by someone who knew he could not see far, choosing repeatedly to keep things open rather than to make them good, on the grounds that openness is what lets other people make them good later. The Web is the evidence that this works. Whether the rest of it works is not yet known, which is the condition every chapter here was written in.

What happened since

  • Public provision is still missing. No widely available service offers ordinary people a personal store of this kind, which the note identifies as the gap.
  • Government pilots continued. Regional and national programmes exploring citizen-held data expanded, still for limited purposes rather than as general storage.
  • Portability rights strengthened. European data regulation from 2024 and 2025 extended access and portability, which is the non-technical route Chapter 93 identified.
  • The imagination problem persists. It remains as hard to describe this arrangement to someone as the note says, and for the reason it gives: the examples that would make it obvious have not been built yet.
