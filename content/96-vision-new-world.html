The short version

Berners-Lee has been here before. Describing a new arrangement of the Web, he remembers describing the first one, when there were only a handful of websites and he was persuading somebody to make the sixth.

He could not imagine then what would be built, and cannot now. He says so, and then tries anyway to describe the new one.

The question

When there were only a few websites, Berners-Lee could tell people to “imagine a world with all the online documents linked together”. He could not imagine Google search, Wikipedia, GitHub or XKCD. In December 2024 he finds himself there again:

There has been a common problem in all this, though, that it is really hard for people to imagine what this new world will be like. The Vision of a new World, 2024

96.1 The admission

“I’ve been here before,” the note says, and draws a lesson from the first time:

So I had to persuade a person to get involved with the web, even though neither of us could really imagine how exciting and powerful it would become. The Vision of a new World, 2024

With Solid, “it is now, again, hard to imagine what it will be like.” Developers, he writes, “can’t imagine a world where one universal data store stores all the different types of data in the world, without being reconfigured.”

96.2 What cannot be imagined this time

The new arrangement is Solid: each person, or community, keeps data in a pod, a personal store on the Web that any application they permit can read and write. Today a chat server, such as Bluesky’s or Threads’, stores only messages; Dropbox stores any file but takes minutes to sync (Figure 96.1).

The pod software is stable, in several compatible versions, some open and some commercial, but almost nobody offers it to the public. The Flanders government provides pods to its citizens, for a limited number of purposes.

96.3 Why one app is not enough

Bob Metcalfe, inventor of Ethernet, proposed that a network’s value grows with the square of its members: a telephone is worth more the more people it can call. The note applies this to applications sharing a pod:

In the vision of the world where everyone does everything with Solid apps on Solid pods, the extra value is not just from the number of apps a person uses. The apps all connect with each other, in a network of apps -- and so the extra value from those connections goes as the square. Hence the difficulty in people seeing the point of getting Solid for just one app. The Vision of a new World, 2024

People advise picking a killer app and rolling it out; the note answers that everyone’s killer app is different.

A glimpse exists inside one large platform: an email in Gmail asks for a meeting, and a couple of clicks add it to Google Calendar and send everyone a Google Meet link. Solid would offer that integration across every application, not only within one company’s.

Linking across applications

The Solid slogan is “you can do anything with anything”. Today an event lists invitees from the user’s contacts, but from a contact there is no way to find their events. Figure 96.3 shows what changes when links run both ways.

A colleague, John Bruce, compares it to “imagining you could actually trust all the apps on your phone”. Planning a trip, he told an airline’s app which flight he wanted, so it knew where he would be and when, yet it never told Google Maps where he needed to be at the airport.

What changes for users

The first change is relief; people, the note suggests, “may adapt very quickly to the new freedoms.” A photo album on Flickr could be shared with friends on Facebook and colleagues on LinkedIn at once, because the platforms have become Solid-compatible or users have moved to open ones. Medical records could move into a person’s own store, to be analysed by apps and AI and shared with chosen doctors and family. “The large medical software providers do not come out shining with virtue historically,” the note remarks, but at least four groups were writing code to bring medical data into pods.

Second, new things appear. “No code” apps, driven by a data file and assembled by dragging components, are easy for users to copy and tweak. The author asked an AI where integrating personal data would bring the greatest gains, and printed seven of its twenty answers. He notes LLM firms reaching for personal data too: Anthropic’s Model Context Protocol, Perplexity’s purchase of Carbon. Charlie, the assistant of Chapters 93 and 94, would need such access, but working for its owner.

Who pays

Pods could be paid for in familiar ways: free at first with heavier users paying, as with cloud storage; bundled with an email account or a club membership; or run by a co-operative. But the note declines the larger question:

So don't ask "what is the business model for Solid"? It's like asking "what is the business model for the web?". The Vision of a new World, 2024

As with the dot-com boom, he expects more new business models than anyone can imagine.

96.4 Communities of every size

The note’s conclusion names the unit it cares about, and it is not the individual. Newly empowered individuals work in communities “of all scales, from pairs through groups to nation-sized movements”, and this “fractal blend of community” is, he writes, “the best situation for individuals solving problems”.

The Web was always meant for collaboration: one person may have a problem in mind and another, across the planet, the solution. What carries it across is overlapping communities; he lists his own, from family to W3C groups and companies. Today, passing something between them means copying a message between WhatsApp, Slack and email. ActivityPub applications are starting to make this smooth for chat; Solid, he says, could do it for every type of data.

96.5 Building what cannot be pictured

Ninety-six chapters, written from 1996 to 2025, end with an admission that the author could not imagine what his own work would produce, and an attempt, made “with some humility”, to describe it anyway. Three early pieces of advice are all about acting without knowing what comes next:

ChapterAdviceWhat it assumes cannot be known
1Choose the least powerful means; assume someone else will independently invent the same thingWhat others will do with the work, or build beside it
16Design so that version four can read version fiveWhat the next version will contain
19Build interfaces for systems that have not been imaginedWhat will be connected later

Read across the collection, many of the notes record decisions taken by someone who said he could not see far, and who kept things open so that others could improve them. Whether that approach will work a second time is not yet known. What it aims at is the note’s own closing picture: “groups getting ideas they need from other groups a few clicks away in the social graph.”

What happened since

  • The Flemish pods have an operator. Athumi, the data utility company set up by the Flemish government and launched in 2023, runs the citizen pods the note mentions.
  • Portability rights strengthened. The EU’s Digital Markets Act obligations on the largest platforms, including data portability, applied from March 2024, and the Data Act, which gives users access to data from their connected devices, applied from September 2025. This is the non-technical route Chapter 93 identified.
