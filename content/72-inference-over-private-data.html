The question

Written in 2023, this note opens with an epigraph that is itself part of the argument: a passage about private data and artificial intelligence, attributed to a conversational system rather than to a person. Berners-Lee quotes the machine and then spends the note examining whether what it says is achievable.

The question is what an assistant that genuinely works for you would need, and why it cannot simply be given your data.

72.1 What the launch changed

He dates the shift precisely and describes its effect on expectations:

As a result, there was a tendency on the ensuing months to assume that any powerful AI would be packaged and exposed in the same way. Inference from Private Data, 2023

Three shapes had become the assumed shapes: a generator of images from a prompt, a generator of text from a prompt, and a conversational assistant. In each case a person types, the system answers, and the arrangement of who benefits is fixed by the packaging rather than by the technology.

The warning of the period is recorded without editorialising: people were told not to discuss confidential things with such systems, because there was no guarantee that what they shared would not be used by the company running it.

72.2 Rewiring, not rebuilding

The note’s framing is the useful part. What is wrong is not the model but the wiring:

From the point of view of privacy and user empowerment, to be a beneficent app, an improved wiring of the LLM would involve all of the insights and benefit being available to the user themselves. Inference from Private Data, 2023

Same machinery, different arrangement of who it serves. Chapter 92 is the general form of that argument, and this is its application to the case where the input is a person’s own life.

72.3 The observation that is the heart of the note

Berners-Lee reports a journalist’s experience of granting such a system access to a personal mailbox, and notes that it did not gain the insight a person would have. Then he asks why, and the answer is the most interesting thing in the chapter.

Berners-Lee states the obstacle without softening who could overcome it:

Well, it isn't unless you are a very large platform willing to train on all its users, or a state-run system in an autocratic state. Inference from Private Data, 2023

Two parties can do it, and both are the parties one would least want doing it. The capability and the concentration arrive together.

72.4 The proposal: groups rather than everybody

The way out offered is neither the whole private Web nor nothing:

One way to proceed is, rather than doing inference over the whole private web, to do it over a subset, and specifically a group of people who have something in common which gives them a specific inventive to share their data to a achieve greater insight. Inference from Private Data, 2023

The example is patients with a shared condition pooling data in order to learn about treatments, which Berners-Lee calls a classic use case that has been around a long time because it is motivating. The incentive to share is specific, mutual and legible to everyone involved.

What such an arrangement requires is social rather than technical, and he says so: a construction in which the level of trust is raised enough that individuals are willing. Chapter 66’s argument about modelling real trust is what this needs, and Chapter 22’s account of how a definition becomes shared is how such a group would form.

72.5 The warning about siloing

One observation is easy to pass over and is the note’s most durable prediction:

Obviously this is going to work most easily when you have a lot of personal data with the same provider and the same provider as your AI, and so the risk of further monopolization and data siloing is increased. Inference from Private Data, 2023

An assistant works best where your data already is. That gives whoever holds the most of your data the best assistant, which draws more data to them. The silo of Chapter 54 acquires a new reason to exist, and a much stronger one than convenience.

What happened since

  • Assistants were wired to personal data, by the platforms that hold it. Every large provider connected its assistant to the mail, documents and calendars it already stored, which is the concentration section 72.5 predicts.
  • The shown-against-trained distinction was partly closed. Retrieval at question time, fine-tuning on small corpora, and long contexts narrowed the gap, without supplying the general understanding of a private genre that Figure 72.1 describes.
  • Group-scale sharing grew slowly. Data trusts and patient collectives exist, and remain rare relative to the platform arrangement, for the reason given here: the social construction is the hard part.
  • Training on private data without training on it became a field. Federated and privacy-preserving learning are attempts to obtain the wisdom of section 72.3 without collecting the corpus.
