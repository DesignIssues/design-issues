The question

Written in August 2009 and sent as an email to the Technical Architecture Group's list, this note is the plainest thing Berners-Lee wrote about the Semantic Web's central terminological mess. It is placed after Chapters 7 and 8 because it explains how the argument they record came to be possible: a single word, “resource”, acquired four meanings over fifteen years, and each community inherited the one that suited it.

It is also the most candid chapter in Volume II. Berners-Lee describes a position he held, the pressure that broke it, and his decision to give way.

9.1 Four meanings, in order of arrival

The history is short enough to state as a sequence, and the sequence is the argument.

WhenWhoWhat “resource” meant
1990The first Web specificationNothing: the term was “document”, in Universal Document Identifier
early 1990sThe IETF online-information communityA useful thing out there: a page, a file, a film
late 1990sRESTA time-varying mapping between a URI and a representation
1999 onRDFAnything at all; rdf:Resource is the class of everything
2004The Architecture of the World Wide Web“Information resource” for a page; “resource” for anything

The first move is recorded without ceremony. The letter changed from D to R because some felt “document” was not wide enough to include things like films. Berners-Lee observes that the URI specification then wisely declined to constrain what a resource could be, which left the word open for everything that followed.

On the gap that opened up, he is precise about who was and was not obliged to close it:

There was no real definition of what a resource or document was -- maybe because it seemed obvious. The HTTP spec did not even specify whether the URI denoted a person or a document about them, it just explained that the thing returned representation of the resource. A Short History of “Resource” in web architecture, 2009

9.2 Why REST was not enough

The assessment of Roy Fielding's formalisation is generous and limited in the same sentence. A resource as a time-varying mapping from URI to representation was, Berners-Lee says, good enough for HTTP, and not enough for the architecture document to describe how the Web actually worked.

What was missing was the social expectation:

yes, the mapping from URI to representation is a function of time, but not just any old one -- a random function is not typically very useful. There are expectations about it can change with time. Persistence, consistency, with various common patterns which allow the web to be a useful medium. A Short History of “Resource” in web architecture, 2009

This is Chapter 3's conclusion arriving in a different vocabulary. A formal definition can say what a resource is. It cannot say what makes one worth relying on, and reliance is what the Web runs on.

9.3 Webizing, stated in one line

The note contains the clearest statement of the mechanism by which RDF attached itself to the Web without changing it:

The web architecture gave all these languages a common way of building a global identifier for the thing denoted by a local identifier in a given document. The semantics of the hash sign are defined web-wide to mean that “a#b” can be used to denote whatever is denoted by “b” in the document denoted by “a”. A Short History of “Resource” in web architecture, 2009

A graphics language may use local identifiers for lines and points. HTML uses them for anchors. RDF uses them for people and concepts. None of these required a change to HTTP, because each language is entitled to say what its own local names denote, and the hash supplies the same globalising step to all of them. Berners-Lee's verdict on the result is two words: worked a treat.

9.4 Two snags, and a concession

Then the account turns, and it is worth reading slowly because it is an author describing the defeat of his own preference.

The first snag was that many RDF publishers understood that HTTP URIs were the right kind of identifier but not that the document had to be put online for anyone to look up what a term meant. Having skipped that, they had no reason to keep the hash either.

The second was structural rather than careless. Some large systems found the hash unworkable because the natural document size was wrong in both directions: sometimes one term per file, sometimes a million, with no comfortable way to divide the vocabulary into middle-sized pieces. Others simply disliked how it looked.

The consequence is stated without softening:

I among others pushed back against using non-hash URIs for arbitrary things his but eventually gave in. A Short History of “Resource” in web architecture, 2009

And the nature of what followed is described more bluntly here than anywhere else in the collection. The specification was not changed. The specification was interpreted. Berners-Lee calls the resolution a truce, negotiated between the existing HTTP specification, the systems already built, and the people who wanted hashless URIs for people. His summary of the outcome is the fairest short assessment of httpRange-14 anyone has written:

It isn't a very bad design, nor very beautiful. Other designs would have worked, but that one was the only one which didn't have major problems for some community. A Short History of “Resource” in web architecture, 2009

9.5 The proposal at the end

The note closes with a recommendation that was never adopted and is still the best available advice: abandon the word. Berners-Lee suggests editing the documents to use Document and Thing and to eliminate Resource, while acknowledging why no English word will fit.

the web is a new system, a design in which new concepts are created, so we can't expect english words to exist to capture exactly the concepts. So we take those nearby and abuse them as little as we can as far as we can tell at the time, and then write them in initial caps to recognize that that is what we have done A Short History of “Resource” in web architecture, 2009

That last clause is a general rule for reading this entire book. Every capitalised term in Web architecture is an ordinary word under protest.

What happened since

  • The word was not eliminated. “Resource” remains in RFC 3986, in the HTTP specifications, and in RDF, carrying different senses in each. The proposal in section 9.5 has never been acted on.
  • The Web Ontology Language had already moved. As the note records, OWL uses owl:Thing for the universal class rather than rdf:Resource, which is the substitution Berners-Lee recommends, made in one vocabulary a decade before he recommended it generally.
  • The truce held. No serious attempt to reopen httpRange-14 has succeeded, which is the outcome this note advocates when it says that continuing to debate it as an open issue is expensive.
  • The first snag never went away. Vocabulary URIs that do not dereference to a definition remain common, which is the failure mode described here: publishers adopting the form of the practice without the obligation that makes it work.
