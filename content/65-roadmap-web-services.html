The question

Volume VI closes with the note Berners-Lee wrote in October 2002 about the technology everyone was excited by, and his first move is to object to its name.

The Web in Web Services is, from the first point, a misuse: the term Internet Services would be more appropriate. Web Services, 2002

The complaint is not pedantry. The whole note is about what the word Web is supposed to mean, and what is lost when a system built on Web protocols abandons the thing that made them work.

65.1 Three common themes

He begins by summarising what everyone agreed on, since little else was agreed:

ThemeWhat it implies
A departure from a quasi-static information space to one where interactions are primaryThe model of Chapter 61, changing under everyone
Use of existing Web standards as building blocksWell understood and well debugged, so worth reusing
A focus on operations within and between enterprisesWhat makes the requirements different

The first point is the one that earns the objection to the name. A system where interactions rather than documents are primary is not the Web, whatever it is built from. The second is why the Web's protocols were used anyway. And the third produces the requirement that separates this from ordinary remote procedure calls:

The fact that data is exchanged for business purposes and between different social entities means that accountability is required, rather than just reliable transmission. Web Services, 2002

Reliability is a property of the network. Accountability is a property of a record that survives the exchange and can be produced afterwards by either party. That is the paper trail of Chapter 62, and it is the requirement that distinguishes a business message from a function call.

65.2 What the vendors wanted

One sentence, offered without comment, explains most of what happened to this technology:

The vendors of software see web services as way to repackage existing capability in a way which makes it interoperable with other systems. Web Services, 2002

Not to build something new: to wrap what already existed. That motive predicts the shape of the result, which is a stack of specifications for describing, securing, routing and transacting things that were already being described, secured, routed and transacted, now with a common envelope.

65.3 The discovery cloud

Reducing the architecture to the interaction is the same move as Chapter 19: find the small interface everything else is built on, and refuse to let accessories into it. If discovery were essential, every party would need a registry, and the registry would be the centre.

65.4 Where he sends the reader instead

The philosophy section does not argue. It lists earlier notes, and the list is a summary of the whole volume's position on interaction.

It points at the axioms, for the information space concept. At Chapter 62, for the relationship between read-write state and read-only documents, with a question that is the best one-line statement of the issue: which came first, the journal or the database? And at Chapter 61, for the trend away from shared information spaces across many fields at once.

The answer to the journal question is the paper trail's answer. The journal came first, and the database is a convenience computed from it. A system that keeps only the database has thrown away the thing that makes accountability possible, which is precisely what section 65.1 says these systems require.

65.5 Why this closes the volume

Volume VI has described an architecture in which data is named, published, linked, written back, arranged, watched and reacted to, all in an open space that anyone may extend. This chapter is the contemporary alternative: the same protocols, used to build closed interactions between named parties, with the information space removed.

Berners-Lee treats it fairly, notes what it genuinely needs that the Web does not supply, and records his objection in the first paragraph. The two approaches were competing for the same attention in 2002 and the outcome is recorded below.

What happened since

  • The stack was built and then abandoned. The full suite of specifications was completed through the 2000s and largely displaced in the 2010s by simpler resource-oriented interfaces exchanging JSON, which is closer to the architecture of Chapter 49 than to this one.
  • The objection to the name was vindicated. The term fell out of use, and what replaced it is called an interface or an endpoint, with no claim about the Web.
  • Discovery stayed optional, as predicted. The universal registry envisaged at the time was shut down in 2006, and service discovery is now done by configuration and by documentation.
  • Accountability came back separately. Signed messages, audit logs and event sourcing supply what section 65.1 says these exchanges require, arrived at independently of this architecture.

Connections

The trend away from shared state is Chapter 61, and the trail that supplies accountability is Chapter 62. The information space concept is Chapter 2, and the remote-operations argument is Chapter 5. The alternative this volume has been describing is Chapters 49 to 58.

Questions

65.1 Accountability is distinguished from reliability. Name what a system must keep to provide the first.

65.2 Which came first, the journal or the database? Answer for a system you maintain, and say what you would lose by keeping only one.

65.3 Drawing discovery as a cloud keeps it optional. Name a system where an optional component became mandatory, and what that cost.

65.4 Repackaging existing capability predicts the shape of the result. Test that claim against a standards effort you have watched.
