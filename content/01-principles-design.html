The short version

Before anyone builds something that other people will build on in turn, the same handful of choices arrive. Simple, or capable? Strict about what is accepted, or forgiving? Control kept in one place, or given away?

Berners-Lee had answered these questions many times in arguments and had never written the answers down. This is the note where he does: six rules, two from software engineering, two from the Internet, and two he found the Web needed. They are simplicity and modularity; tolerance and decentralization; and the test of independent invention and the principle of least power. The rest of this book keeps applying them.

The question

By 1998 Berners-Lee had been arguing about Web design for seven years, and had noticed that the same appeals to good practice kept recurring in arguments without ever being written down. This note exists to give those appeals an address. Its opening sentence is unusually candid about the motive:

Again and again we fall back on the folklore of the principles of good design. Sometimes I need a URI for them so this is started as collection of them. Principles of Design, 1998

That is a working note, not a manifesto, and the author says so: he calls the content “largely motherhood and apple pie” while insisting it still needs a home. The chapter is placed first because everything after it is an application of one of these six principles to a particular problem.

1.1 Three pairs, three traditions

The six principles are not a flat list. Berners-Lee groups them by where they come from, and the grouping is the most useful thing in the note. Simplicity and modularity, he says, are “the stuff of software engineering”. Decentralization and tolerance are “the life and breath of Internet”, and he credits Brian Carpenter’s RFC 1958, Architectural Principles of the Internet, of June 1996. The remaining pair, the test of independent invention and the principle of least power, he claims as the ones he found he needed for the Web specifically.

The three layers explain why the principles sometimes conflict. The software-engineering pair is about the system one is building. The Internet pair is about the network one is building on. The Web pair is about the systems other people will build later, which do not exist yet and cannot be consulted.

1.2 Simplicity, and what it is not

The definition offered is narrow and worth keeping: “A language which uses fewer basic elements to achieve the same power is simpler.” Berners-Lee then separates this from familiarity. A two-line solution that uses recursion (a function that calls itself) is simpler than a ten-line one that avoids it with an explicit loop, even where some readers find the longer version easier to follow.

The worked example is XML. An XML document is built from two kinds of thing: elements, the named and nested pieces such as <p>, and attributes, the name-and-value settings attached to them, such as the class="x" in <p class="x">. Processing instructions are a third kind, the constructs that begin with <?, such as <?xml-stylesheet …?>.

His objection is not that processing instructions are large but that they are contagious. They complicate what was a clean design of elements and attributes, and so every specification that refers to XML processing must afterwards say what it does about them as well. Simplicity here is measured in other people’s specifications, not in one’s own.

1.3 Modularity, and being a module

The first half is conventional. Group features so that the bindings within a group (the ways one feature depends on another) are tight, and the bindings between groups are loose. The groups then become the units of independent change: one person can alter one part, understanding and testing only that part, while others alter other parts at the same time.

Berners-Lee adds the standard warning. Modularity depends on a simple, abstract interface between the modules. A design in which each module must know the insides of the others is not modular but merely partitioned.

The second half is the part that is rarely met, and he flags it as such:

It is also necessary to realize that your own system, no matter how big and wonderful it seems now, should always be designed to be a part of another larger system. This is often much more difficult than modularity. Principles of Design, 1998

This is modularity turned inside out, and it is the bridge to the fifth principle. A system designed to be a component of something unspecified must do one thing, must leave the rest alone, and must not claim to be the centre. The note calls this corollary “the TOII”, its shorthand for the test of independent invention.

1.4 Tolerance, and its cost

The rule is stated in quotation marks in the note: “Be liberal in what you require but conservative in what you do”. It is usually credited to Jon Postel, who edited many of the early Internet protocol specifications and put a version of it into the specification of TCP in 1981. The note does not name him; it says only that the rule applies “pretty well in life” as well as across the Internet.

The note gives its own concrete case: “Write HTML 4.0-strict. Accept HTML-4.0-Transitional (a superset of strict).” HTML 4.0 came in two main versions. Strict left out the older presentational features; Transitional kept them, so every Strict document is also a Transitional one. A careful program sends the narrower kind and accepts the wider.

Berners-Lee records the objection as well as the rule, which is why the section survives re-reading. When browsers are lax about what they expect, the system works better, but the laxness teaches page authors to be lax too.

He then draws the limit. Tolerance in a program is never a licence for vagueness in a specification. The specification must still draw a precise line between conformance (meeting every requirement it sets) and non-conformance, whatever programs then choose to accept across that line. Nor is tolerance an excuse for a product that breaks a standard.

1.5 Decentralization, including of concepts

The network argument is familiar: a common point involved in every operation limits how far the system can grow, and is a single point at which all of it can fail. The extension is the part worth carrying forward. Berners-Lee applies decentralization to vocabulary as well as to infrastructure.

His example is a knowledge representation system, a system that records facts in a form a program can reason with. Suppose it requires anyone who uses the concept of an automobile to use the one term http://www.kr.org/stds/industry/automobile. It then works only for those whose idea of an automobile matches that one formulation. The Semantic Web, he writes, must avoid such conceptual bottlenecks just as the Internet avoids network bottlenecks.

This is the seed of the argument that runs through Volumes IV and VI: that the Semantic Web must let anyone mint a term, and must solve agreement by mapping between vocabularies rather than by mandating one.

1.6 The test of independent invention

The test is a single question: “If someone else had already invented your system, would theirs work with yours?” Put another way, does this system have to be the only one of its kind? The note says the test is described in more detail in another of the Design Issues notes, Evolution, and adds three points of its own.

First, the test is “modularity inside-out”: designing a system not to be modular in itself, but to be one part of a larger system that has not yet been specified (the third panel of Figure 1.2). Second, such a system should try to do one thing well, and leave other things to other modules. Third, it must avoid conceptual and other centralization, because “no two modules can claim the need to be the unique center of a larger system”.

Chapter 2 shows the test applied to the Web’s own foundation, where Berners-Lee argues that URI space passes it because nothing prevents a rival universal space from existing and enveloping URI space as a subset.

1.7 The principle of least power

This is the longest section of the note. It turns on the difference between two kinds of language. A declarative language states what is the case and leaves the reader to decide what to do with it; HTML says “this is a heading”. A procedural language is a list of steps for a computer to carry out, as in Java or C.

Berners-Lee sets out a scale of five rungs, shown in Figure 1.5. A language is Turing complete if it can in principle express any computation at all. Two of the examples need a gloss: Dublin Core is a standard set of library-catalogue fields, and content negotiation (“conneg”) is where a server picks a format for the client. The note places PostScript and PDF on the rung that verges on complete, with the aside that PostScript in fact is and PDF is not. XSLT and SQL are complete, though “one is led not to use them that way”.

The powerful end of the scale is attractive: an open-ended hook into which anything can be placed. The profession spent the 1960s to the 1980s trying to move up it. His claim is that for data on the Web the incentive runs the other way:

The reason for this is that the less powerful the language, the more you can do with the data stored in that language. If you write it in a simple declarative from, anyone can write a program to analyze it in many ways. Principles of Design, 1998

The comparison offered is weather data. Published as a table of values with a declarative description, it can be averaged, plotted, combined with other sources, and queried in ways its publisher never considered. Published through a Java applet, a small program that the browser downloads and runs inside the page, it may look better and can be analysed by nobody: the only way to discover what an applet means is to run it in front of a person.

He then gives the design decision this principle was extracted from:

I chose HTML not to be a programming language because I wanted different programs to do different things with it: present it differently, extract tables of contents, index it, and so on. Principles of Design, 1998

A follow-up added later in the life of the page credits Meredith Patterson’s talk The Science of Insecurity with the point that the principle of least power is also important for the security of interfaces that may be exposed to attack.

What happened since

  • Least power was promoted. It left this personal note and became a W3C Technical Architecture Group finding, The Rule of Least Power, edited by Berners-Lee and Noah Mendelsohn, published in February 2006.
  • The security reading became a field. Patterson and Len Sassaman’s language-theoretic security (LangSec) work, presented from 2011, argued that parser expressiveness is itself the attack surface. It holds that inputs an interface accepts should be in a language simple enough to be decidable: simple enough that a program can always tell whether a given input is valid.
  • Tolerance was reconsidered. The Internet Architecture Board published RFC 9413, Maintaining Robust Protocols, in June 2023, arguing that liberal acceptance degrades interoperability over time because divergent implementations are never corrected. The 1998 objection recorded in this note is the same objection, sustained.
  • The tolerance argument was settled differently on the Web. HTML5, whose first W3C Recommendation came in October 2014, specified error handling exactly, so that lax input produces one defined result rather than many. That is neither strictness nor tolerance but a third answer the 1998 note does not anticipate.

Where these principles are used

Decentralization and independent invention are applied to URI space in Chapter 2. Modularity in the inside-out sense becomes the independence axioms of Chapter 4. Simplicity and the cost of extra constructs return in Volume III, in particular in Chapter 16 on evolvability and Chapter 19 on modularity. Least power is the argument beneath the whole of Volumes IV and V, and is stated again at the opening of Chapter 5.
