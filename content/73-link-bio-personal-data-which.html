The question

Written in February 2024, this note identifies a category that falls between the two everyone discusses:

Much has been written about around open public data and personal private data, but there is an important case of people's personal public data which has almost been overlooked. Link in Bio: Personal Public Data, 2024

Data about a person, published deliberately by that person, for anyone to read. Everyone has some. Almost no infrastructure treats it as a category.

73.1 The third column

73.2 A history of attempts

The note traces the idea back to the beginning, and the trajectory is the argument:

The idea of someone's personal presence on the web is as old as the web, though initially personal pages were mainly parts of an academic site. Anyone who ran an internet-connected computer could put up a home page. Link in Bio: Personal Public Data, 2024

Berners-Lee then lists the hosting services that came and went through the 2000s, noting that they were about hypermedia rather than data. A page said things to a reader; nothing about it was machine-readable, so nothing could be done with it except read it.

Two projects tried to change that and are described with care. One held that everyone should have their own site, obtained by registering a name and running a server. The other, a vocabulary created around the turn of the millennium, let anyone who controlled a site publish machine-readable statements about themselves and their relationships.

It was linked data - you could follow the foaf:knows graph across the web. Link in Bio: Personal Public Data, 2024

That is the social network of Chapter 49, built openly, in which the network is the set of documents and no company holds it.

73.3 Where the energy went

The note then records, without complaint, what happened to that effort:

The enregy of the FOAF project has recently gone into the schema.org ontolgies. Link in Bio: Personal Public Data, 2024

And explains why that vocabulary won, in terms that are entirely about incentives:

This allows Google to display info boxes about your products, which is much mre effective. It is also essential for Search Engine Optimization -- so a lot of web sites have this. Link in Bio: Personal Public Data, 2024

Machine-readable data about people and things is now published at enormous scale, because a search engine rewards it. Chapter 49's four rules are followed by millions of sites that have never heard of them, for a reason the rules never mentioned.

73.4 The business card

The proposal is characteristically modest about where to start. The basic level of public data is what people put on a business card: a name, a way to reach you, sometimes a picture. And then one sentence that makes the whole thing concrete:

If you put that on your pod, you can use a QR code as business card. Link in Bio: Personal Public Data, 2024

Beyond that, an instant photograph, the most recent of a stream or the whole stream, a blog post or a whole blog, a list of papers. The list grows by adding things, not by adding infrastructure.

Berners-Lee assumes throughout that the same data will be served as a page for people and as data for machines from the same place, which is Chapter 10's genericity doing quiet work.

73.5 Why it belongs in a volume about trust

Personal public data is the part of yourself you choose to expose, and the choosing is the whole point. Volume VII has been about systems that model trust too narrowly. This is the case where a person wants to be identified, wants to be read, and still needs the arrangement to be theirs.

The title names the thing this replaces. A single link in a profile, on a platform that owns the profile, pointing at a page listing your other accounts, because there is nowhere else for that list to live.

What happened since

  • The category acquired a market and not a standard. Services offering a single page of links became widely used, which is evidence of the need and not of the architecture.
  • Verification arrived through the platforms. Proving that an account and a site belong to the same person is now done by markup that the platform checks, which answers part of section 73.2's problem inside one company at a time.
  • Emergency information stayed with device makers. Allergies, blood type and next of kin are held by phone operating systems and shown on a locked screen, which is the use case of Figure 73.1 solved in a place the Web cannot reach.
  • Structured data about people is ubiquitous and not portable. It is published for search engines, in the shape search engines want, and cannot easily be moved or reused by its subject.
