The short version

Some data about a person is meant for everyone: a name, a job, a way to make contact, even a blood group. Today it is scattered across platforms. Berners-Lee proposes keeping it in one place the person controls, readable by people and programs alike, and pointing every profile at it.

The question

Many social platforms allow exactly one clickable link in a profile. People point it at a page listing every other place they can be found, often on a link-list service such as Linktree, which the note cites. That page is for people to read, not data for programs. Berners-Lee’s 2024 note starts from the category this belongs to, which falls between the two everyone discusses:

Much has been written about around open public data and personal private data, but there is an important case of people's personal public data which has almost been overlooked. Link in Bio: Personal Public Data, 2024

Where should the things a person wants everyone to know about them actually live?

73.1 The middle column

Personal private data is shared only with people its owner chooses: health records, messages. Open public data is published by institutions for anyone: government statistics, maps. The note’s subject sits, in its own words, “in the middle column of the table”.

73.2 Home pages, then FOAF

The note traces the idea back to the beginning:

The idea of someone's personal presence on the web is as old as the web, though initially personal pages were mainly parts of an academic site. Anyone who ran an internet-connected computer could put up a home page. Link in Bio: Personal Public Data, 2024

Then came hosting services such as GeoCities and MySpace. All of them held pages for people to read, not data for programs. Had they held data, the note says, events could have been calendar files (iCalendar), and blogs and podcasts RSS feeds that a program can collect.

Two projects tried to change that. IndieWeb held that everyone should have their own site, on a domain name they register and a server they run. FOAF, Friend of a Friend, created around 2000 by Dan Brickley and Libby Miller, was a vocabulary that let anyone who controlled a site publish machine-readable statements about themselves and their relationships. Its key term, foaf:knows, says on one person’s page that they know another, by linking to theirs.

It was linked data - you could follow the foaf:knows graph across the web. Link in Bio: Personal Public Data, 2024

That is an open social network built on Chapter 49’s rules, in which the network is the set of documents and no company holds it. One history of the project, cited in the note, calls FOAF “the Facebook that could have been”.

73.3 Where the energy went

FOAF’s energy, the note says, went into schema.org, a vocabulary the major search engines read:

The enregy of the FOAF project has recently gone into the schema.org ontolgies. Link in Bio: Personal Public Data, 2024

The reason was money (Search Engine Optimization is tuning a site so that it ranks higher in search results):

This allows Google to display info boxes about your products, which is much mre effective. It is also essential for Search Engine Optimization -- so a lot of web sites have this. Link in Bio: Personal Public Data, 2024

A lot of sites now publish machine-readable statements, as Chapter 49 hoped, but because a search engine rewards it. Those statements describe shops and products, published to please a search engine; the person-controlled profile FOAF aimed at did not come with them.

73.4 Start with a business card

The proposal: keep a personal data store, a pod in Berners-Lee’s Solid project, on the Web under the owner’s control (Chapter 54), with a WebID, a single web address that stands for the person. Put on it the things meant for everyone to see, as data rather than only as a page. The note starts modestly, with what people put on a business card: a name, a way to make contact, sometimes a picture.

If you put that on your pod, you can use a QR code as business card. Link in Bio: Personal Public Data, 2024

From there the list grows by adding things, not infrastructure:

KindExamples from the note
Business cardName, phone or email, a picture; shared as a QR code
StreamA photograph, a photo stream, a blog post or a whole blog
ProfessionalPapers, skills, experience, qualifications, organisations worked with
SafetyBlood group, allergies, next of kin
PointersOther social media accounts; registries such as ORCID

Berners-Lee assumes the pod serves one address in two forms: a page for people and data for programs (Chapter 10).

73.5 One link in a bio

Back to that single link in a profile, or bio. Berners-Lee suggests that it should be the person’s WebID, which can lead to the others.

Proving the accounts belong to one person

The note ends with a use for the pod that no single platform can offer:

Put your Solid WebID in each bio, and link each account from your pod. Then you have proved that all accounts belong to the same person. Link in Bio: Personal Public Data, 2024

What happened since

  • Verification arrived through the platforms. Since version 2.6 (October 2018) Mastodon checks rel="me" links: if a person’s site links to their profile with that marker and the profile links back, the link is shown as verified. That is section 73.5’s two-way proof, run inside one platform at a time.
  • The category acquired a market and not a standard. Link-list services such as Linktree (launched 2016) became widely used, evidence of the need, not of the architecture.
  • Emergency information stayed with device makers. Apple’s Medical ID (iOS 8, 2014) and Android’s emergency information (Android 7, 2016) show allergies, blood type and emergency contacts on a locked phone: the use case of Figure 73.1 solved in a place the Web cannot reach.
