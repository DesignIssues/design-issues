The short version

A plain link is like a footnote reference: it says nothing about the thing it points to. An embedded image is like a photo pasted into a letter: it is part of what was sent, and the sender answers for it. Frames blur the two.

The question

Put a picture from another site into a page and its author answers for it; put a link there and nothing follows. In April 1997 “strange suggestions” threatened that difference, and Berners-Lee wrote it down:

Links in hypertext are new in that they can be followed automatically, but the concepts of reference and inclusion of material predate paper. There should not therefore be much confusion about what links imply, but as there have been some strange suggestions recently which would seriously damage the web, I write this note. Links and Law, 1997

75.1 A reference and an inclusion

The intention in the design of the web was that normal links should simply be references, with no implied meaning. Links and Law, 1997

Basic HTML had three ways to point at other material, each written as an address in the markup. Berners-Lee calls the first two “normal” links, since the reader moves from one document to another, and the third “embedding” links.

MarkupWhat the reader seesKind
AUnderlined words to click; a new page replaces the old one or opens beside itnormal
LINKNothing in the text; a relation such as a stylesheet or the next pagenormal
IMG, OBJECTThe picture or object appears inside the pageembedding

75.2 Where the meaning actually is

So the existence of the link itself does not carry meaning. Of course the contents of the linking document can carry meaning, and often does. Links and Law, 1997

75.3 What a copy loses

Why it matters what is part of a document, in the note’s example:

If I say, "To understand this you only have to read this article", or "This is the agreement between us", I am talking about a particular document. It is important that we have a clear picture of what is part of that document and what isn't. Links and Law, 1997

Including a part by reference keeps its address, and the address carries more. Suppose a news page shows a photographer’s picture by pointing at the photographer’s address. A reader can fetch the current version; the photographer’s server can to some extent know, and perhaps control, who asks; and in future, Berners-Lee expects, anyone could look up the owner and the licence terms. A copy saved on the news site’s own server loses all three, and the third, the note says, “is important for intellectual property rights to be respected on the Web”. Linking by address respects the owner; copying does not.

75.4 The proposal that was never adopted

The note’s rule is plain: “Images, embedded objects, and background sounds and images are by default to be considered part of the document.” It allows one exception:

Advertising provides an exception to this rule: a case in which the embedded image is not part of the document. Links and Law, 1997

Berners-Lee proposed that the markup say which embedded material is not part of the document, so that a browser could, for example, put a border round an advertisement. He knew the cost: it risked making it too easy for readers to turn advertising off. He still judged it an important step forward in the integrity of the web, and one extra word on the image would do: 

<IMG src="banner-ad.gif" foreign>

75.5 Someone else’s page inside the frame

Frames split one browser window into separate panes. Clicking a link inside one pane could load another site’s page there, while the first site’s logo and menu stayed around it. The note explains the trouble:

The parts of the document do appear with the same window, but because within a single frame (subsection of a window) one can follow hypertext links replacing content with a separate document, it is easy to create the impression that the owner of the surrounding frames is in fact responsible for the defining document. Links and Law, 1997

Until markup can express the difference, the note says, content providers should make sure readers are not left with the illusion. As background (the note names no case): in February 1997 the Washington Post and other news publishers sued TotalNews for showing their pages inside its own frames, beside its own advertisements, and in June TotalNews settled by agreeing to stop.

What happened since

  • Frames were replaced and the problem persisted. Inline frames (HTML 4.0, 1997) and embedded players reproduce the confusion section 75.5 names; HTML5’s sandbox attribute limits what they can do rather than saying whose they are.
  • The foreign flag never arrived, and readers turned advertising off anyway. Extensions such as Adblock (2002) and Adblock Plus (2006) hide adverts by matching their addresses against filter lists: guesswork, because markup still does not say what is foreign.
