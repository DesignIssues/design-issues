The question

The companion to Chapter 75, written the same month, taking three claims that were being made about links and answering each. The claims were not academic: each had been asserted by somebody trying to stop somebody else linking to them.

76.1 Three myths

76.2 The first myth, and the mail he refused

The answer to the first claim rests on an equivalence:

The ability to refer to a document (or a person or any thing else) is in general a fundamental right of free speech to the same extent that speech is free. Making the reference with a hypertext link is more efficient but changes nothing else. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

Efficiency is the only novelty, and efficiency is not a legal category. He adds the corollary about content that was already unlawful: its illegality should not be affected by being in electronic form.

Then a personal anecdote that is really an argument about norms:

I received a mail message asking for "permission" to link to our site. I refused as I insisted that permission was not needed. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

Refusing to grant permission, in order to avoid establishing that permission is a thing that can be granted. Had he agreed, the practice of asking would have been confirmed, and Berners-Lee names the consequence he was avoiding: people frightened to make links for fear of legal implications.

76.3 The second myth, and the right it would create

The second claim is conceded almost entirely before being refused. Berners-Lee agrees that your document is made more valuable by good links, and gives an ordinary example: a review in a consumer magazine has added value from the quality of the products it refers to. He extends it to people: he may be more valuable to you if he refers you to others by name, telephone number or address.

And then the sentence that does the work:

This doesn't mean I owe those people something. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

Which yields the general form of the objection:

We cannot regard anyone as having the "right not to be referred to" without completely pulling the rug out from under free speech. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

76.4 The third myth, and what obscurity is worth

The third answer is about a practice rather than a principle. Hiding something behind an unguessable address has one property, and it is the property that decides the case:

The "security by obscurity" method of hiding things behind secret URLs has the property that anyone knowing the URL (like a password) can pass it on. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

If the address is the only protection, the protection travels with the address. Berners-Lee's conclusion is narrow and correct: this is a breach of confidentiality only if there was a confidentiality agreement. The obligation, if any, is on the person who received the address, not on a stranger who repeats it.

76.5 The hall of flame

The note ends with a running list of cases, added to over years, and the list is more persuasive than the arguments because each entry is somebody acting against their own interest.

CaseWhat was attempted
A ticket seller, late 1990sStopping a city guide from linking readers to where they could buy tickets
A Danish court, 2002Enjoining a news service from linking to a newspaper's pages
An Olympic site, 2004Forbidding links to inner pages, and constraining how the entry page might be linked
A newspaper, 2010Disallowing linking even to its home page

Berners-Lee's comment on the first is the general verdict on all four: it was widely perceived not only as philosophically wrong but as crazy, since it was a protest against someone bringing them traffic and business.

On the second he states what is at stake if such reasoning is accepted: the whole working of the Web would break down. And on the third he allows himself one moment of feeling, noting the pity that a site celebrating what is best in humanity should exclude itself from the global discourse.

What happened since

  • The principle largely won in law. European case law from 2014 established that linking to material already freely available is not a new communication to the public, which is close to the position argued here.
  • The exception is knowledge. Later judgments qualified this where the linker knew the material was unlawfully posted, which corresponds to the note's own concession that unlawful speech does not become lawful by being electronic.
  • Asking permission died out. The practice of requesting permission to link, which the note refused to legitimise, disappeared entirely.
  • Obscurity became a recognised weakness. Unguessable addresses as the sole protection are now treated as a known vulnerability class rather than as a form of privacy.
