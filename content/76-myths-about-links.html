The short version

A link is only an address, like a footnote, and copies nothing. Pointing at something needs no permission and creates no debt. A “secret” address protects nothing once it is passed on.

The question

Berners-Lee recalls one request:

I received a mail message asking for "permission" to link to our site. I refused as I insisted that permission was not needed. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

Saying yes would have implied that it was needed. This note, written the same month as Chapter 75, takes on three claims that would make it so.

76.1 Three claims against linking

The note names three claims that would restrict linking, and calls each a myth.

76.2 The first myth: a link is not a copy

The first claim is that a link incites copyright infringement, encouraging people to copy a work its owner has not released. But a link is only an address written into a page.

The answer is an equivalence:

The ability to refer to a document (or a person or any thing else) is in general a fundamental right of free speech to the same extent that speech is free. Making the reference with a hypertext link is more efficient but changes nothing else. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

So a libel stays a libel if it contains a link, and a lawful sentence does not become unlawful by having one. That is why he refused the request for permission: agreeing would have confirmed the habit of asking, and the note names the result to avoid, people frightened to make links for fear of legal implications.

76.3 The second myth, and the right it would create

The second claim, that a link makes a document more valuable and so should be paid for, is conceded almost entirely before being refused. Good links do add value, as a consumer magazine’s review gains value from the products it covers. But:

This doesn't mean I owe those people something. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

In general form:

We cannot regard anyone as having the "right not to be referred to" without completely pulling the rug out from under free speech. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

76.4 The third myth, and what obscurity is worth

The third claim concerns “security by obscurity”: protecting a page with an address nobody could guess instead of a password. Its weakness decides the case:

The "security by obscurity" method of hiding things behind secret URLs has the property that anyone knowing the URL (like a password) can pass it on. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

Berners-Lee’s conclusion is narrow: passing the address on breaches confidentiality only if a confidentiality agreement (a promise to keep something private) was made. “Anyone with the link” sharing of documents and photo albums works this way today; the 2014 TAG draft (see What happened since) lists how such addresses leak, from browser history to referrer headers and server logs.

76.5 The hall of flame: four attempts to forbid deep links

The note closes with a Hall of Flame, a pun on a hall of fame. Although the note is dated April 1997, Berners-Lee kept adding to this list: the Danish entry is dated July 2002, and the others begin “In 2004” and “In 2010”. Each case is an attempt to stop deep linking: links that lead to an inner page rather than the front page.

Ticketmaster. Microsoft’s Seattle Sidewalk city guide linked readers into Ticketmaster’s pages to buy event tickets. Ticketmaster sued in 1997; the case was settled in 1999. The note, citing it from memory, calls the suit widely seen as not only philosophically wrong but crazy: Sidewalk was bringing traffic, and hence business, to Ticketmaster.

A Danish court, 2002. An injunction stopped a news filtering service linking to a Danish newspaper’s pages. Berners-Lee expected an appeal to clear it up: “If such decisions are accepted, the whole working of the web would break down.”

Athens, 2004. The Olympic site tried to forbid links to inner pages, and even to constrain links to its entry page. A pity, the note says, when the Olympics celebrate what is best in humanity, that “the web presence should exclude itself from the global discourse.”

Nikkei, 2010. The note quotes the New York Times: links even to the Japanese paper’s home page required “a detailed written application”, the site disabled right-click (which offers to copy a link’s address), and its link policy ended “We may seek damages for any violations of these rules.” The stated aim was to protect its pay wall.

76.6 A paper bag over the head

The note ends by raising the stakes. Lawyers, users and technology and content providers “must all agree to respect these principles”, and “It is difficult to emphasize how important these issues are for society.” The First Amendment to the United States Constitution addresses the right to speak, and the right to refer to something is inherent in it:

On the web, to make reference without making a link is possible but ineffective — like speaking but with a paper bag over your head. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

What happened since

  • The principle largely won in European law. In Svensson (February 2014) the Court of Justice of the EU held that linking to material already freely available online is not a communication to a new public (a fresh act of publishing that needs the owner’s consent), close to the position argued here.
  • The exception is knowledge. In GS Media (2016) the same court qualified this where the linker knew, or ought to have known, that the material was posted unlawfully, echoing the note’s point about speech that is already unlawful.
  • Secret addresses got guidance, not a ban. A 2014 W3C TAG draft, Good Practices for Capability URLs, treats unguessable addresses as acceptable if used with care.
