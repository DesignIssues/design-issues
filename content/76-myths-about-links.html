The short version

A link is only an address, like a footnote, and copies nothing. Pointing at something needs no permission and creates no debt. A “secret” address protects nothing once it is passed on.

The question

Does anyone have the right to stop someone pointing at their page? This companion to Chapter 75, written the same month, answers three claims that said yes. Each had been made by somebody trying to stop somebody else linking to them.

76.1 Three myths

76.2 The first myth, and the mail he refused

The first claim is that a link is an incitement (an encouragement to do wrong) to copyright infringement, which means copying a protected work without its owner’s consent. But a link is only a web address (a URL) written into a page.

The answer to the first claim rests on an equivalence:

The ability to refer to a document (or a person or any thing else) is in general a fundamental right of free speech to the same extent that speech is free. Making the reference with a hypertext link is more efficient but changes nothing else. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

A link makes referring faster. Being faster does not change what the law should say about it. He adds a corollary, a point that follows directly: content that was already unlawful should be no more and no less unlawful for being in electronic form.

Then an anecdote that is really about norms, the unwritten expectations of how people behave:

I received a mail message asking for "permission" to link to our site. I refused as I insisted that permission was not needed. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

He said no, not to forbid the link, but because saying yes would have implied his permission was needed. Agreeing would have confirmed the habit of asking, and he names the consequence he wanted to avoid: people frightened to make links for fear of legal implications.

76.3 The second myth, and the right it would create

The second claim is conceded almost entirely before being refused. Berners-Lee agrees that good links make a document more valuable, as a review in a consumer magazine gains value from the quality of the products it refers to. A person may be more valuable to others if he refers them to other people by name, phone number or address. Then the sentence that does the work:

This doesn't mean I owe those people something. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

Which yields the general form of the objection:

We cannot regard anyone as having the "right not to be referred to" without completely pulling the rug out from under free speech. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

76.4 The third myth, and what obscurity is worth

The third claim concerns “security by obscurity”: protecting a page not with a password but by giving it an address nobody could guess. That practice has one property, and it decides the case:

The "security by obscurity" method of hiding things behind secret URLs has the property that anyone knowing the URL (like a password) can pass it on. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

Berners-Lee’s conclusion is narrow: passing the address on breaches confidentiality only if a confidentiality agreement (a promise to keep something private) was made, and then only by the person who made it.

76.5 The hall of flame

The note ends with a list of cases, added to over years. Three entries are site owners refusing free traffic; one is a court.

CaseWhat was attempted
Ticketmaster, 1990sStopping the Seattle Sidewalk city guide from linking readers into its pages to buy tickets
A Danish court, 2002Ordering a news filtering service to stop linking to a Danish newspaper’s pages
The Athens Olympic site, 2004Forbidding links to inner pages, and constraining how the entry page might be linked
Nikkei Shimbun, 2010 (as reported by the New York Times)Requiring “a detailed written application” before linking even to its home page

His comment on the first applies to most of them: it was widely perceived not only as philosophically wrong but as crazy, since it was a protest against someone bringing them traffic and business. On the Danish case he states what is at stake if such reasoning is accepted: the whole working of the Web would break down. On the Olympic site he notes the pity that a site celebrating what is best in humanity should exclude itself from the global discourse.

76.6 Why it matters

The note’s conclusion ties linking to the right to speak. The First Amendment to the United States Constitution protects speech, and Berners-Lee argues that the right to refer to something is part of that right:

On the web, to make reference without making a link is possible but ineffective — like speaking but with a paper bag over your head. Links and Law: Myths, 1997

What happened since

  • The principle largely won in European law. In Svensson (February 2014) the Court of Justice of the EU held that linking to material already freely available online is not a communication to a new public, close to the position argued here.
  • The exception is knowledge. In GS Media (2016) the same court qualified this where the linker knew, or ought to have known, that the material was posted unlawfully, matching the note’s point about speech that is already unlawful.
  • Asking permission became rare. Demands for permission to link became rare and are widely ignored, though the 2010 Nikkei entry shows they did not vanish.
  • Secret addresses got guidance, not a ban. A 2014 W3C TAG draft, Good Practices for Capability URLs, treats unguessable addresses as acceptable if used with care, while setting out the ways they leak.
