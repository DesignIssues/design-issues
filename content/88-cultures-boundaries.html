The question

Written in July 2007 with the editing status “stable”, which in this collection is rare. It describes a mechanism that everyone has experienced and almost nobody has named, and it states it as a law.

88.1 The mechanism

A group that talks among itself develops its own language. Berners-Lee describes three ways this happens: borrowing a term one party already uses and settling its meaning together, adjusting an existing meaning to cover something new the group has found, or deliberately coining a word chosen to be unlike any other.

The first effect is the intended one: a shared vocabulary broadens what can be discussed without misunderstanding. The second effect is not intended by anybody:

88.2 What it looks like in practice

The example is drawn from his own working life and will be recognised by anyone who has sat on a committee:

So it is, then, that a working group will, given a free rein, work in relative isolation for several months, and when they have finished have great difficulty explaining the specification documents to their peers outside the group. Often this will be a surprise to the members of the group. Cultures and boundaries, 2007

The surprise is the diagnostic detail. The group did not notice the wall going up, because from inside it looked like progress.

Then the escalation, described without softening:

It may be worse: those on one side of the boundary may see those on the other as being stupid, malicious, or even heretical. Cultures and boundaries, 2007

88.3 The remedy, and what it costs

The solution offered is called incomplete but essential, and it is not a technique:

An incomplete but essential solution to the problem is for those involved to think about what those on the other side of the boundary are thinking. This is hard work. Cultures and boundaries, 2007

Berners-Lee cites neuroscience for the claim that this involves specific parts of the brain, and describes what it means in a technical setting: a careful study of the words in the other party’s seemingly senseless protestations, in order to build up logically a conclusion about how those words must be related in the other’s mind.

Not deciding they are wrong. Working out what would have to be true of their vocabulary for what they said to make sense.

The consequence for standards work is stated as a cost:

There is constant tension between the need to get things done quickly with less effort, by working within a small group, and the need to get this wider understanding which takes so much more time. Cultures and boundaries, 2007

88.4 Why the technology was shaped by this

The last section makes the connection that places this chapter at the centre of the book:

The Semantic Web is a technology engineered specifically for this situation. Cultures and boundaries, 2007

The four sentences that follow are the whole design compressed. Terms are defined in groups of consistent related terms. Those groups are defined by communities. A person belongs to many communities. A message mixes terms from many groups, and an operation requires consistency only between the parts actually in use.

And the two failures it is built to avoid, named as things already known:

We know that a single huge ontology of everything cannot be done, as it the effort of getting consensus on it becomes unimaginable. We know that stovepipe systems with only local ontologies leave us with communication, and especially the re-use of data, which just does not happen, to our great detriment. Cultures and boundaries, 2007

Those are Chapter 87’s two deaths, arriving as a specification requirement. And the conclusion is a single clause about what follows:

And so we engineer specifically for a fractal topology. Cultures and boundaries, 2007

What happened since

  • The mechanism became a standard observation. The way shared vocabulary both bonds and excludes is now routinely described in work on organisations and on online communities, usually without reference to this note.
  • Mixing vocabularies became normal practice. Combining terms from several communities in one message is how structured data is published, which is the engineering response section 88.4 describes.
  • Translation work remained undervalued. The listening described in section 88.3 is still done by individuals rather than institutionalised, and standards bodies still discover late that their documents are unreadable outside the group.
  • The escalation got worse. Groups seeing each other as stupid or malicious became a defining feature of public discourse in the following decade, which Chapter 90 treats directly.
