The short version

A blockchain is a shared ledger, copied to every participant. Mining spends computing power to add to it, and is paid in new coin. The note asks which part of such a system people mean, and concludes that blockchains are a poor general place for Web data.

The question

Volume VIII closes with a note from October 2017 that opens by refusing the question:

What technical problem is there in the world in 2017 where someone has not asked "Will the blockchain solve that?". The response is then typically "Well, what do you mean by blockchain exactly?", as it really depends which aspect of which system you are talking about. Blockchain and the Web, 2017

Claiming no expertise, Berners-Lee separates the things one word names, as in Chapter 25.

81.1 Four things in one system

The ledger is a list of records in blocks, each linked to the one before; mining is the computing work that adds a block. The original Bitcoin system announced four things, and a cloud of further ideas. Notarising puts a hash in the ledger: a short fingerprint of data that changes if the data changes at all, proving the data existed by that date.

The parts depend on each other: the ledger cannot be taken without the currency (Figure 81.2).

81.2 Two ironies

The first concerns openness:

The ledger system works and maintains its integrity only because of the complete openness of the blocks in the chain, and the fact that many systems were constantly checking it; and yet bitcoin in fact provided a way that value could be used anonymously for criminal purposes with impunity. Blockchain and the Web, 2017

A completely open record was used to hide who the participants were. The second is about cost: a system offered as a fast, light way to transfer value runs, by design, on “burning huge amounts of energy, which is not ecologically sustainable.”

81.3 The warning about encrypting in public

One point applies far beyond this subject:

In general the practice of encrypting stuff and making the encrypted version public requires faith that the encryption method don't be cracked in any timescale which is important to the parties. Blockchain and the Web, 2017

Breaking encryption gets cheaper with time, while the published copy stays. Even unreadable data gives something away: “even if people can’t decrypt they see that you have put something there, which may already be revealing.”

81.4 Speculation, and an old chart

On value, the note is unsentimental:

To first order, for lots of operations transferring money between different currencies and countries, the value (say in USD) of a bitcoin is irrelevant. For those making a remittance, say of USD from the USA to (say) Haiti, all one need to do is own some Bitcoin for a minute between buying them in the US and selling them in Haiti. Blockchain and the Web, 2017

Beside a chart of the price’s rise the note places the NASDAQ up to 1999, when people bought at prices they imagined others would pay. Yet the price matters to the system:

The promise of rise in value of Bitcoin motivates the miners, who keep the blockchain operating and ensure its integrity. Blockchain and the Web, 2017

This is the loop of Figure 81.2, and nothing guarantees anyone will pay what each new coin costs in energy. The remitter, by contrast, risks only a sharp move in the price during the minute the coins are held.

81.5 Re-centralisation through portals

If an application reaches the chain through an online service, so that the blockchain code runs on one of a few portals rather than the user’s computer, the system is not decentralised in effect: “The monopoly portals are back in control.”

81.6 What stored data depends on

On a blockchain everybody stores everything; on the Web each site stores its own. Either way, something must keep working (Figure 81.3).

A web server lasts while someone pays for it, as AOL Hometown’s users learned when it closed. Without miners, a chain has no new blocks and nobody checks the old ones.

81.7 Too slow, too expensive, too public

One chain for everyone is one club with one fee and speed; gamers, retailers and banks would soon each want their own.

For an application storing data, the note lists four problems. A node is one computer taking part.

ProblemWhy
PublicEvery node holds a copy of the whole chain.
SlowAll users must agree on each new block.
ExpensiveEach transaction costs a fee in tokens.
Re-centralisedUsers reach it through a few portals (81.5).

The conclusion:

But in general use as a place for Web apps to to store data, blockchains are too slow, too expensive, and too public. Blockchain and the Web, 2017

81.8 Doing it with the Web instead

A page on a trusted party’s site can be the head of a ledger, recording the latest entry. Signed documents can be chained, each citing by URI and hash the ones it depends on, as in Chapter 62 (Figure 81.5). Version-control systems such as git already fill the Web with hash trees, naming each version of a set of files by a hash of its contents.

Nor do other decentralised stores: a distributed hash table spreads hashed chunks of data across servers, and IPFS shares unchanging files by their hashes.

The weak point is the domain name. Without one, people’s data sits under their provider’s name, tying them to it; anything that helps people get a domain, such as a Solid pod (Chapter 54), helps.

What happened since

  • The energy objection was answered by one network. Ethereum replaced mining with staking in September 2022, cutting its consumption by around 99 per cent; Bitcoin did not.
  • The note’s own update tested the loop. It links a CryptoSlate report of 12 December 2022 that a bitcoin cost less than mining one: the dashed exit of Figure 81.2.
  • Identity was standardised without a blockchain. Decentralised identifiers, the identity part of Figure 81.1, became a W3C Recommendation in July 2022.
  • Payments arrived in the browser, generically. Payment Request became a Recommendation in September 2022: the note’s modular slot, favouring no currency.
  • The speculation chart kept swinging. The price rose far beyond 2017 levels, then lost more than three-quarters of its value in both 2018 and 2022.
