The short version

A blockchain is a shared ledger, copied to every participant; mining adds to it and is paid in new coin. The note asks which part people mean. Its verdict: as a store for Web data, blockchains are too slow, too expensive and too public.

The question

Some hoped blockchains would end the Web’s dependence on a few huge platforms. Berners-Lee’s note tests that hope part by part, and opens by refusing the question:

What technical problem is there in the world in 2017 where someone has not asked "Will the blockchain solve that?". The response is then typically "Well, what do you mean by blockchain exactly?", as it really depends which aspect of which system you are talking about. Blockchain and the Web, 2017

Claiming no expertise, he splits the one word into the separate things it names.

81.1 Four things in one system

The ledger is a list of records in blocks, each linked to the one before; mining is the computing work that adds a block. The original Bitcoin system offered four things, and a cloud of further ideas.

Notarising puts a hash in the ledger: a short fingerprint that changes if the data changes at all, proving the data existed by that date. The ledger cannot be taken without the currency (Figure 81.2).

81.2 An open ledger for anonymous money

Two ironies follow. The first concerns openness. The ledger keeps its integrity only through “the complete openness of the blocks in the chain” and constant checking, yet bitcoin “provided a way that value could be used anonymously for criminal purposes with impunity.”

The second concerns cost: a system offered as a fast, light way to transfer value runs, by design, on “burning huge amounts of energy, which is not ecologically sustainable.”

81.3 The warning about encrypting in public

One point applies far beyond this subject:

In general the practice of encrypting stuff and making the encrypted version public requires faith that the encryption method don't be cracked in any timescale which is important to the parties. Blockchain and the Web, 2017

Breaking encryption gets easier with time, while the published copy stays: “all the stuff on the blockchain can be held encrypted by people just waiting for a time when they are able to decrypt”, helped by “faster computers, (not to mention quantum)”. Even unreadable data gives something away: “even if people can’t decrypt they see that you have put something there, which may already be revealing.”

81.4 Why the price matters even when it should not

For a remittance from the USA to Haiti, the price of a bitcoin barely matters: the sender need only “own some Bitcoin for a minute between buying them in the US and selling them in Haiti.”

The note sets a chart of bitcoin’s 2017 price beside one of the NASDAQ rising to 1999, when people bought tech shares at prices they imagined others would pay. Yet the price matters to the system:

The promise of rise in value of Bitcoin motivates the miners, who keep the blockchain operating and ensure its integrity. Blockchain and the Web, 2017

This is the loop of Figure 81.2, driven by guesswork: the coin’s market value is “only the extent to which other people imagine other people imagine other people will value it in future.”

81.5 Re-centralisation through portals

In practice an application often reaches the chain through an online service: “the blockchain code doesn’t run on the user’s computer, but on one of a small number of portals”, such as exchanges or wallet websites. So the system “isn’t really decentralized, in effect. The monopoly portals are back in control.”

81.6 Who keeps stored data alive

Storing data on a chain means “connecting it to a financial system, whose continued functioning will be required to keep the data accessible.” On a chain or on the Web, something must keep working (Figure 81.3).

A web server lasts while someone pays for it, as AOL Hometown’s users learned when it closed in 2008. Without miners, a chain has no new blocks and nobody checks the old ones.

81.7 Too slow, too expensive, too public

The note likens one chain for everyone to a single club with one fee. If gamers, retailers and banks share the Bitcoin chain, the banks will object to “maintaining an infrastructure used mostly by gamers”, and the gamers to “paying transaction fees used by the banks”. The note expects the banks to spin up a bank-only system like Ripple, and the gamers their own Ethereum chains.

For an application storing data, the note lists four problems.

ProblemWhy
PublicEvery participating computer holds a copy of the whole chain.
SlowAll users must agree on each new block.

Re-centralisedUsers reach it through a few portals (81.5).

81.8 Doing it with the Web instead

A page on a trusted party’s site can be the head of a ledger, recording the latest entry. Signed documents can be chained, each citing by URI and hash the ones it depends on, as in Chapter 62 (Figure 81.5). Version-control systems such as git already fill the Web with hash trees.

Other decentralised stores need no chain: a distributed hash table spreads hashed chunks of data across servers, and IPFS shares unchanging files by hash.

Filecoin, from the designers of IPFS, ties its coin to a real service: the protocol obliges a provider paid in coin to store data for the agreed time. The catch: “you are not guaranteeing that the filecoin world will exist, but that if it does, your data will be stored.”

The Web approach has one weak point: the domain name. Without one, people’s data sits under their provider’s name, tying them to it; anything that helps people get a domain, such as a Solid pod (Chapter 54), helps.

Names or identities can be moved to a blockchain, “but when that is done, the world has to be aware of a new dependency on the community which runs that system”. The verdict:

But in general use as a place for Web apps to to store data, blockchains are too slow, too expensive, and too public. Blockchain and the Web, 2017

What happened since

  • The energy objection was answered by one network. Ethereum replaced mining with staking in September 2022, cutting its energy use by around 99 per cent.
  • The note’s own update tested the loop. It links a CryptoSlate report of 12 December 2022 that a bitcoin cost less than mining one: the dashed exit of Figure 81.2.
  • Identity and payments were standardised without a chain. Decentralised identifiers became a W3C Recommendation in July 2022, and the currency-neutral Payment Request API in September 2022.
  • The speculation chart kept swinging. The price fell by more than three-quarters from its December 2017 peak by late 2018, rose far beyond it by November 2021, then fell by more than three-quarters again by late 2022.
