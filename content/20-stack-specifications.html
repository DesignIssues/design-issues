The short version

Each specification has a field naming the next one. Plugging in commits a computer to the whole chain, so fields have agreed meanings and lying in one is a lie. Higher up, central lists give way to URIs anyone can create. Quoting lets a sender pass something on without claiming it.

The question

Written in May 2002 from an April 2002 keynote in Hawai’i, this note asks why voltages arriving at a computer mean anything. The answer walks up from an Ethernet cable to a bank statement, and ends in an argument that is not technical.

20.1 One commitment, then no more choices

Suppose Alice walks into a meeting room and is handed an Ethernet cable. To use it, her computer must send packets, small blocks of data laid out as the Ethernet specification says. A field in each packet, a slot at a fixed place, names the specification that governs the rest. Committing to the cable commits her computer to everything above it (Figure 20.1).

In the figure, 0x0800 is the code for “an Internet packet”; an RFC (Request for Comments) is a numbered Internet standard; a port number says which service a connection is for; and IANA, the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority, keeps the list of ports.

Each specification also sets rules of behaviour, and each can be cheated. An Ethernet computer that always waits least after a collision crowds the others out, “just like people who always seem to be the one talking in a meeting”. One that resends TCP data too early slows everyone down. One that keeps opening connections it does not want can exhaust a server’s memory.

20.2 Why this is an argument about law

Berners-Lee then turns the chain into an argument about law:

The Internet specifications, to which any Internet user implicitly agrees in using the Internet at all, define what the fields in an email message mean. To put incorrect information in these fields is to make a misrepresentation, just as it would have been in any other medium. It should be subject to the same penalties as lying or fraud in any other medium. The Stack of Specifications, 2002

He then gives the change in stakes, and his conclusion:

Now that the Internet is such as large force in society, it is possible to make a lot of money and create a lot of damage by protocol abuse. You can compare a lie in an internet message, depending on how it is done, to forging a check, connecting to the electricity supply the other side of the meter, or to poisoning the water supply. Society must therefore be careful to be absolutely clear about the illegality of such misuse. The Stack of Specifications, 2002

20.3 The same walk, for a Web page

Publishing a Web page follows a chain of the same shape (Figure 20.2). The http: at the front of an address is its scheme; a public list says it means HTTP. HTTP says to connect to port 80. The reply carries a Content-Type label, such as text/html for a page a person reads, or OFX (Open Financial Exchange, a bank-statement format) for a program.

Neither party can plead ignorance:

So long as the writers of the specification do a good job (and goodness knows they work hard enough at it) then there can be no argument as to what the actual fields in your bank statement mean. The Stack of Specifications, 2002

One more sentence settles who is answerable for what is sent:

The server is the agent of the publisher. The Stack of Specifications, 2002

20.4 The registry that is not a registry

The walk then reaches XML, and a change in kind. A document sent as plain application/xml is read according to the namespace of its outermost element: a URI naming the vocabulary in use. Every list below this point is kept by one authority. A namespace is not: a shop can create, or mint, http://example.org/ns/invoice# tomorrow without asking anyone, and put a page there explaining its terms.

That page may be a specification for people, a schema for machines, or both. The note admits that in practice, for MathML and XHTML, this did not always happen.

Berners-Lee calls these three stages: numbers, which need a central registry; URIs, which anyone with a little domain space can mint; and URIs whose definitions can be fetched from the Web. Central lists suited the few early protocols and strained as protocols multiplied.

RDF is the Semantic Web’s language for data:

  • a triple is one statement: a thing, a property, a value, such as a hat, its colour, blue256;
  • the property, the kind of fact stated, is named by a URI, such as http://www.w3.org/2002/05/example#color.
And whoever made up the URI gets to say what the property means, that is, what any triple using that property means. The Stack of Specifications, 2002

Define that property to take Pantone colour names, and an order for a hat in blue256 cannot be read as any other blue.

20.6 When the chain does not connect

Sometimes a sender wants to pass on something not meant. Most languages have a way to break the chain:

Understanding the exceptions is as important as understanding the general rule. The Stack of Specifications, 2002

In email it is the attachment, used only as the cover note says: “Look at this stupid program - but whatever you do don't run it!” XML in 2002 had no standard for such “packages”. In Notation3, a text syntax for RDF, curly brackets quote: {my:car srgb:color "000044"} mentions the claim that the car is blue without making it (000044 is dark blue; the braces mark a sentence talked about, not asserted). Forwarding a scam email to warn a friend works the same way: its claims travel quoted, unmade.

The fact that you can do this removes the last excuse for anyone claiming not to have meant whatever they did say in the main message! The Stack of Specifications, 2002

What happened since

  • The legal argument was partly taken up. The EU ePrivacy Directive (2002/58/EC, 2002) barred marketing email that hides its sender, and the US CAN-SPAM Act (2003) outlawed misleading header information: narrower, but the same reasoning.
  • The technical answer came first. SPF (RFC 4408, 2006), DKIM (RFC 4871, 2007) and DMARC (RFC 7489, 2015) made the From field checkable rather than merely defined.
  • The namespace-as-registry idea half worked. Anyone may mint a namespace URI, and many serve nothing at it. HTML, as Chapter 23 describes, dropped namespaces altogether.
  • The links proved replaceable. IPv6 now runs alongside IPv4, and RFC 822 has been replaced twice, by RFC 2822 (2001) and RFC 5322 (2008), without changing what a From line means.
