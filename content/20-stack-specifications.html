The question

Written in May 2002 from a keynote given at the World Wide Web conference in Hawaii, this note answers a question nobody in engineering thinks to ask: when a computer receives a pattern of voltages, why does it mean anything?

The chapter title in the original is The Stack of Specifications and its first section heading is three words: bits mean something. What follows is a walk up the stack from an Ethernet cable to a bank statement, and the argument at the top is not technical at all.

20.1 One commitment, then no more choices

The device is a chain. At each step, a field in what you have just committed to names the specification that governs the next step. Committing to plug in the cable commits you, on your behalf, to everything above it.

20.2 Why this is an argument about law

Having built the chain, Berners-Lee uses it for something unexpected. If the specifications define what the fields mean, and both parties have implicitly agreed to the specifications by using the Internet at all, then falsifying a field is not a technical trick:

The Internet specifications, to which any Internet user implicitly agrees in using the Internet at all, define what the fields in an email message mean. To put incorrect information in these fields is to make a misrepresentation, just as it would have been in any other medium. It should be subject to the same penalties as lying or fraud in any other medium. The Stack of Specifications, 2002

He then gives the change in stakes, which is the part that has aged into understatement:

When the Internet was young and used by research institutions, its misuse would inconvenience other users and lead to reprobation and the disdain of one's peers. Now that the Internet is such as large force in society, it is possible to make a lot of money and create a lot of damage by protocol abuse. The Stack of Specifications, 2002

The analogies he reaches for are physical and escalating: forging a cheque, connecting to the electricity supply on the far side of the meter, poisoning the water supply.

20.3 The same walk, for a Web page

The second half repeats the exercise for publishing, and the chain is the same shape with different links. A URI is chosen from space its owner has authority over. The scheme names the specification, through the registry of URI schemes. That specification says which port to contact. The response carries a content type, and the registry of content types names the specification that says what the body means.

The conclusion is put in terms of what a publisher may not later claim:

So long as the writers of the specification do a good job (and goodness knows they work hard enough at it) then there can be no argument as to what the actual fields in your bank statement mean. The Stack of Specifications, 2002

Along the way one sentence does a great deal of work, and is the reason this chapter matters to Volume IV:

The server is the agent of the publisher. The Stack of Specifications, 2002

20.4 The registry that is not a registry

The last step of the walk introduces XML, and with it a change in kind. Ethernet types, protocol numbers, ports and content types all live in central registries kept by one authority. A namespace does not.

Berners-Lee notes in passing that the Technical Architecture Group was at the time debating what format such a definition should take, and that HTML, RDDL and RDF had all been used in combination. He also records, in a parenthesis marked as a problem, that the mechanism was not being followed in practice.

What happened since

  • The legal argument was partly taken up. Anti-spam legislation on both sides of the Atlantic from 2003 onward made header falsification specifically unlawful, which is narrower than the general principle here but is the same reasoning.
  • The technical answer arrived before the legal one. Sender authentication, through the Sender Policy Framework, DomainKeys Identified Mail and finally DMARC in 2015, made the From field checkable rather than merely defined. The protocol was repaired rather than enforced.
  • The namespace-as-registry idea half worked. Anyone may mint a namespace URI, and most do not serve anything at it, which is the problem recorded in Chapter 9. The registry exists; the documents largely do not.
  • The stack itself proved replaceable. Every layer in Figure 20.1 except the top has since been substituted in practice, by wireless links, by QUIC over UDP, and by submission on port 587 with TLS, without changing what a From line means.
