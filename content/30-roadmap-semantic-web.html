The short version

A page on the Web is written for a person to read. A machine can fetch it and display it, but it cannot answer a question about what the page says, because nothing in the page tells it which words are prices and which are dates.

This chapter is the first full plan for fixing that: a stack of layers, each one useful on its own before the layer above it exists. It is worth reading for the plan, and worth reading again for how much of it happened.

The question

The companion to Chapter 5, written the same month of September 1998 and spun off from it because the subject needed more room than a survey could give. Its own description of its standing is candid:

A road map for the future, an architectural plan untested by anything except thought experiments. Semantic Web Road map, 1998

It is the first full statement of the Semantic Web as a programme rather than an idea, and it is a plan with an order: each layer assumes the one below and is useful before the one above exists.

30.1 The problem being solved

The diagnosis is one sentence and has not aged at all:

One of the major obstacles to this has been the fact that most information on the Web is designed for human consumption, and even if it was derived from a database with well defined meanings (in at least some terms) for its columns, that the structure of the data is not evident to a robot browsing the web. Semantic Web Road map, 1998

Berners-Lee then sets aside the approach he is not taking. Leaving the artificial intelligence problem of training machines to behave like people to one side, the Semantic Web approach develops languages for expressing information in machine-processable form. The negative claim is made properly in Chapter 31.

30.2 The layers

Berners-Lee justifies the base by minimalism. A universal web of assertions must rest on a model general enough that any prospective application maps onto it, and generality forces simplicity. The base contains two things only: the assertion, and quotation, which is making assertions about assertions. He gives two reasons for including quotation this early, and the second is practical: most early applications are data about data, where assertions about assertions are basic even before logic arrives.

He is honest about how little the base layer does. There is no negation and no implication, so given a set of facts it is easy to say whether a proof exists, because neither facts nor questions have enough power to make the problem intractable.

30.3 Why bother at the bottom

The note anticipates the obvious objection. If an application’s data is simple, why express it in this model at all? The answer is the argument for the whole enterprise:

The answer is that we expect this data, while limited and simple within an application, to be combined, later, with data from other applications into a Web. Semantic Web Road map, 1998

The example given is access control, where a decision may combine privacy preferences, group membership and data typing, each of which is trivial alone and none of which shares a format with the others. Chapter 37 works that example through in detail.

30.4 Proof, and the asymmetry that makes it work

The most important design decision in the roadmap is a restriction, and it is what separates this plan from the artificial intelligence projects it is compared to. The system is required to validate proofs, not to find them.

Finding a proof for an arbitrary question is intractable. Checking one that arrives with the request is cheap. The applications Berners-Lee has in mind, access control first among them, need only the second. Chapter 31 makes the point at length, and it is the single answer to most objections raised against the whole programme.

30.5 Engines of the future

The closing section is the most speculative and, read now, the most interesting. Search engines cover a huge part of the Web, return many inappropriate answers, and have no notion of correctness. Logical engines restrict output to provably correct answers, and have failed to rummage through intertwined data to construct them.

Berners-Lee proposes combining them: an engine that reaches out to indexes holding complete lists of occurrences of a term, and then uses logic to discard all but those useful for the problem. His estimate of the depth available is modest and specific:

many real life problems can be solved using just a few (say two) steps of inference out on the wild web, the rest of the reasoning being in a realm in which proofs are give, or there are constrains and well understood computable algorithms. Semantic Web Road map, 1998

He expects commercial incentive to produce engines for specific problem types, with caches of intermediate results analogous to a search index. And he states the limit plainly: no machine will guarantee to answer arbitrary questions.

What happened since

  • The bottom three layers shipped. RDF became a Recommendation in February 1999, RDF Schema in February 2004, OWL in the same month, and SPARQL for querying in January 2008. The plan was executed upward, in order, over a decade.
  • The proof layer did not. No interoperable proof exchange format was standardised. The asymmetry of section 30.4, which the roadmap treats as its central bet, was never cashed.
  • The search-plus-logic engine arrived in another shape. Large knowledge graphs behind search, beginning with Google’s in May 2012, combine indexing with structured data at exactly the scale described here, without the proof machinery.
  • Conversion between vocabularies remains the hard part. The layer Berners-Lee places third is where most practical effort still goes, and it is largely done by hand.
