The question

Three years after the argument of Chapter 7, the Technical Architecture Group decided the matter. This note is Berners-Lee's account of the decision, written in June 2005, and it is the shortest chapter in the book. Its brevity is the point: a question that had consumed years of debate is disposed of in two pages, because the answer turned out to be a rule about response codes rather than a rule about meaning.

8.1 What was decided

The resolution is stated twice in the note, once as an abstract and once in the body, in nearly identical words. The repetition is worth preserving because the precision is load-bearing:

The W3C Technical Architecture group eventually decided to resolve the architectural problem that if an HTTP response code of 200 (a successful retrieval) was given, that indicated that the URI indeed was for an information resource, but with no such response, or with a different code, no such assumption could be made. This compromise resolved the issue, leaving a consistent architecture. What HTTP URIs Identify, 2005

The logical shape is worth stating plainly, because it is easy to read the rule as stronger than it is. A response of 200 licenses one conclusion. Any other response licenses none. The rule is one-directional: it tells a client when it may conclude that a URI names a document, and is silent otherwise.

8.2 What it cost and what it bought

Berners-Lee describes the effect as an extension of range rather than a change of principle:

The TAG resolution effectively extends the range of things one can use HTTP URIs. However, it does not allow one to simply serve a web page at a URI which is used for something else. What HTTP URIs Identify, 2005

The second sentence is the constraint that makes the first safe. A publisher who wants a URI to name a person may not also serve that person's home page from it with a 200. The cost of naming a thing is that the obvious thing to serve at its address becomes forbidden, and a redirect must be served instead.

The compromise is a compromise in a precise sense. Chapter 7 argued that this question should be fixed at the level of the specification; the Group fixed it at the level of the protocol exchange. Berners-Lee accepts the result without pretending it is what he asked for, and the page he wrote in 2002 acquired a header saying the compromise works quite well.

8.3 Why the account is worth reading despite its length

The note is also a record of how the problem arose in practice rather than in theory. Berners-Lee names the vocabularies:

The Dublin Core project, one of the first RDF vocabularies, and later Friend of a Friend, and various others simply used HTTP URIs to identify RDF Properties. The result was that one could no longer be sure that an HTTP URI was intended to identify the web page one got when one used the URI in a browser. What HTTP URIs Identify, 2005

The architecture did not fail. It was outrun. Deployed practice created a usage the specifications had not contemplated, and by 2005 the choice was between blessing the practice and invalidating a large body of published data. Chapter 9 gives Berners-Lee's fuller account of that pressure, and is franker about who conceded what.

What happened since

  • The redirect pattern was codified. The Group's finding and the W3C Interest Group note Cool URIs for the Semantic Web, of December 2008, turned the resolution into publishing guidance, setting out both the 303 pattern and the hash pattern as acceptable.
  • The rule was loosened in practice. The cost of an extra round trip made 303 unpopular, and later work explored serving descriptions directly. The Group revisited the area in 2012 without overturning the 2005 resolution.
  • Neither pattern won. Vocabularies published after 2005 use both. A consumer of arbitrary Linked Data must still handle each.
  • The underlying ambiguity outlived the fix. Structured data embedded in pages, which became the dominant way of publishing descriptions of things after Schema.org launched in June 2011, sidesteps the question entirely by describing things inside a document rather than giving them dereferenceable URIs of their own.
