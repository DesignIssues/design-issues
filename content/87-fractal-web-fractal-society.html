The short version

A society can fail in two ways. Everybody ends up sharing the same language and ideas, and nothing new can arise; or people divide into sealed groups that cannot speak to each other.

Avoiding both needs groups of every size, from the world down to two people: a scale-free pattern, which looks much the same at any zoom. Web traffic at DEC’s firewall followed one such curve, a Zipf distribution. In 2000 a mathematical result supported part of the claim.

The question

Written in 1998 and appended to at intervals, this much-cited note opens by correcting its own title:

This article was originally entitled "The Fractal nature of the web". Since then, i have been assured that while many people seem to use fractal to refer to a Zipf (1/f) distribution, it should really only be used in spaces of finite dimension The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

Then it says what kind of claim it makes:

This isn't an observation so much as a requirement. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

87.1 Two ways for a society to die

In a monoculture communication is easy, but nothing different is left to say. A cult, in the note’s sense, is a group that talks only to itself.

The case for the middle, argued from experience:

  1. A group is stable when it has peers and a substructure.
  2. Groups cannot work with too many liaisons or too many parts.
  3. So there must be a continuum of group sizes from the very largest to the very smallest.

He admits what this lacks:

I have no mathematical theory to demonstrate that this is an optimization of some metric for the resilience of society and its effectiveness as an organism, nor have I even that metric. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

87.2 The same tension, in language

The claim is restated for language:

Locally defined languages are easy to create, needing local consensus about meaning: only a limited number of people have to share a mental pattern of relationships which define the meaning. However, global languages are so much more effective at communication, reaching the parts that local languages cannot. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

And located where it is felt:

This tension is exemplified in the standards process, when ideas have to be exposed to successively larger and larger groups, with friction and hard work at each stage. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

87.3 What the Web changes

Geography used to make communities nest: a household in a town in a region in a country. The Web allows groups that do not nest, such as a profession, a medical condition or a global concern. Berners-Lee’s hunch is that a spread across all scales will matter.

Someone (he asks who) suggested the bicycle improved health in the Welsh valleys by widening the gene pool; global travel could instead reduce diversity.

87.4 Looking for a metric

What would effective mean? Two ambitions in one breath:

We would like to preserve world peace by allowing xenophobia to disperse in a web of understanding, while at the same time preserving the diversity of culture which gives the human race its richness. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

Too little global understanding costs war; too little family communication, broken families. The usual “small world” measure, the shortest path between two points, captures global effectiveness but not what he calls the “chewiness”, the structure at every scale in between.

87.5 Kleinberg’s answer, 2000

In March 2000 a “Stop Press” reported Jon Kleinberg’s result. On a grid of cells with links of various lengths, a message reaches any cell in steps growing only with the logarithm of the grid’s size in just one arrangement:

each cell must have the same number of links (on average) to cells 1-10 squares away as to cells 10-100 away, etc. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 2000

Sending a postcard to a stranger in Massachusetts through friends (Figure 87.2):

The person they find in Massachusetts must be able to pass it to people successively closer and closer to the target. this only works if there is connectivity on each scale. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 2000

The proof justifies one metric, the number of hops, like those between a problem and its solution in a large organisation. Chewiness remains unmeasured.

In 2007 he added: think of communities, not one domain of discourse. Tagging a photo of his car, offering it to a car pool and registering it with the Registry of Motor Vehicles probably use three ontologies. A message mixes global terms such as geo:longitude with local ones (Figure 53.4); how much another agent can reuse depends on the communities they share.

The answer is a mix of a few ontologies at different scales.

What happened since

  • The Web’s link pattern was measured. Albert, Jeong and Barabási (Nature, 1999), cited in the note, found a power law: a few pages have vast numbers of links. Broder and colleagues mapped the Web graph’s large-scale shape in 2000.
  • Weblogs showed the same curve. Clay Shirky’s 2003 essay Power Laws, Weblogs, and Inequality, listed in the note, found a few weblogs drawing most links.
  • The argument reached the Semantic Web. Berners-Lee and Lalana Kagal published The Fractal Nature of the Semantic Web in AI Magazine, 2007.
  • Half the metric arrived. Kleinberg justified counting hops; no measure of chewiness has been justified.
