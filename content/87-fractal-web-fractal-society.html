The short version

There are two ways for a society to fail. In one, everybody ends up sharing the same language, the same assumptions and the same ideas, and nothing new can arise. In the other, people divide into sealed groups that each make perfect sense internally and cannot speak to each other at all.

This note argues that avoiding both requires groups of every size, from the whole world down to two people, with no gaps. It is offered not as an observation but as a requirement, and its author admits he cannot prove it.

The question

Written in 1998 and appended to at intervals, this note has been cited by a dozen chapters of this book already. It opens by correcting its own title, which is characteristic:

This article was originally entitled "The Fractal nature of the web". Since then, i have been assured that while many people seem to use fractal to refer to a Zipf (1/f) distribution, it should really only be used in spaces of finite dimension The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

And then states what kind of claim it is making, which is the important sentence:

This isn't an observation so much as a requirement. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

87.2 The same tension, in language

The claim is then restated in terms this book has met repeatedly:

Locally defined languages are easy to create, needing local consensus about meaning: only a limited number of people have to share a mental pattern of relationships which define the meaning. However, global languages are so much more effective at communication, reaching the parts that local languages cannot. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

And located where it is felt:

This tension is exemplified in the standards process, when ideas have to be exposed to successively larger and larger groups, with friction and hard work at each stage. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

That is Chapter 88’s subject, Chapter 22’s five models, and Chapter 51’s mixture of vocabularies, all identified here as one phenomenon.

87.3 What the Web changes

The observation that makes this a note about architecture rather than sociology concerns the shape of the possible:

Before, geography constrained groups, so communities nested in two dimensions: a household in a town in a region in a country. Connection without that constraint allows membership of a professional field, a medical condition, or a global concern, none of which nest geographically. The topology that emerges depends on the individual choices of many people, and Berners-Lee’s hunch is that a distribution emphasising all scales will matter.

He also lists other things worth modelling as they pass through such a system, and the list is unexpectedly wide: inherited traits through intermarrying populations, viruses through schools and travelling workers, and problems propagating to someone who has a solution. The suggestion that the bicycle improved health in Welsh valleys by enlarging the effective gene pool is offered with a question mark over who said it.

87.4 The metric he could not find

The closing section asks what effective would mean, and the answer is a pair of ambitions in one breath:

We would like to preserve world peace by allowing xenophobia to disperse in a web of understanding, while at the same time preserving the diversity of culture which gives the human race its richness. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

He then makes the comparison that keeps the note from grandiosity:

These are of course the same classic problems of the management of a large organization, of combining individual creativity with corporate vision. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

And states the cost of getting the balance wrong in the plainest terms in the collection: we pay for insufficient global understanding with war, and for insufficient family communication with broken families. The same failure at two scales, which is what a scale-free claim predicts.

What happened since

  • The measurement was confirmed and refined. Power-law degree distributions in the Web graph were established in the late 1990s and early 2000s, becoming a founding result of network science.
  • The requirement was not met. Attention concentrated sharply in a small number of platforms through the 2010s, which is the monoculture end of Figure 87.1 rather than a continuum.
  • The other failure appeared too. Fragmentation into mutually unintelligible communities became a recognised phenomenon in the same period, so both deaths occurred at once at different scales.
  • The metric is still missing. No agreed measure of the health of a society’s communication structure exists, which is the gap the note identifies and asks for help with.
