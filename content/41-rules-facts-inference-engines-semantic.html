The question

Chapter 40 concluded that inference rules cannot live outside the language. This note, from 1998, shows what happens when you take a rule out of an engine and try to put it on the Web, and finds that a rule is two things wearing one coat.

41.1 A rule contains two separable things

The worked example is kinship. An engine holds a rule about daughters and granddaughters, and Berners-Lee reads it aloud in the way the engine understands it: whenever a new daughter relationship is found, look for the next one up, and conclude the granddaughter relationship, where “conclude” means add to the database.

The trade is stated without regret, because answering arbitrary queries was never the goal:

The goal is to unify everything which can be expressed in classical logic (including more mathematics when we get to it) without futher constraint. Rules and Facts, 1998

And the reason no compromise is available:

A system which tries to constrain the expressive power cannot be universal. Rules and Facts, 1998

41.2 The penguin

The hardest case is a system where adding a fact can make a previous conclusion false. Berners-Lee works it with the standard example: birds fly, a penguin is a bird, so Pingu flies, until the system learns that penguins do not. Such systems rest on defaults, and defaults are implicitly relative to a fixed set of facts.

Two ways of exporting such knowledge are offered, and the first is described in a way that makes its unattractiveness clear. One can export the rule system itself, producing a statement that a certain assertion is unchallenged in a certain corpus by anything contradicting it for birds or any superclass of penguins. That is a reification of the inference system rather than a claim about penguins, and Berners-Lee calls it possibly so unwieldy that only systems using the same engine can use it.

The second is to record the output as a plain statement: that a particular system has concluded that Pingu flies. He notes that a system might risk incorporating such statements, and describes what doing so amounts to:

This is the logical equivalent of declaring, “Well, I don't think such a book exists becase it wasn't in Blackwell's catalog”. Rules and Facts, 1998

People reason this way constantly, and a secure system should not. The alternative he sketches is to obtain a definitive list from a party entitled to give one, which converts an absence of evidence into a positive statement that someone is answerable for.

41.3 Why classical logic, and not as a matter of taste

The note's central claim is put as a denial that this was a preference:

The choice of classical logic for hte Semantic web is not an arbitrary choice among equals. Classical logic is the only way that inference can scale across the web. Rules and Facts, 1998

Two alternatives are ruled out for specific reasons. Fuzzy systems may lack a consistent set of axioms, and Berners-Lee's objection is that such a system tends to believe something more strongly as a function of how often evidence has been presented, which on an open network is a description of how to be manipulated. Closed-world systems do not scale because the scope of a default is typically implicit and differs from fact to fact.

What classical logic buys is a particular social move, and he names it with a phrase that recurs in his writing:

When a fact is presented as a fact, the “Oh yeah?” function of demanding justification can be satsfifed by a roof in a universal language of proof. Rules and Facts, 1998

The concession that follows matters as much as the claim. Non-classical heuristic systems may have been used to discover the proof. Once found, it can be checked as valid by any system. How an answer was reached stops being anyone's business, which is the asymmetry of Chapter 40 doing its work.

41.4 What publishing a rule strips out

The operation at the centre of this chapter is given its plainest statement near the end:

Exposing rules as classic logic facts strips the (pragmatically useful) hint information which controls the actual sequence of operation of a local inference engine. Rules and Facts, 1998

The hint is called pragmatically useful, not worthless. It is discarded because it cannot travel, not because it does not matter. Its owner keeps it; everyone else gets the fact.

The companion note of Chapter 31 names the underlying error in five words, saying that most knowledge representation systems confuse information with inference tips. A rule stored in a table of rules to be fired at a particular moment has had its meaning entangled with its scheduling, and the classification between data and kinds of rule becomes a meta level the language cannot express. Two systems must be able to exchange what a rule means even when neither recognises the other's notion of when to apply it.

What happened since

  • The separation was standardised. The Rule Interchange Format, a W3C Recommendation of June 2010, exists precisely to exchange rules between engines with different execution strategies, which is this chapter's requirement.
  • Non-monotonic reasoning was kept out and let back in narrowly. Chapter 42 describes the scoped form of negation that permits default reasoning without the global closed-world assumption this note rejects.
  • The catalogue argument stayed live. Treating absence from a dataset as evidence of non-existence remains one of the most common errors in practical data work, and the remedy is still the one given here: get a positive statement from a party entitled to make it.
  • The confusion persists. Systems that store policy as ordered rules evaluated by one engine, rather than as statements with a separate execution strategy, remain the norm in access control and routing, with the consequences described in Chapter 32.
