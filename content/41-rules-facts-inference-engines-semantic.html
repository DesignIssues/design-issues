The short version

A reasoning program stores rules that mix two things: what is true, and when to work it out. Only the first can be shared on the Web. The note argues that classical logic is the only way reasoning can scale across the Web: however a proof was found, anyone can check it, and new facts published elsewhere never quietly overturn it.

The question

A program told that birds fly concludes that Pingu, a penguin, flies. Told later that penguins cannot fly, it changes its mind. On the Web, where no one holds all the facts, which of its two answers should anyone else rely on? This 1998 note answers by taking a rule apart, and finds two things wearing one coat.

41.1 Two things wearing one coat

An inference engine is a program that draws new conclusions from the facts in its database. The worked example is kinship. As the engine reads its rule, whenever a new daughter relationship is found, it looks one generation up and concludes the granddaughter relationship, where “conclude” means add to the database. Whether to do this at once or only when someone asks is decided outside the rule, possibly by a person: an out-of-band decision.

Written formally, the two look almost identical (g stands for granddaughter, d for daughter; ∀ reads as for all, |= as is to be concluded when and <= as is true whenever):

FormNotationWhat it means
As a ruleg(a,c) |= d(a,b) & d(b,c)An instruction: on finding the two daughter links, add the granddaughter link.
As a fact∀a,b,c g(a,c) <= d(a,b) & d(b,c)A statement: whenever the two daughter links hold, so does the granddaughter link.

Publishing the fact is a deliberate loss:

Exposing rules as classic logic facts strips the (pragmatically useful) hint information which controls the actual sequence of operation of a local inference engine. Rules and Facts, 1998

The hint is useful, not worthless; it is dropped because it cannot travel. Its owner keeps it, and everyone else gets the fact. Nor is it regretted; answering arbitrary queries was never the goal:

The goal is to unify everything which can be expressed in classical logic (including more mathematics when we get to it) without futher constraint. We must be able to describe hte world, and our hopes and needs and terms and conditions. Rules and Facts, 1998

Classical logic here means the ordinary logic of mathematics, in which a statement once proved stays proved. Quotations keep the draft’s own spelling. And the reason no compromise is available:

A system which tries to constrain the expressive power cannot be universal. Rules and Facts, 1998

41.2 The penguin

The hardest case is a system where adding a fact can make a previous conclusion false. The note calls these non-monotonic “logics”, scare quotes included: “Describing them as logics may be regarded by some as questionable”. Their stock of conclusions does not only grow. They rest on defaults, things assumed unless one is told otherwise, and so they are closed-world systems: whatever is not in their fixed set of facts is treated as false.

The note offers two ways to export it:

OptionWhat gets publishedWho can use itProblem
Export the rule systemA report on the engine: in its collection, nothing contradicts “birds can fly” for penguins or any wider groupIn practice, only engines of the same kind (the note calls it unwieldy)It describes the engine’s state, not penguins
Export the outputA particular system has concluded that Pingu fliesAnyoneTreating it as true is the catalogue mistake below

Treating the second as plain truth, Berners-Lee says, amounts to this:

This is the logical equivalent of declaring, “Well, I don't think such a book exists becase it wasn't in Blackwell's catalog”. We do things all the time, but a secure system is unlikely to be set up to incorporate such information. Rules and Facts, 1998

Blackwell’s is an Oxford bookshop; a book missing from its catalogue may still exist. The remedy he sketches is a definitive list from a party entitled to give one, such as the publisher’s list for that year, which turns absence of evidence into a positive statement someone answers for.

41.3 Why classical logic, and not as a matter of taste

The central claim denies that this was a preference:

The choice of classical logic for hte Semantic web is not an arbitrary choice among equals. Classical logic is the only way that inference can scale across the web. Rules and Facts, 1998

Two alternatives are ruled out. Fuzzy systems, which hold beliefs to a degree rather than as true or false, tend to believe something more strongly the more often evidence for it is presented. On an open network that invites manipulation: a claim repeated on a thousand spam pages, for example, would gain strength with every copy.

Closed-world systems do not scale because the scope of a default is usually left implicit and differs from fact to fact. A list showing no flights on Sunday, for example, may be true of one airline’s timetable but not of the world, and a bare “there are none” does not say which list it meant.

What classical logic buys is a social move, named with a phrase that recurs in his writing. When a fact is presented as a fact, the “‘Oh yeah?’ function of demanding justification” can be met by a proof in a universal language. Heuristic systems, which search by rules of thumb rather than strict logic, may have found the proof; once found, any Semantic Web system can check it. The note places heuristic systems above a shared Semantic Web bus (a channel every system plugs into) and classical systems below it.

41.4 Meaning versus scheduling

The companion note of Chapter 31 names the underlying error: most knowledge representation systems confuse information with inference tips. A rule stored in a table to be fired at a particular moment has its meaning tangled up with its scheduling. Two systems must be able to exchange what a rule means even when neither recognises the other’s notion of when to apply it.

The note then raises the obvious objection against itself:

What, they say, will happen when this huge mass of classical logic meets its first inconsistncy? Surely, once you have one staement that A and another somewhere on the web that not A, then doesn't the whole system fall apart? Surely, then you can deduce anything? Rules and Facts, 1998

Chapter 46 gives the answer.

What happened since

  • Rule exchange was standardised in two families. The Rule Interchange Format (W3C, June 2010) has a logic dialect, BLD, matching “publish the fact”, and a production-rule dialect, PRD, whose rules carry actions and a conflict-resolution strategy: the procedure the note would strip.
  • The open world became the default. OWL works on the open-world assumption, under which a missing statement may still be true: the opposite of the closed-world defaults rejected here.
