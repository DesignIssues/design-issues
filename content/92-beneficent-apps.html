The question

A beneficent app is one that works in the interest of the person using it. Who does a program work for, and how would anyone know? This note, begun in 2018, was revisited in 2023 because Solid (Chapter 54) needs the concept.

The word comes from the rules at Berners-Lee’s own institution for experiments with human subjects: besides being informed, the subjects must benefit, so the work must be trying to cure disease rather than generate it. The idea is not foreign to the Web. The HTTP specification calls the browser a user agent, because it acts for the user.

92.1 The moment

On the floppy-disk PCs people bought a program to do something useful or fun: play a game, write an essay, do their taxes. The program sat in the A: drive and the user’s data on a disk in the B: drive. That data could go to other programs, and the program was the user’s tool. It worked for them.

The argument turns on one incident. Quicken was a personal-finance program he had bought:

Until, that is, for me one day when Quicken, the program I had bought ages ago to do my finances and taxes, when it had done my taxes, asked words to the effect of "Are you sure you have enough insurance? Would you like to buy some great insurance?" Beneficent Apps, 2018
That was the end of an era: the era in which I trusted Quicken to be my representative and work on my behalf. Beneficent Apps, 2018

People who have only known web-based tools, he observes of millennials, are so used to those tools working in someone else’s interest that they may take it as the default.

92.2 What a browser owes

The browser is his main example of a beneficent app. Its duties are specific enough to audit, and each can fail silently.

DutyExample of failure
Help the user know which party they are talking toA look-alike bank site passes as the real one
Help the user remember where they have beenA page that cannot be found again
Help the user curate a valuable subset of the WebEverything worth keeping is lost in the flood
Store passwords and keys safelyA password leak
Help the user avoid being tricked, fooled or manipulatedA trick button that signs the user up

Beneficent apps are not the norm on the Web or on mobile devices, yet they are common: every open source application should be one, because it is built by a community for its own use, with the end user’s needs in mind.

92.3 The design question, and the disqualifying condition

The advice is a single question to ask at every choice:

When designing a Beneficent App, always just think at each design choice -- what would the user want the app to do? Beneficent Apps, 2018

The designer must also think of users who differ from them, in technical ability, sociability, situation and personality. Then comes the sentence that decides the matter before any design begins:

If you have an income stream from selling the data from your users, then you are not likely to build a Beneficent App by default. Beneficent Apps, 2018

92.4 The measurement problem

The good version is harder to run as a business, and the reason is not ethics:

It is so easy to make metrics for non-beneficent apps: the engagement level, click-through, the ad revenue or sales revenue they provide. It is more difficult to measure how useful you have been to your user. Beneficent Apps, 2018

A user may become well informed of something important and do nothing the app can observe. Users can be surveyed, and activity can be measured. For an app that organises parties, count the parties; since those are infrequent, measure what leads up to them, then check which measures accompanied good parties. Each candidate can also be tried in A/B testing, where two versions go to different users and the results are compared.

The warning attached names a specific harm:

Did they people building social networks imagine the effects on teenage health of a beauty based economy? Probably not, but now we know that systems build to be happy centers of collaboration can end up being toxic for classes of users. To be beneficent, you have to also do no harm! Beneficent Apps, 2018

92.5 The precedent

Agents bound to a client’s interest already exist:

The concept of apps which work for you, the concept of something which is your agent, is not in fact foreign to our current world. We have it, after all with doctors, and with lawyers. Beneficent Apps, 2018

A doctor takes an oath; a lawyer is bound to put the client first. Berners-Lee offers those bodies of law as a source for anyone drafting such commitments for software. As AI grows more powerful, he adds, it matters more at every step that it works for the user and not for someone else.

His example is an online advertising system that recommends a restaurant: is it the best one for the user, or the winner of an instant auction among restaurants bidding for their custom? The test he proposes is a single question to put to the agent: “who do you work for?”

Hey, Siri, who do you work for? Hey, Alexa, who to you work for? Beneficent Apps, 2018

The note ends by asking whether one can imagine an AI that works for its user. He can, and names it Charlie, the subject of Chapter 93.

What happened since

  • Regulation approached the duty. The EU Digital Services Act, applying in full from 17 February 2024, bans dark patterns in online platform interfaces in Article 25, encoding part of what section 92.3 asks designers to ask themselves.
  • Professional duties were proposed for software. Jack Balkin’s 2016 argument that data holders should be information fiduciaries applies the doctor and lawyer precedent to them. The US Data Care Act, a 2018 bill built on it, did not pass.
  • The measurement problem persists. Products are still largely steered by engagement, the mechanism of Chapter 90.
