The short version

This note lists eight terms Berners-Lee would most like people to use when publishing data: two each for who, when and where, and two for documents. They are chosen for practical reasons, from vocabularies already in use. Data from unrelated sources can be joined wherever it shares one of them. The note is an unfinished first draft.

The question

Volume V ends with the shortest note in the book, three hundred and seventy words, an undated first draft with a gap where the date should be and another where a name should be. It answers, in one page, the question the preceding eight chapters raise: granted all this machinery, what would actually make a difference? The answer is eight or ten well-chosen words.

48.1 A little semantics

The note opens by borrowing a phrase from Jim Hendler, a researcher who helped found the Semantic Web, and then narrowing it:

“A little semantics goes a long way”, says Jim Hendler. He uses the phrase in lots of ways. I'm going to use it to say that the sense that a few ontological terms are so common that they will pull together all kinds of data ion all kinds of places. My Top Ten Terms

An ontology here means an agreed list of terms and their meanings. Each term in this note is a named property, such as a mailbox or a title, and its prefix names the shared vocabulary it comes from: foaf is Friend of a Friend, for people; cal is iCalendar, for events; geo is the W3C basic geo vocabulary, for positions on the Earth; and dc is Dublin Core, for documents.

The organising idea is credited to someone at Mitre whose name the draft leaves as a placeholder. That person calls it loose coupling: connect things through a few shared terms, and leave other connections to future projects. The same person asks “Who, where, when?”, and the terms are chosen against those three words. Figure 48.1 shows what one shared term makes possible.

The note says the terms are chosen because they are important common dimensions, and because data connected using more of them would make the data in the world a great deal more connected. The choice is pragmatic.

The draft has other gaps. foaf:name, the time and place terms and foaf:maker are listed with no explanation at all. The section on time breaks off after noting that there are many complete ontologies of time; the section on place says only that it is a huge one.

48.2 Why an email address, of all things

The mailbox is chosen as the main way to identify a person on grounds that have nothing to do with elegance:

The email address is the practical way most people on the net are identified. Web sites use email callback to establish that someone really does have the given email address. My Top Ten Terms

Email callback is the familiar check: the site emails a link and treats the address as the person’s only once it is clicked. The next sentence stops halfway, “Yes, it isn't perfect, as”, and is never finished. The choice stands on what already works, the same reasoning Chapter 34 uses when it identifies a person by an unambiguous property rather than by an identifier they do not have.

Where the vocabularies come from

The vocabulary for people is chosen for practical reasons too. Friend of a Friend was designed and used bottom-up, to connect people through networks of acquaintance, and was already in use. For documents Berners-Lee takes the choice of others: the Dublin Core group picked their top ten properties for documents, and of those, dc:title is the one he has found most called for. He equates it with the HTML <title> tag, which makes the point that most Web pages already publish this term.

48.3 The economics of the fractal world

The closing line ties this note to Chapter 87, where “fractal” means the same pattern at every scale, from the whole Web down to a single group:

In a fractal world, the total cost of ontologies for any project is reduced bny the fact that ontologies already exist in various commiunities My Top Ten Terms

Nobody builds a vocabulary from nothing. The eight terms here sit at the largest scale, where reuse is nearly free because everyone already has them.

48.4 Why this closes the volume

Volume V has been about logic: what can be expressed, what can be checked, what happens when statements conflict, and how to write it all down. This note sets all of it aside.

This is the least powerful thing that could possibly work: the principle of Chapter 1, applied to vocabulary rather than to language. Two datasets that agree on how to write a date can be joined by anyone, with no further agreement between their publishers.

What happened since

  • The strategy was vindicated at a larger scale. Schema.org, launched in June 2011, is this idea: a small shared vocabulary covering who, where, when and what, adopted because search engines consume it, and now widely deployed.
  • The specific terms fared differently. Titles spread widely: the Open Graph protocol, launched in April 2010, made og:title one of its four required properties. Mailboxes published openly were harvested by spammers, and FOAF came to offer foaf:mbox_sha1sum, a scrambled form of the address that can still be matched but not read.
  • Time got its complete ontology. Time Ontology in OWL, a W3C Recommendation of October 2017, models intervals, durations and calendars in detail: the kind of ontology the note passes over in favour of two simple terms.
