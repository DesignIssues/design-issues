The question

Volume V ends with the shortest note in the book, three hundred and seventy words, an undated first draft with a gap where the date should be and another where a name should be. It is a list of the terms Berners-Lee would most like people to use, and it is included because it answers, in one page, the question the preceding eight chapters raise: granted all this machinery, what would actually make a difference?

The answer is eight or ten well-chosen words.

48.1 A little semantics

The note opens by borrowing a phrase and then narrowing it:

“A little semantics goes a long way”, says Jim Hendler. He uses the phrase in lots of ways. I'm going to use it to say that the sense that a few ontological terms are so common that they will pull together all kinds of data ion all kinds of places. My Top Ten Terms

The idea is loose coupling: connect things through a few shared terms and leave the rest to future projects. The organising question is given in three words, who, where, when, and the terms are chosen against it.

48.2 Why an email address, of all things

The choice of a mailbox as the primary way to identify a person is defended on grounds that have nothing to do with elegance:

The email address is the practical way most people on the net are identified. Web sites use email callback to establish that someone really does have the given email address. My Top Ten Terms

The sentence that follows breaks off mid-thought: yes, it isn't perfect, as. The note never says as what. Whatever the reservation was, the choice stands on the observation that this is what is already working, which is the same reasoning Chapter 34 uses when it identifies a person by an unambiguous property rather than by an identifier they do not have.

The vocabulary itself is chosen for the same reason. Berners-Lee picks two terms from an ontology designed and used in a bottom-up way, connecting people through networks of acquaintance. Not the most rigorous available; the one people had adopted.

For documents he reports the work of others rather than choosing himself. A group sat down to pick their top ten properties for documents, and of the ones they produced, one is the one he has found the most call for. He then equates it with the ordinary markup element of the same meaning, which quietly makes the point that most Web pages already publish this term.

48.3 The economics of the fractal world

The closing line connects this note to Chapter 87 and explains why a short list is a strategy rather than a shortcut:

In a fractal world, the total cost of ontologies for any project is reduced bny the fact that ontologies already exist in various commiunities My Top Ten Terms

Nobody builds a vocabulary from nothing. A project reaches for terms already defined at the scale it needs, and pays only for the terms unique to itself. The eight terms here are the ones at the largest scale, where reuse is nearly free because everyone already has them.

48.4 Why this closes the volume

Volume V has been about logic: what can be expressed, what can be checked, what happens when statements conflict, and how to write it all down. This note sets all of it aside.

Loose coupling through a handful of common terms requires no rules, no proof validation, no inconsistency handling, and no notation beyond the ability to write a statement. It is the least powerful thing that could possibly work, which is the principle of Chapter 1 applied to vocabulary rather than to language. Two datasets that agree on how to write a date can be joined by anyone, forever, with no further agreement between their publishers.

What happened since

  • The strategy was vindicated at a scale the note does not imagine. Schema.org, launched in June 2011, is exactly this idea: a small shared vocabulary covering who, where, when and what, adopted because search engines consume it, and now present on a large fraction of the Web.
  • The specific terms fared differently. Coordinates and titles are universal. Identifying people by mailbox became a privacy hazard rather than a convenience, and the practice of publishing such identifiers openly has largely stopped.
  • Time stayed harder than it looks. Start and end times are widely used, and recurrence, uncertainty and historical dates remain unsolved, which is why the note picks the two simple terms and leaves the many complete ontologies alone.
  • Loose coupling became the default. Joining datasets on a few agreed fields, rather than on a shared schema, is how data integration is now done everywhere, whether or not anyone calls it that.

Connections
