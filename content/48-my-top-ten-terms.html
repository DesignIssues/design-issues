The short version

This note lists eight terms Berners-Lee would most like people to use when publishing data: two each for who, when and where, and two for documents. They are chosen for practical reasons, from vocabularies already in use. Data from unrelated sources can be joined wherever it shares one of them. The note is an unfinished first draft.

The question

The note is titled My Top Ten Terms and names eight. At about 370 words it is the shortest note in this book, an unfinished draft with placeholders where its date and a colleague’s name should be. It still answers the question the preceding eight chapters leave open: with all this machinery for logic in place, what would actually connect the world’s data? Its answer is a handful of words already in use.

48.1 A few words that join everything

The note opens by borrowing a phrase from Jim Hendler, a researcher who helped found the Semantic Web, and then narrowing it:

“A little semantics goes a long way”, says Jim Hendler. He uses the phrase in lots of ways. I'm going to use it to say that the sense that a few ontological terms are so common that they will pull together all kinds of data ion all kinds of places. My Top Ten Terms

An ontology here means an agreed list of terms and their meanings. Each term in this note is a named property, such as a mailbox or a title, and its prefix names the shared vocabulary it comes from: foaf is Friend of a Friend, for people; cal is iCalendar, for events; geo is the W3C basic geo vocabulary, for positions on the Earth; and dc is Dublin Core, for documents.

The organising idea is credited to someone at Mitre whose name the draft leaves as a placeholder. That person calls it loose coupling: connect things through a few shared terms, and leave other connections to future projects. The same person asks “Who, where, when?”, and the terms are chosen against those three words, as important common dimensions: the more data is connected through them, the more connected the world’s data becomes. 48.2 Why an email address, of all things

The mailbox is chosen as the main way to identify a person on grounds that have nothing to do with elegance:

The email address is the practical way most people on the net are identified. Web sites use email callback to establish that someone really does have the given email address. My Top Ten Terms

Email callback is the familiar check: the site emails a link and trusts the address once it is clicked. The next sentence stops halfway, “Yes, it isn't perfect, as”, and is never finished. The choice stands on what already works, the same reasoning Chapter 34 uses when it identifies a person by an unambiguous property rather than by an identifier they do not have.

Where the vocabularies come from

Documents are of course the lifeblood of the Web and the Semantic Web. The first call for structured data, which gave rise to RDF, was for data about documents. My Top Ten Terms

Friend of a Friend was chosen because it was already in use: it was designed and used bottom-up, to connect people through networks of acquaintance. For documents Berners-Lee takes the choice of others: the Dublin Core group picked their top ten properties for documents, and of those, dc:title is the one he has found most called for. He equates it with the HTML <title> tag, which makes the point that most Web pages already publish this term.

48.3 Who pays for a vocabulary

The closing line ties this note to Chapter 87, where “fractal” means the same pattern at every scale, from the whole Web down to a single group:

In a fractal world, the total cost of ontologies for any project is reduced bny the fact that ontologies already exist in various commiunities My Top Ten Terms

Nobody builds a vocabulary from nothing. The eight terms here sit at the largest scale, where reuse is nearly free because everyone already has them.

In the terms of Chapter 1, this is the least powerful means applied to vocabulary. Two datasets that identify a person the same way can be joined by anyone, with no further agreement between their publishers.

What happened since

  • Schema.org, launched in June 2011, applies the same idea at larger scale: a small shared vocabulary covering who, where, when and what, adopted because search engines consume it.
  • The specific terms fared differently. Titles spread widely: the Open Graph protocol, launched in April 2010, made og:title one of its four required properties. FOAF also offers foaf:mbox_sha1sum, a scrambled form of the address that can still be matched but not read; by 2003 the FOAF project recommended it over the plain mailbox, because openly published addresses were harvested by spammers.
  • Time got a W3C standard ontology. Time Ontology in OWL, a W3C Recommendation of October 2017, models intervals, durations and calendars in detail: one more of the kind the note passes over in favour of two simple terms.
