The short version

RDF records facts as three-part statements: subject, property, object (the workshop, began on, 2010-06-26). In 2010 Berners-Lee asked for unused features to be removed, the model to be documented apart from any syntax, a JSON form, and more kinds of thing allowed in each part of a statement. The documentation and JSON arrived in 2014, the removals became advice, and the changes to the statement were not made: they would change every program and dataset built on the old shape.

The question

In 2010 RDF could record that a workshop began on 26 June 2010, but it could not record that 26 June 2010 was a Saturday. Berners-Lee asked for that to change in one line of a note written for W3C’s RDF Next Steps workshop, which he could not attend. Neither RDF 1.1 nor the RDF 1.2 work has made the change. Which of his other requests were adopted shows what a deployed standard can and cannot change.

47.1 Nine requests and a scorecard

The note keeps to a narrow scope:

This does not address the many other exciting things which need to be done in the semantic web, but just the RDF model and its common serializations. The Future of RDF, 2010

A serialisation is a way of writing data down as text, such as RDF/XML or Turtle. The note’s extensions to N3 (Chapter 42), Berners-Lee’s experimental extension of RDF’s notation, are left aside, apart from dates (section 47.4). Figure 47.1 glosses each proposal with its outcome. In the right-hand column, the requests that reach into the statement itself fared worst.

47.2 A date that cannot be a subject

One line changes the model itself:

In the RDF model, add the ability of the subject to take any of the forms allowed for the object. Currently, this means including literals The Future of RDF, 2010

A literal is a plain value, such as a date, a number or a piece of text. The model lets a literal be the object of a statement but not its subject. To say anything about a value, one must first invent a node to stand for it, which in Chapter 38 Berners-Lee called frightful.

47.3 How hard to push

Two of the removals concern bags and sequences, an early way of writing an unordered or numbered group. RDF’s lists (collections, written ( a b c ) in Turtle) now do the ordered job. To deprecate a feature is to mark it as not to be used while leaving it in place for old data, and one paragraph asks how strong that should be:

How strong should the deprecation be? One possibility is for them to be just best practice notes. It will have the great advantage of simplifying the documentation. The Future of RDF, 2010

The benefit claimed is shorter documentation. The stake was live data: deployed formats used these containers (RSS 1.0 lists a feed’s items as an rdf:Seq; Adobe’s XMP metadata lists a photo’s creators as one), so a hard removal would have broken them.

He also proposes a migration path rather than a cliff: a parser could, as an option, read the sequence rdf:_1 :a; rdf:_2 :b as the list ( :a :b ), so programs that understand only lists could still use old data. That is the evolvability argument of Chapter 16: give implementations a way to move rather than a date by which to have moved.

47.4 Making dates easy to write

Under its heading on extending N3, not the RDF model, the note asks that a date or time be writable bare, in the standard form, such as 2010-06-26 or 2010-06-26T12:04Z, without the datatype label normally attached to it. A parser could not confuse such a date with a number: “This format is distinguishable from other numeric forms.” He gives the reason:

Date-times are a common and important datatype and reading and writing them with an explicit datatype is boring. The Future of RDF, 2010

The consequence is not about boredom:

In practice people end up putting them in data on the web as strings which prevents processing software and user agents providing appropriate functionality The Future of RDF, 2010

47.5 Consistency as a constraint on proposals

One line sets a rule for everything else on the list:

Extensions to the RDF model should be done consistently with N3 to avoid unnecessary incompatibility. The Future of RDF, 2010

Two languages exist side by side: N3, the research language, and RDF, the standard. When the standard grows, it should grow towards N3. Without the rule, RDF might add a way of putting a graph inside a statement that is incompatible with N3’s braces, leaving two notations for the same thing. It is the test of independent invention (section 16.5), applied in advance.

47.6 What a standard can change

The outcomes, set out below, fall into three kinds. New documents were adopted: nothing already written has to change for them. Removals became advice, which leaves existing data valid. Every change to what a statement may contain stalled or arrived only in part, because each one alters the shape that deployed programs and datasets are built on. So the one line about Saturday is one that sixteen years have not granted.

What happened since

  • The documentation proposals were carried out. RDF 1.1, a W3C Recommendation of February 2014, defines the model independently of any syntax, and JSON-LD, a JSON serialisation from the same working group, became a Recommendation the month before: both are on this list.
  • The deprecations were softened to advice. Reification, bags and sequences remain in the specification and are avoided by convention, which is the weakest of the options section 47.3 considers.
  • Bare dates were not adopted. Turtle 1.1 (2014) lets numbers and true/false be written bare, but a date still needs its datatype.
  • Literals as subjects are still not allowed. The RDF 1.2 work that W3C began in 2022 kept the restriction; like RDF 1.1 before it, it mentions literal subjects only in an unofficial generalised form.
  • Graphs in statements arrived partially. RDF-star work from 2021 onward lets a single statement, not a whole graph, be described by another (in RDF 1.2, only as its object), which is the narrower half of the proposal here.
