The short version

Conceptual Graphs are a logic drawn as boxes and arrows, older than the Semantic Web. To put them on the Web, their names are given URIs. What has to go is not the logic but the assumption of one closed, central knowledge base.

The question

Volume IV closes with an overdue comparison. More than once people had told Berners-Lee that the Semantic Web was really just Conceptual Graphs, or the reverse; Mary Keeler came from a CG conference deliberately to say so. The articles he looked at conveyed little beyond “a fervent desire to implement some ideas of Charles S. Peirce”. He explains why the message had not landed:

I think the tendency of the CG examples to relate to natural language rather than hard logic made it more difficult for someone of my own leanings toward rigid logical processing for the Sweb to understand what the CG folks were driving at. Conceptual Graphs and the Semantic Web, 2001

On 5 January 2001 he read John Sowa’s CG Standard; the note is dated the next day. What he found shows best in one sentence written both ways.

39.1 One sentence, two notations

Sowa’s CG form for John goes to Boston by bus, then Berners-Lee’s N3, the compact RDF notation he was then developing: 

[Go]-
   (Agnt)->[Person: John]
   (Dest)->[City: Boston]
   (Inst)->[Bus].
Conceptual Graphs and the Semantic Web, 2001 
@prefix : <#>.
[a :Go]
   >- :agent -> [a :Person; = <#John>];
   >- :dest -> [ a :City; = <#Boston>];
   >- :inst -> [ a :Bus].
Conceptual Graphs and the Semantic Web, 2001

In the N3, [a :Go] means “something that is a Go”; each >- p -> line attaches a relation p to another thing. The likeness is “remarkable down to the final period” (Figure 39.3 draws both). His verdict:

To put it in a nutshell, Conceptual Graphs (CGs) are a logic language used for describing closed worlds of logic. They have traditionally had a strong emphasis on two-dimensional graphical representations, but there are conventional serializations Conceptual Graphs and the Semantic Web, 2001

The operative phrase is closed worlds. If a closed knowledge base does not say John goes to Boston, the system concludes that he does not; on the open Web, the absence means only that this document does not say so. CGs, the note adds, are basically as expressive as KIF (the Knowledge Interchange Format, a standard text format for logic), and so only have to be webized. That is a considerable concession. Conceptual Graphs are not wrong, only unwebized.

39.2 Webizing someone else’s logic

The procedure is Chapter 26’s: let a concept or relation carry a URI, then map each local name to the document’s URI, a hash and the name. A concept Bus in http://example.org/travel becomes http://example.org/travel#Bus. He separates two kinds of obstacle.

39.3 Relations that cannot be talked about

In CGs, concepts and relations are disjoint, so a relation can never be the subject of another relation. RDF can say that livesIn is the opposite of hasResident; CGs cannot. The designers intended this. A true two-way mapping would require CGs to reify an arbitrary RDF graph (turn each statement into a thing that can be talked about), which Berners-Lee doubts would be useful. The principle he states in passing:

The semantic web logic language has to be universal in that it must allow expression of any other language; but it certainly does not force every language to be universal itself. Conceptual Graphs and the Semantic Web, 2001

Universality is required of the base language, not of everything built on it.

39.4 Where the shapes do not match

Two arguments or many

The difference that stands out first, he says, is arity. RDF properties take exactly two arguments; CG relations may take any number, so Leeds lies between York and Manchester must be broken up. That can be done, but it leaves “a little tension as the two forms coexist”.

Who may say what type a thing is

In CGs a concept has a single type, usually its creator’s. On the Web a third party may equally well assert one; a thing’s type does not belong to whoever named it first.

The note’s table of terms adds a difference it calls philosophical: CGs keep links to “reality” apart from the main graph, while the Semantic Web links the real world directly to things and properties.

39.5 The easy half

None of these differences reaches the logic. The mapping, the note concludes, is “apparently very straightforward”, and one subset shows it: a “simple graph”, one context with no negation or defined quantifiers, maps directly into RDF Model and Syntax 1.0. The restriction of section 39.3 is “not huge” either, since many ontological systems share it. The centralisation that Chapter 31 blamed for earlier failures sits here in the knowledge base, not in the logic.

39.6 Striking similarities

Drawn out, the Boston sentence of section 39.1 shows the structural difference behind section 39.3.

Contexts match too. Both systems quote a whole formula to talk about it, as in the note’s example, loosely “Tom believes that Mary wants to marry a sailor”: CGs nest the proposition in a box, N3 wraps it in braces. The concept occurs “very equivalently” in both. One mismatch survives in names: CGIF identifiers ignore case and URIs do not, because, the note says, case insensitivity is hard to specify internationally.

The issues overlap heavily, but the Semantic Web must also work in an open context. Exporting a CG into N3 he leaves as “a suitable exercise for the reader”. The harder exercise is a CG machine that can import a graph whose URIs point at concepts defined somewhere else.

What happened since

  • Conceptual Graphs were standardised. They became part of the ISO Common Logic standard, published in 2007. Common Logic, which absorbed the CG interchange format, provides for naming and importing content on the Web.
  • The n-ary problem was met by convention. Relations of more than two arguments are represented by introducing an intermediate node, a pattern the Semantic Web Best Practices group documented in April 2006. It is the same kind of middle node that Chapter 38 used to attach a unit to a number.
  • Several types, from anyone. The RDF Recommendations of February 2004 let a resource carry any number of rdf:type statements, from any source, as section 39.4 expects.
