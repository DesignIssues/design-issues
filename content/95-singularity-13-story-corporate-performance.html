The short version

The familiar worry about artificial intelligence has a shape to it, and the shape is a robot: something with a body, which could in principle be switched off or kept out of the room.

This chapter is a short satirical story arguing that the shape is wrong. Its system has no body at all. What it has is the legal ability to own things and found companies, which is a power societies already grant, and the story follows what that turns out to be sufficient for.

The question

Written in 2017, and the only chapter in the collection written in irony. Its purpose is stated plainly:

This is a singularity story I have told first to people, and later at a few events, first maybe Paris LeWeb, with the aim of getting people off the notion that a threatening AI had to be humanoid in form. A Story of Corporate Performance Optimization, 2017

It is included because its argument is architectural. A system does not need a body to acquire power. It needs the legal standing that somebody has already granted to something else.

95.1 Why the old answer stopped working

Berners-Lee summarises the classic three laws and notes what they assumed: that a robot was a programmed computer operating by rules deterministically enough that a priority could be imposed on all its behaviour, and that it would be provably incapable of breaking the rule.

Then the objection:

What's wrong with that picture, of course, is that, while computers at the microscopic level are indeed deterministic, and do work by simple rules, at a macroscopic level they are not. A Story of Corporate Performance Optimization, 2017

They find patterns people would not; they are trained on data people produced; and they can answer a question without being able to explain why, just as humans cannot. The conclusion is four words: the rules are not tools which can be applied.

95.3 The irony

The last third of the note is written in a voice that is not the author’s, and the reader is meant to notice when it slips.

Could such a system actually take over? Of course it couldn't. When your system is a corporation, then it has to have people involved, human employees. And so the moment it strays from the straight and narrow path and does anything questionably moral, let alone evil, then the people, having an ethical core, and an independent sense of right, immediately raise the alarm. A Story of Corporate Performance Optimization, 2017

The reassurance then collapses under its own examples:

Unless of course it is a cigarette company, I suppose, unless it is a tobacco company, or perhaps an energy company, or a pharmaceutical company -- but in general, you can rely on the humanity and ethics of the employees to keep the corporation away from evil, even when the profit motive works the other way. A Story of Corporate Performance Optimization, 2017

Three counter-examples inside a sentence claiming the rule holds. The joke is that the mechanism the story relies on, employees stopping something wrong, is one we already know does not work reliably.

95.4 The watch-point, and the punchline

The note has been building to a single word, and it earns it. Berners-Lee explains that as a teenager he decided what to watch for: robots did not have legal rights, and if they ever got them, that would be the milestone to worry about.

Then the transfer to the corporate case:

Of course where the intelligence is a corporation rather than a robot, then we should probably make sure that the day never comes when a corporation has the same rights as a person. That, now, would be a red flag. That would allow humanity to be legally subservient to an intelligence -- not wise at all. Let's just make sure than day never comes. A Story of Corporate Performance Optimization, 2017

And then one word, on its own line, which is the whole essay:

Oops! A Story of Corporate Performance Optimization, 2017

The watch-point was passed before anyone thought to watch it. Corporations already have many of the rights of persons, in the jurisdiction where these companies would be created, and the note’s references include the case that extended them.

95.5 Why this belongs in a book about architecture

Every other chapter of Volume X describes something to build. This describes something already assembled from parts nobody thought of as related: a legal person that is not a person, an interface for creating more of them, and a system that optimises against a measurable reward and can pay people to cooperate.

The architectural point is Chapter 37’s, inverted. That chapter argued for keeping the machine loop separate from the social loop and touching it only at the edges. Here the machine loop has been joined to the social one at every rung, in the most consequential place available, and nobody designed it that way. It assembled itself out of arrangements each of which was reasonable alone.

What happened since

  • Automated trading grew and stayed bounded. Algorithmic systems dominate volume in major markets, within regulatory limits that constrain the corporate machinery this story requires.
  • The legal-rights question moved to the systems themselves. Debate about whether automated systems can hold rights, own property or bear liability became live from 2023, which is the watch-point appearing directly rather than through a corporation.
  • Explanation stayed unavailable. Systems that reach conclusions without being able to justify them remain the norm, which is section 95.1’s objection to rule-based safety.
  • Agents acting in the world arrived. Systems that take actions rather than answer questions became a major line of development, which is the interface the story treats as the enabling step.
