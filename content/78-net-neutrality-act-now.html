The question

This is not a design note. It is a call to action, published on 10 December 2017, two days before a regulator voted on removing the rules it defends. It is included at chapter length because the architectural argument inside it is the clearest statement in the collection of why a market boundary matters.

78.1 The personal argument

Berners-Lee makes the case from his own position, and the sentence is the most quoted thing he wrote about this subject:

When I invented the World Wide Web in 1989, I didn't have to pay a fee, or ask anyone for permission to make it available over the internet. All I had to do was write a new app and plug my computer into the net. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

The counterfactual follows immediately:

If US net neutrality rules are repealed, future innovators will have to first negotiate with each ISP to get their new product onto an internet package. That means no more permissionless space for innovation. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

Permissionless is the operative word, and it is the same property Chapter 1 calls decentralisation and Chapter 49 relies on when it says anyone may publish without asking.

78.3 Why it was not needed at first

One paragraph explains why the principle had to be written down only later, and it is a general observation about how norms become rules:

In the early years of the internet, ISPs didn't have the technical capacity to discriminate traffic online. Their computers were not fast enough and so net neutrality was a fact of life. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

Neutrality was an accident of capability. When the capability arrived, the incentive was already there, and Berners-Lee names it: as the value of the content flowing through the network increased, carriers developed both the ability and the reason to take a cut.

A property that held because nobody could break it is not a property. It is a gap that eventually closes, and at that moment somebody must decide whether to write the rule down.

78.4 What the argument asks for

Berners-Lee’s statement of what he wants is three sentences of parallel construction, and it is the clearest thing in the piece:

I want an internet where content businesses grow according to their quality, not their ability to pay to ride in the fast lane. I want an internet where ideas spread because they're inspiring, not because they chime with the views of telecoms executives. I want an internet where consumers decide what succeeds online, and where ISPs focus on providing the best connectivity. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

Each sentence names a decision and says who should make it. Quality decides, not payment. Readers decide, not carriers. Consumers decide, and carriers do the thing they are good at.

78.5 The tactical note

The last section is unusual for this collection in being about lobbying, and it contains one observation of some coldness:

Some, more than others, understood the importance of preserving net neutrality. But they all understand how many votes they need to stay in office. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

Understanding is unevenly distributed, and the incentive to respond is not. The instruction that follows is four words long: not tomorrow, not next week, now.

Chapter 15 argued that every system has a technical protocol and a social one. This is the same author operating on the social protocol directly, because the technical one has no defence against a change in the law.

What happened since

  • The vote went the other way. The rules were repealed on 14 December 2017, four days after this was published.
  • The predicted collapse did not immediately follow. Blocking and throttling of competitors did not become widespread in the years after, though zero-rating arrangements, which favour some content, did.
  • The rules came back and went again. They were readopted in April 2024 and then vacated by a court in January 2025, leaving the question open.
  • Europe held the line. The European Union’s open internet regulation has applied since 2016 and was not repealed, so the separation of the two markets described in Figure 78.1 persists there.
