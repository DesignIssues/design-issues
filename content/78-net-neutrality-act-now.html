The short version

Net neutrality is “the principle that internet service providers (ISPs) treat all traffic equally”: an ISP must not block sites, slow them down, or charge them to go faster. Berners-Lee wrote this as the US regulator was about to drop those rules. Keeping the carrier out of the content business, he argues, keeps both markets competitive.

The question

This is not a design note but a call to action, dated 10 December 2017, in the days before the US Federal Communications Commission (FCC) voted on 14 December to remove the rules it defends. Should the company that carries a reader’s data be allowed to decide which sites reach the reader, and how fast?

78.1 The personal argument

Berners-Lee makes the case from his own experience:

When I invented the World Wide Web in 1989, I didn't have to pay a fee, or ask anyone for permission to make it available over the internet. All I had to do was write a new app and plug my computer into the net. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

He then says what would change:

If US net neutrality rules are repealed, future innovators will have to first negotiate with each ISP to get their new product onto an internet package. That means no more permissionless space for innovation. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

Permissionless (needing no one’s approval to launch) is the operative word: the decentralisation of section 1.5. Figure 78.1 follows one newcomer through both worlds.

78.2 The architectural argument

The core of the note is about markets:

Net neutrality separates the connectivity market from the content market. As separate markets, both have flourished. But if the US allows the internet to become like the old cable TV model — with the same firms controlling the cables and the content — competition in both markets will suffer. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

Connectivity is what a customer pays an ISP for; content is what the customer reaches through it. Under the cable television model one firm controls both.

The note names three things the FCC told ISPs they could not do: “blocking content, throttling site speed, or charging for content to be prioritised”.

78.3 Why it was not needed at first

The note explains why the rule came late:

In the early years of the internet, ISPs didn't have the technical capacity to discriminate traffic online. Their computers were not fast enough and so net neutrality was a fact of life. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

Neutrality was an accident of slow hardware. As technology improved and content grew more valuable, carriers gained the ability and the incentive to take a cut.

Once carriers could favour some sites, neutrality would last only if someone wrote it down as a rule. Figure 78.3 shows who did, and when.

78.4 What the argument asks for

His statement of what he wants is the clearest thing in the piece:

I want an internet where content businesses grow according to their quality, not their ability to pay to ride in the fast lane. I want an internet where ideas spread because they're inspiring, not because they chime with the views of telecoms executives. I want an internet where consumers decide what succeeds online, and where ISPs focus on providing the best connectivity. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

The instruction that follows is short: “act now. Not tomorrow, not next week. Now.”

78.5 The tactical note

The last section, unusually, is about lobbying:

Some, more than others, understood the importance of preserving net neutrality. But they all understand how many votes they need to stay in office. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

Some politicians understood the issue and some did not, but all of them respond to voters. So the note asks readers to contact their representatives. Chapter 15 argued that every system has a technical protocol and a social one. This is the same author operating on the social protocol directly, because the technical one has no defence against a change in the law.

What happened since

  • The vote went the other way. The FCC voted to repeal the rules on 14 December 2017 (effective June 2018). Several US states then passed neutrality laws of their own, California’s in 2018.
  • The rules came back and went again. They were readopted in April 2024 and then struck down by the US Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit in January 2025, leaving the question open.
  • Europe held the line. The European Union’s open internet regulation has applied since 2016 and was not repealed, so the separation of the two markets described in Figure 78.2 persists there.
