The short version

The proposal in the previous chapter sounds, to anyone who knows the history, like a repeat of experiments that already failed. Machines that understand us; systems that reason about everything; the grand encyclopaedia of facts. Those projects were real, and they did fail.

This chapter answers six such objections, and the method is the interesting part. Each fear is granted rather than denied, and then the assumption that caused the original failure is shown to be one the new plan never makes. In every case the claim turns out to be smaller than the thing being feared.

The question

Berners-Lee describes this note in his own subtitle as parenthetical, written so as not to disturb the flow of what a Semantic Web is. It answers the objections, and its framing of why they need answering is worth noting:

Where the other models are related to previous unmet promises of computer science, now passed into folk law as unsolvable problems, they suggest a fear that the goal of a Semantic Web is inappropriate. What the Semantic Web can represent, 1998

The objections are not technical mistakes. They are memories of earlier failures, and the note’s method is to grant each failure and identify the assumption that caused it.

31.1 Six denials

31.2 Not artificial intelligence

The first denial is the one the note is remembered for:

The concept of machine-understandable documents does not imply some magical artificial intelligence which allows machines to comprehend human mumblings. It only indicates a machine's ability to solve a well-defined problem by performing well-defined operations on existing well-defined data. What the Semantic Web can represent, 1998

And the cost is stated in the same breath, which is the part usually left out. Instead of asking machines to understand people’s language, it involves asking people to make the extra effort. The whole programme’s difficulty is in that clause.

31.3 Complete language, limited applications

The second denial separates two things that objectors run together. The language must be complete, capable of expressing paradox and tautology, because it must represent the world. Berners-Lee accepts the consequence frankly: it will be possible to phrase questions whose answers would require a search of the entire Web and an unimaginable amount of time.

This should not deter us, he says, from making the language complete. Each application uses a schema to restrict itself to a deliberately limited language. Completeness at the bottom, restraint at the top.

31.4 The globalisation argument

The central claim of the note is an analogy, and it is the clearest statement anywhere of what the Semantic Web was meant to be:

Knowledge representation is a field which is currently seems to have the reputation of being initially interesting, but which did not seem to shake the world to the extent that some of its proponents hoped. It made sense but was of limited use on a small scale, but never made it to the large scale. This is exactly the state which the hypertext field was in before the Web. What the Semantic Web can represent, 1998

And the prescription:

The Semantic Web is what we will get if we perform the same globalization process to Knowledge Representation that the Web initially did to Hypertext. We remove the centralized concepts of absolute truth, total knowledge, and total provability, and see what we can do with limited knowledge. What the Semantic Web can represent, 1998

Three things are given up in that sentence, and the giving up is the design. Chapter 26 gives the recipe in general form; this is its application to a whole field.

The diagnosis of the earlier systems is specific rather than dismissive. Many knowledge representation systems could not merge two knowledge bases because the model held that a concept had one and only one place in a tree of knowledge. So they did not scale and did not pass the test of independent invention. He names KIF as centralised in its approach and observes that the Cyc ontology suggests everyone agree on the same terms for common English words, which RDF does not.

31.5 On inconsistency, and what he will not concede

The sharpest passage answers the objection that predicate calculus is intolerant of contradiction, where anything follows from a contradiction, while the Web is full of them.

Berners-Lee refuses the fuzzy answer. A real system will work not by believing anything it reads but by checking the source, and a rule will let a system infer things only from statements of a particular form signed by particular keys. Within such a system inconsistency is a serious problem, not something to be worked around. His examples are domestic and then abrupt: if my bank says my balance is one hundred dollars and my computer says two hundred, we need to figure out the problem. Same with launching missiles.

He also identifies a confusion in earlier systems that is still common. Most of them store a rule not as a statement but as an entry in a table of rules to be applied at a particular time, which makes the classification of rules a meta level that the language itself cannot express. Two systems must be able to interchange what a rule means even when neither recognises the other’s notion of when to apply it.

31.6 The closed world

The note names, in its opening, the difference it treats as fundamental between the Semantic Web and most programming data models: the closed world assumption. A database that does not list a fact treats it as false. An open system may only treat it as unknown.

That assumption is what makes the mappings in Chapter 33 approximate rather than exact, and it is the reason the deduction in Chapter 7 could not default to treating a URI as a page.

What happened since

  • The denial had to be repeated. The claim that the Semantic Web was artificial intelligence by another name persisted for twenty years, and the distinction this note draws, between operating on well-defined data and comprehending language, is now the distinction between knowledge graphs and language models.
  • Both approaches arrived, separately. Systems that understand human mumblings were built after all, and they are not this. The two are now often combined, with structured data used to ground and check what a language model produces.
  • The globalisation argument held. Decentralised vocabularies that anyone may extend, and merging by mapping rather than by agreement, are how large-scale data integration is now done, whether or not RDF is used.
  • Inconsistency was handled by provenance, as predicted. Production systems record who said what and filter by source, which is section 31.5’s prescription, arrived at by necessity.

Questions

31.2 The language must be complete while applications stay limited. Explain why limiting the language instead would fail, using the test of independent invention.

31.3 Three things are given up: absolute truth, total knowledge, total provability. For each, name something that becomes impossible.

31.4 Berners-Lee refuses to tolerate inconsistency and requires source checking instead. Describe a case where a system must act despite contradictory sources, and say what it should do.
