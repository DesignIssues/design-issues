The short version

Between 1994 and 1998, web pages gained tables, colours, frames and a dozen other features, none of which anybody had agreed on. Companies simply invented them, protected by a rule inherited from electronic mail: anything not understood is to be ignored rather than rejected.

That rule is why the Web grew so fast. It is also why nothing could ever be relied on. This note describes the cycle that resulted, explains why it had to end, and sets out the thought experiment its author used to test every design he made.

The question

This note is dated March 1998 and was the basis of Berners-Lee’s keynote at the seventh World Wide Web conference (WWW7) that April; the status line calls it an incomplete first draft. It examines the founding mandate of the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), the body that coordinates the Web’s standards:

The World Wide Web Consortium was founded in 1994 on the mandate to lead the Evolution of the Web while maintaining its Interoperability as a universal space. Evolvability, 1998

Interoperability was well understood. Evolvability was a word without a technique, and this note is the attempt to give it one.

16.1 The old rule, and what it bought

The Web inherited one rule from Internet mail. A mail header is one of the labelled lines, such as From: or Subject:, at the top of a message, and Berners-Lee states the rule as he found it:

A rule explicit or implcit in all the email-like Internet protocols has always been that if you found a mail header (or something) which you did not understand, you should ignore it. Evolvability, 1998

He calls this free extension, adopted it for HTML, and credits it with HTML’s fast growth. He is equally clear about the bill:

It has its advatage of rapid prototyping and incremental deployment, and the disadvantage of ambiguity, confusion, and an inability to add a mandatory feature to an existing protocol. Evolvability, 1998

The last clause matters most. Nothing can be marked as having to be understood, so no feature can ever be relied upon.

16.2 The HTML cycle

The note’s best passage is an account of how HTML actually grew between 1994 and 1998, as browser makers added new tags (a tag is the bracketed label, such as <table>, that marks out part of a page). It is a cycle with a motor, and the motor is the tension between wanting to beat a competitor and needing to interoperate with one.

The worked example is the table tag (step 3 of Figure 16.1). Rival browsers had each added tables their own way, so a browser meeting <table> could not tell which the author meant. And if the table mattered, the server never learned whether it had been handled, since a browser that did not know the tag silently ignored it.

16.3 The other extreme, and why it also fails

The note then looks at distributed object systems, such as remote procedure call (RPC), where every interface is declared in machine-readable form and checked when the system is compiled. The verdict is even-handed:

With well-defined interfaces, either everything works, or nothing. Evolvability, 1998

That is certainty, bought at a price: change an interface and the other side must change too, which is why such systems never spread, in Berners-Lee’s phrase, in a web-like or virus-like manner. The requirement is therefore a demand for both:

One must be able to freely extend a language, but do so with an extension language which is itself well defined. Evolvability, 1998

That sentence is the design brief for XML namespaces, a way of labelling each set of tags with the URI of the vocabulary it comes from. Chapter 17 is the specification of it.

16.4 Partial understanding

The note’s test case is an invoice. Most of it follows a well-known invoice vocabulary, but each item also carries a part description in the supplier’s own private vocabulary. An invoice program should be able to process it without understanding the part description. The obvious fix, marking that part “optional”, fails because optionality depends on who is reading.

The precise version, then, is a transformation. The invoice system in Figure 16.3 does exactly this: it deletes the part description and is left with a valid invoice. That deletion is the transformation.

The rules for such a conversion could be written in anything from the crudest language to one that is Turing complete, able to express any computation. Berners-Lee applies the rule from Chapter 1:

When expressing something, use the least powerful language you can. Evolvability, 1998

His smallest case is a one-line rule that says, in effect, delete this part. If the rule is allowed, the deleted part was optional. The section then breaks off, marked “to be continued”; section 16.7 takes the idea up.

16.5 The test of independent invention

Chapter 1 introduced this thought experiment in one line; here it gets its full form.

Suppose another group, unknown to the first, designs the same sort of thing. Being just as smart, they make the same important architectural decisions, since those follow from logic.

But they make every arbitrary decision differently: every convention that could have gone either way but must match on both sides, like the side of the road. In Berners-Lee’s list, they drive on the other side, flip one bit of every character code, put red buoys on the other side of the channel, and use a different number of lines on their television screens. Then the two systems meet.

The Web survives the meeting because of the third axiom of Chapter 2: URI space is universal but need not be unique, so the rival space can be folded in, as the foot of Figure 16.4 shows.

Without the test, worldwide interoperability comes only when one design beats the others, or when a worldwide committee designs the whole thing top down before anyone starts. That works for a new idea, the note says, but not for practices centuries old: the library community has had endless trouble agreeing on a single library card format, the MARC record. The other route is for one system to be dropped. When Europe abandoned its ISO networking protocols in the late 1980s, because they could not interwork with the Internet, much work was lost. Arbitrary choices can be reversed, but at national cost:

Sweden actually changed from driving on the left to driving on the right. Evolvability, 1998
The test of independent invention is not just a thought experiment, it is happening all the time. Evolvability, 1998

16.6 Two assumptions

Midway, the note derives its requirements from two premises. The first is that we will be smarter in the future. Today that produces a flag day: a version 4 word processor refuses a file saved by version 5, and everyone must upgrade at once. The requirement is that version 4 read the version 5 file, at least in part. The second premise is more unusual, and he flags the difficulty:

The philosophical assumption that we may not be smarter than everyone else (a huge step for some!) leads us to realise that others will have gret ideas too, and will independently invent the same things. Evolvability, 1998
AssumptionWhat the technology must handle
We will be smarter in the futureMoving from version 1 to version 2
We are not smarter than everyone elseMoving between parallel versions A and B, invented independently

Humility, here, is a technical requirement.

16.7 Six kinds of schema

The second half of the note turns to schemas. A schema is the document that describes a language; an XML document points to its schema by URI. The note ranks six kinds by how much a machine can do with them.

Even the bottom step lets software check whether it knows the language, which is all most RPC systems do. The second matters more than it looks: in the end, the note argues, what a document commits to must be defined in human-readable legal language. The fourth is the one section 16.4 was waiting for:

All you need to do is to mark in the schema of a new version of the language which elements of the langauge can be ignored if you don't understand them. Evolvability, 1998

Old software can then filter a new document down to one it understands, which solves what Berners-Lee calls the elusive version 2 to version 1 conversion. A Turing-complete converter can do more, but nobody can see its rules, so it must be trusted. What excites him is the top step: stating, in machine-processable form, which terms in one language are equivalent to terms in another (for illustration, a rule that one vocabulary’s price means another’s cost). That is the programme of RDF and the Semantic Web.

What happened since

  • The mechanism shipped and was then declined. XML Namespaces became a W3C Recommendation in January 1999. HTML5, whose first Recommendation came in October 2014, does not use them, which is the fork described in Chapter 23.
  • Partial understanding was solved by other means. Versioned wire formats such as Protocol Buffers, first released publicly in July 2008, make forward compatibility a property of the encoding: unknown fields are kept rather than merely ignored (proto3 at first discarded them, until version 3.5 in 2017). That is closer to what section 16.4 asked for than anything the Web adopted.
  • The cycle did not stop. Vendor-prefixed CSS properties through the late 2000s reproduced it exactly. Browser makers then moved away from prefixes towards hiding experimental features behind settings flags; Chrome’s Blink engine adopted that policy at its launch in 2013.
