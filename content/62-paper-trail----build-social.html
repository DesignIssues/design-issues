The question

Chapter 61 asked for a way to formalise a sequence of messages. This note, from February 1999, is the proposal, and its status line describes it as an example of how a social machine can be made without a centre.

The observation it rests on is one sentence:

Social processes look like state machines. However, they don't exist as a state variable stored in one place, but as a trail of documents. Paper Trail, 1999

62.1 No state variable anywhere

The consequence is drawn immediately, and it is the thing that makes the design decentralised without anyone having to make it so:

You know the true state of the machine only if you have access to the latest documents. Paper Trail, 1999

Berners-Lee adds a parenthesis worth noticing: this is not the problem being addressed, this is real life being modelled. The absence of a single place holding the truth is not a limitation of the design. It is an accurate description of how organisations work, and any system that assumes otherwise is modelling something else.

62.2 Prerequisites and hints

The schema carries two different kinds of thing, and the distinction is the design’s neatest feature. Prerequisites say what must exist for a document to be valid, expressed in terms of other documents. Hints say what kinds of document you may make from this one, which are the state transitions.

Validity is checked; transitions are offered. The first is enforcement and the second is a user interface, and putting both in one place means the interface cannot drift from the rules.

The promotion example shows how requirements compose: moving a draft onward needs everything the previous step needed, plus endorsement by particular parties. Nothing is restated; the new schema refers to the old.

62.3 The general form

Halfway through, the note stops being about documents and gives the mathematics, which is the same in either reading:

A protocol defines a state as a function of a message, the previous state and the time. Equivalently, it defines the state as a function of all the messages to date. The second formulation is the paper trail: there is no state variable, only the messages and a function.

Berners-Lee then ranks what the state might be, and the ranking is a piece of editorial opinion smuggled into a technical note:

The state could be a logical formula, an RDF graph, or an XML document, or just a number, in decreasing order of interest. Paper Trail, 1999

A number tells you where you are. A formula tells you what follows.

The worked example is a network connection, where the state must hold what is needed to compute future states, and may on completion hold either just a terminal marker or the whole transfer. Which is to say: a protocol’s state may be a receipt or an archive, and that is a design choice rather than a fact.

62.4 Why it is a social machine

The reason this belongs in Volume VI rather than in a chapter on workflow is that it describes a process nobody administers. There is no server holding the status of the publication, no privileged party to ask. Each document is checkable by anyone who can fetch the documents it points at, and the transitions available are readable from the schema.

Chapter 37 argued that the machine loop should be kept closed and joined to the social world only at the edges. This is what that looks like when the process being modelled is itself social: the loop is the documents, and the edges are the moments a person presses the button.

What happened since

  • The pattern won without the vocabulary. Version control systems are paper trails: state is a function of the commits, no variable holds it, and validity is checkable by anyone with the history.
  • Append-only became an architecture. Event sourcing, where current state is derived from a log of events rather than stored, is section 62.3’s second formulation, and became a standard pattern in the 2010s.
  • Notarisation became cheap. Signed commits, transparency logs and timestamping services supply the check-and-record step this note assumes, at a scale it does not anticipate.
  • Rules-in-the-schema remained rare. Most workflow systems still hold their rules in code rather than in a document the participants can read, which is the part that would make the machine social rather than administered.
