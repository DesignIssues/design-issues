The question

In October 2001 Berners-Lee spent a few hours reading fifty pages of a calendar specification with a view to evaluating proposals for putting it into markup. The note is what he found, and it is included because it is the only worked example in the collection of the analysis Volume IV keeps recommending.

My conclusion early on was that the spec should be written in terms of RDF properties, particularly as it has a clear property/value and parameter/value structure. Building an RDF model: A quick look at iCalendar, 2001

Early on, from a specification that never mentions the subject. The structure was already there.

The summary lists what he noticed, and every item is a failure mode that recurs in other specifications.

The second is the one that costs most. Berners-Lee says plainly that a summary of properties with their domains and ranges would make the specification much clearer, and it is missing because prose specifications describe fields where they are used rather than collecting them.

The third is a specific and serious kind of defect:

The parameter value type of "URI" implicitly causes dereferencing. This is not clear from the spec but is assumed by the examples. Building an RDF model: A quick look at iCalendar, 2001

A network request is triggered by a value's type, and the only place that is visible is in an example. Chapter 21's rule about specifying meaning rather than behaviour is being broken in the opposite direction: behaviour is happening and nothing specifies it.

63.3 The line that stops general parsing

One quoted sentence from the specification draws the sharpest reaction:

"Each property defines the specific ABNF for the parameters allowed on the property" This makes general parsing impossible, direct conversion into XML difficult. Building an RDF model: A quick look at iCalendar, 2001

If every field defines its own grammar for its own parameters, no program can parse the format without knowing every field. There is no generic reader, only readers that implement the whole specification, and each new field is a change to every one of them.

The mail header problem, where each header has its own syntax and general processing is impossible, appears in Chapter 32 as a symptom of a missing model. Here it is the same defect, reproduced in a format designed thirty years later.

63.4 The absence that worries him most

The closing observation in the summary is not about syntax at all:

I am concerned that I have not found very much protocol defining what how agents interact, or what a message containing a calendar entry means. Building an RDF model: A quick look at iCalendar, 2001

A format for describing a meeting, and nothing saying what it means to send one to somebody. That is the gap Chapter 61 names and Chapter 62 proposes filling, found in the wild, in the one application where everyone has experienced the consequences.

What happened since

  • The mapping was built. An RDF calendar workspace, which the note's own preface mentions, began in late 2002 with running code, example data and schemas.
  • The scheduling protocol existed and did not help. Companion specifications for scheduling were standardised, and calendar interoperability failures remain among the most familiar in computing, which is the concern of section 63.4.
  • Per-property grammars persisted. The format is still parsed by readers that know every property, and extensions still require changes to each.
  • Recurrence remained the hard part. The nested rule Berners-Lee flags as a structure escaped into a string is still where calendar implementations disagree most.
