The question

This note answers the objection that everyone raises, and Berners-Lee states it in the reader's own voice before answering:

What, many people ask, will happen when this huge mass of classical logic meets its first inconsistncy? Surely, once you have one staement that A and another somewhere on the web that not A, then doesn't the whole system fall apart? Inconsistent data, 1998

The answer is that the objection is correct about logic and wrong about what a system does with documents.

46.1 Where the objection goes wrong

This fear of course is quite valid - or would be if all assertions in the whole world were regarded as bing on equal footing. Some imagine that an RDF parser will simply search all XML documents on the web for any facts, and add them to a massive set of belived assertions. This is not how realisic systems will actually work. Inconsistent data, 1998

The correction is a chain. A fact is asserted in an expression, which is part of a formula, which is found by parsing a document. And then the sentence that does the work:

There is no a priori reason to believe any document on the web. Inconsistent data, 1998

A reason to believe a document is itself information about that document, which is a statement found in another document, and so on. Belief is not a property of facts encountered; it is something conferred by a traceable chain.

46.2 Forwards and backwards

Two ways of organising such a system are named, and they behave quite differently under attack.

46.3 Scoping trust to a key and a form

Signature adds the security step, and Berners-Lee is precise about the first requirement: a document is not endorsed without recording the checksum it had when it was believed. Endorsing a location endorses whatever appears there later; endorsing a checksum does not.

Then the rules themselves, which are quoted in the note as sentences rather than as syntax:

“whatever any document says of the form xxxx is a meber of W3C so long as it is signed wiht key 32457934759432”. Inconsistent data, 1998

Trust is not granted to a party but to a party for a purpose. Berners-Lee makes the point twice, with a second example about employment, because the narrowness is what limits the damage.

46.4 The choice he does not make

The centre of the note is a dilemma left open, and both horns are bad in ways worth understanding.

RuleCost
Believe statements of this form signed with this keyBureaucratic: the form must be preserved, so equivalent statements cannot be substituted
Believe what can be inferred from information signed with this keyOne contradiction under that key makes everything derivable from it

The first breaks a property we normally rely on. Berners-Lee puts it as a preference we have to give up: normally we like to be able to replace any set of formulae with another set deducible from it, and here the actual form must be preserved in case it needs to be matched against a pattern. He calls this very messy.

The second is the inconsistency trap, and the description of the failure is the most vivid sentence in the note:

However, the attacked system may not realize what has happened before it has been convinced that the sun rises in the west. Inconsistent data, 1998

The containment is real but partial. Only that key is broken, and a trust system can withdraw its reasons for trusting it. The damage is bounded by the key, which is the whole achievement, and it is not bounded in time.

The way out he offers is to qualify what counts as inference: to limit the kinds of rule that may be used. He notes what that costs the implementer, and does not pretend to have finished the thought.

46.5 Time, and the tortoise again

The last section addresses a source of apparent contradiction that is not an attack at all, and opens with the dialogue characters of Chapter 29:

Tortoise: But Achilles, you just told me just a minute ago it was five minutes past ten. How can I ever believe you again? Inconsistent data, 1998

Two remedies are proposed. The first is expiry: everything carries a date after which conclusions drawn from it are cancelled, unless a fresher copy says the same thing. Berners-Lee illustrates what a fully qualified statement looks like, with a gauge, a place and a time, and contrasts it drily with what a fuzzy system and a classical system would each conclude from it.

The second is more interesting and applies far beyond this subject: make the looseness in the real system visible in the rule. Rather than saying any employee of a member organisation may register, say that any person who was at some time in the last two months an employee of an organisation which was at some time in the last two months a member may register.

The system then does not need to propagate changes instantly, because the slack it was relying on has been written down. Every distributed system runs on such slack. Almost none of them state it.

What happened since

  • Scoped trust became the norm. Trusting a key for statements of a particular kind, rather than trusting a party generally, is how certificate extensions, token scopes and capability systems all work.
  • Content addressing answered the checksum requirement. Endorsing a document by its hash rather than its location is now ordinary practice in package ecosystems and supply-chain attestation.
  • The dilemma of section 46.4 was never resolved. Practical systems take the first horn: they check statements of a given form, and accept that equivalent statements phrased differently will not match.
  • Explicit staleness windows became standard. Tokens with lifetimes and revocation lists with validity periods are the second remedy of section 46.5, arrived at by necessity rather than by this argument.
