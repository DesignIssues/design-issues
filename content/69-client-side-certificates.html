The short version

A person proves who they are to a website with a password, which is sent to the site and stored there. The browser could instead keep a private key and sign a random challenge from the site, so the secret never leaves the person’s machine. Berners-Lee asks browsers to make this easy, and to show which identity is in use.

The question

Ask people about client certificates, Berners-Lee reports, and they say they are going out of fashion, difficult to use and badly supported by browsers. Ask them about passwords and they “scream that they hate passwords”. The Web uses public-key cryptography to prove who the site is, but usually a password to prove who the user is. He grants anonymity its place first:

When you are browsing the news and interacting with incoming information, you may be happy not to be identified or even keen to be anonymous. There are times, though, when you want to be well identified yourself, whether moving money around at the bank or chatting with friends. Web Security - Client side certs, 2015

Why, in those cases, do we still use passwords?

69.1 A secret that never travels

When a site’s password store is stolen, every user’s password is open to attack; in June 2012 about 6.5 million LinkedIn password hashes were posted to a Russian password-cracking forum. A client certificate rests instead on a key pair: a private key that stays with its owner, and a public key that can check a signature but cannot make one.

The note’s case against passwords is one breathless sentence:

For passwords, you should think of a massively big one which is therefore impossible to remember so you end up relying on your computer to remember it, and it should of course be a different one for each of the things you do business with, and the other party has store a copy of this, and your code has to send it across the net risking it being ensnared within your system or their system, and then they have to store it, which can be pretty insecure, to judge from history. Web Security - Client side certs, 2015

Figure 69.2 sets a key pair against it. The obvious objection he turns round: “Yes, you can’t scribble your secret key on a Post-It™ note but that is actually an advantage.”

His summary:

Public key cryptography is a really wonderful gift from mathematics. We should be making better use of it. Web Security - Client side certs, 2015

69.2 Six things a browser would have to do

To picture the list: Alice holds a work key and a personal key; at her bank she picks the personal one, sees it named beside the address, and later logs out.

FunctionWhat it means for a person
Create a key pair and keep it locallyLaptop, phone or hardware device
Choose which identity to use for a site, and remember itWith enough shown to tell one from another
Show who the user is logged in as, near the addressBeside where the site’s own identity is shown
Log outDrop the credentials, optionally choose another identity
Manage those preferencesEditable when the user gets muddled
Back up and synchronise keys, selectivelyExtra credit; some keys never leave one machine

That row exists because the feature did not work: the note’s only reference is Firefox bug 634697, “ff doesn’t remember choice of certificate for https connection from previous session although ‘remember choice was selected’”. It was fixed in Firefox 81 (2020).

The third row reveals the asymmetry most sharply. A browser shows who the site is, prominently, and nothing about who the user is being.

On labelling, a detail that decides usability, the note is blunt:

Make sure the certificates are labelled in a way that makes them distinct -- not all just labelled with my name (duh!). Web Security - Client side certs, 2015

The note also asks that <keygen>, the HTML element that had the browser generate a key pair, fire an event or Promise so the page can continue its workflow. Two years later Chrome removed <keygen> altogether (see What happened since).

69.3 “That’s not how browsers work”

The note’s last section, “Just do it”, targets a habit rather than a technology:

Many of the things which I hear people wish for, mentioned in this article or not, are often put out into the conversation but with a sigh and the caveat that "It would be nice, but that's not how browsers work". Web Security - Client side certs, 2015

His answer: browsers are code, often open source, upgraded constantly for security fixes, so “a new idea can be tried out in browsers really fast”. The closing instruction is four words: “Let’s just change it.”

69.4 What the four notes share

Each of the four notes finds where the machine’s model of a social arrangement is narrower than the arrangement:

Three years later Berners-Lee turned to the opposite problem: a browser that distrusts the app its user chose (Chapter 70).

What happened since

  • The mechanism was replaced rather than fixed. WebAuthn, a W3C Recommendation of March 2019, gives the properties of the bottom lane of Figure 69.1.
  • The interface requirements were met. Passkeys (Apple, Google and Microsoft, May 2022) supply most of section 69.2’s list, through the operating system rather than the browser. Synced passkeys do leave the machine, which the note warns against for some keys.
  • The indicator the note asks for is still missing. Browsers show which profile or container is in use, but as of 2026 no major browser shows, beside the site’s identity, which credential that site was given.
  • The old mechanism was removed. <keygen> left Chrome in version 57 (March 2017) and Firefox in version 69 (2019), before its replacement was widely usable.

Connections
