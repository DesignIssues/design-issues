The short version

Subscribing to a feed was standardised long ago, so any reader app can follow any blog or podcast. Reacting (liking, commenting, rating) never was, so it lives inside whichever platform hosts the thing. Platforms give each person one audience, too coarse for people in many groups. The note’s advice: keep the open protocol, not any one company’s app.

The question

Written in 2021 and extended in 2024, this note surveys what had become of subscription on the Web, and asks why reacting to things is owned by whichever platform hosts them.

A feed is a file on a website listing its latest posts, in a standard format such as RSS. A feed reader checks it regularly and shows what is new. That is subscribing. The note’s definition is deliberately minimal:

Let's call a feed a sequence of published things such that you can subscribe to, with in some cases a mechanism to inform you when the is more added to it. So a feed by itself is a one-way thing with no feedback. Feeds, 2021

64.2 Reaction as a vocabulary problem

Activity Streams, a W3C vocabulary for recording what people do online, defines more than twenty kinds of activity, from accepting and adding to liking, reading and undoing. Berners-Lee extends the idea past social media:

In general, any collaborative system in which the shared state changes form time to time, like for example an issue tracker or action list, then those state-based systems cold well be deemed to create Activity Stream events whenever that state changes. Feeds, 2021

An issue tracker is a feed; Chapter 45 made the same move when it observed that a patch is news. The goal concerns Solid (Chapter 54), where each person keeps their data in a personal store that any app may use:

By agreeing on common interoperable representation and workflows for social actions, the Solid world can allow the same smooth user experience no matter what it it they are reacting to --liking, and so on. Feeds, 2021

64.3 The scope problem

With Instagram and Strava each person has one group of followers, so a choice may be not sharing, sharing with followers, or making something completely public. Feeds, 2021

For someone in many communities, Berners-Lee says, giving a reaction the right scope is very important (Chapter 74 treats this in general). A platform knows each person as one account with one follower list, so it can only offer choices about that one list.

64.4 The warning

A section titled Possible harmful consequences notes that reaction systems can cause harm: unhealthy preoccupation with public reactions, bullying comments, and the online gender-based violence described in a Web Foundation report. Its recommendation:

So any new systems we design should involve investigation and modeling of these things, and where it is possible, explicit design to avoid harm. Feeds, 2021

Take a like-count. Technically it is a number; socially it is read as a measure of a post’s worth. That is the two-protocol analysis of Chapter 15 (a technical protocol and an unwritten social one, which can fail separately), and Chapter 90 shows such failures at scale.

64.5 Subscriptions are data too

To use several reader apps with the same subscriptions, the apps must agree on the list itself. NetNewsWire exports it as OPML, a small XML file giving each feed’s title and address, which another reader can import. The note also points to FreshRSS, open-source software that keeps a person’s feeds on their own server.

64.6 Finding feeds

“Wherever you get your podcasts” assumes the big platforms list every show by name. That works for famous shows but does not scale if everyone is to have a feed. The alternative is links, in posts, email or meeting minutes; a link to one post leads to its feed. Whatever finds feeds decides what people come across, which matters amid much misinformation.

64.7 Protocols, not apps

Some podcasts ask listeners to install their own app. The note says no:

Use your generic podcast reader, like Overcast, or the default one on your device. Keep the RSS protocol alive! Feeds, 2021

One reader keeps track of everything a listener has heard. By 2024 the threat was the large players: Spotify and Apple Podcasts play anyone’s feed but also carry shows only their apps can play. The note’s policy is not to listen to those.

64.8 One story, two formats

Berners-Lee read Neal Stephenson’s Termination Shock in audio and text at once: text when someone is around, sound when driving. That, he says, is about efficiency and accessibility.

In December 2024 he found that the app Speechify does this, though it keeps his place in its own cloud rather than in his Solid pod.

What happened since

  • Federated social media grew. Mastodon and other networks on ActivityPub (W3C Recommendation, January 2018) grew sharply after Twitter’s change of ownership in late 2022.
  • Audiences stayed coarse. Mastodon offers four post visibilities (public, unlisted, followers only, direct), still short of the many groups of section 64.3.
  • Podcasts began to close. The note’s 2024 section records Spotify and Apple Podcasts adding shows only their apps can play.
  • Feeds were reconsidered. On 25 August 2024 Mark Nottingham, co-author of Atom, published “What RSS Needs”, which the note links.
