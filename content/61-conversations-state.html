The question

Written to accompany a talk to the Advisory Committee in November 2000, this note observes that two quite separate developments were pointing away from the Web's founding model at once, and asks what that model actually was.

Berners-Lee describes the occasion and the method:

Often, we notice that Web technology is now entering a field new to the Web but old of itself. In these cases, we can view the process we need to go through either has extending web technology into this field, or of Webizing the field. Conversations and state, 2000

The field this time is the formal specification of protocols.

61.1 What a screen is for

The argument begins with an unexpected question: what is special about a screen? The answer is not resolution but simultaneity.

A screen with a window system presents a large amount of informatoin at the same time to a person. In practice, more or less everything which a person is concentrting on at one time can be presented in its current state. Conversations and state, 2000

Berners-Lee identifies a threshold, roughly the resolution that became standard in the late 1980s, past which direct manipulation became possible: folders one could open, dragging and dropping. And he names the property that made those metaphors work:

The essential things about this is that the computer is at every instant presenting the current state, whether it or the human is manipulating it. The communication betwen personand machine is in terms of the mutual manipulation of a shared state. Conversations and state, 2000

The Web, he says, was intended to extend that form of communication to remote interaction between people. Much of its effectiveness derived from that model, because a shared space of information is something one can navigate, using all the faculties human memory already has for navigation.

61.2 The other model, and its return

His warning about small devices is the sharpest practical point in the note: one can scale a page down and adapt it to a small screen, and doing so might be choosing simply the wrong interface metaphor.

61.3 Why this needs formalising

Three reasons are given, and they run from interfaces to commerce to plumbing.

ReasonWhat it would let us do
InterfacesFormalise the human-machine interfaces that are conversational, on every device without a large screen
TransactionsFormalise commercial dealings, for which a trail of messages is the most accurate model anyway
ProtocolsAnalyse the infrastructure protocols by which the information world is actually implemented

The gap he names is precise, and it is the same shape as the gaps named throughout Volume IV:

The standardization of XML protocols has, with XML (and RDF), a richness in terms of marshalling data formats to build on, and, with xml-schemas xforms and rdfs, a richness to draw on in terms of languages for defining valid documents, but has no basis yet for defining with equivalent power the validity (and semantics) of a sequence of interrlated messages which are a protocol. Conversations and state, 2000

We can say what a valid message looks like. We cannot say what a valid conversation looks like.

61.4 The admission

The note ends by conceding that the distinction it has drawn was never clean in practice:

The moment it was created as a basically stateles system, many web site designers took it as their challenge to get around this model in order to create a conversational interface -- and many still do Conversations and state, 2000

And it connects that to something people usually treat as a separate subject:

Our concerns about privacy stem largel;y from the knowledge that our "reading" of documents is in fact done by a series of protocols which leave a trace. Conversations and state, 2000

That sentence is the bridge to Volume VII. Reading a page feels like looking at something. It is actually a series of exchanges, and the exchanges are recorded. Privacy anxiety is the gap between the model the interface presents and the protocol underneath it.

The last observation is the one a working developer will recognise. A form, on many sites, has stopped being a document and become the definition of a protocol: a set of valid sequences of actions.

What happened since

  • The conversational interface arrived in force. Voice assistants from 2011 onward, and conversational interfaces to almost everything after 2022, made the model this note describes the ordinary way many people use computers.
  • Protocol formalisation did not arrive with it. There is still no widely used way to specify which sequences of messages are valid, which is the gap section 61.3 names.
  • The privacy diagnosis was vindicated. That reading leaves a trace became the central fact of Web privacy, and the subject of Volume VII.
  • Scaling the page down happened anyway. Responsive design adapted the shared-state metaphor to small screens rather than replacing it, which is the choice this note warns may be the wrong one.
