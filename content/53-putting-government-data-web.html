The question

Written in June 2009 after conversations with people in the British and American governments who wanted to publish data and did not know what to do next. Its status line is characteristic: good enough for folk.

It is the most operational note in the collection, and it opens by ranking the steps in the order a busy official should take them.

Start with the "low-hanging fruit". Whatever else, the raw data should be made available as soon as possible. Putting Government Data online, 2009

53.1 The ranking, and what it demotes

53.2 Three reasons, one technique

Government data goes online, Berners-Lee says, for three reasons: to increase citizen awareness and therefore accountability, to contribute valuable information about the world, and to let the government, the country and the world function more efficiently. His claim is that one technique serves all three, and he characterises it in three words.

PropertyWhat it means for a department
OpenReachable by an unlimited variety of applications, because the format is not proprietary
ModularCombinable with anything else published the same way, with no advance planning by either party
ScalableEasy to add to, even when the terms and definitions change over time

The worked example of modularity is the one an official will recognise: health expenditure for an area combined with population characteristics for the same area, in order to assess whether a programme worked. Neither dataset was prepared with the other in mind.

The instruction that follows is deliberately agnostic about everything except the middle:

The essential message is that whatever data format people want the data in, and whatever format they give it to you in, you use the RDF model as the interconnection bus. Putting Government Data online, 2009

Take anything, give anything, and let the common model sit in the middle. That is the least disruptive possible advice, and it is why the note works as guidance rather than as advocacy.

53.3 Two philosophies, and the one that finishes

The central passage is about organisational method rather than technology, and it is the most quoted part of the note inside government:

The top-down one is to make a corporate or national plan, by getting committees together of all the interested parties, and make a consistent set of terms (ontology) into which everything fits. This in fact takes so long it is often never finished, and anyway does not in fact get corporate or national consensus in the end. Putting Government Data online, 2009

The alternative is stated in a single balanced sentence that concedes what the top-down approach is genuinely for:

A top-level mandate is extremely valuable, but grass-roots action is essential. Putting Government Data online, 2009

The mandate supplies permission, which is the first star of Chapter 51 and the hardest thing to obtain. It does not supply the vocabulary, and waiting for it to do so is how programmes die.

53.4 Why this is not merely practical advice

The note is the clearest case in the collection of an architectural property paying off in a place its designers did not have in mind. Chapter 17 argued that one document must be able to carry several vocabularies so that an invoice could name a part nobody else understands. Here the same property lets a department publish using international terms where they exist, national terms where they do not, and local terms for the rest.

The Linked Data technology, unlike any other technology, allows any data communication to be composed of many mixed vocabularies. Each vocabulary is from a community, be it international, national, state or local; or specific to an industry sector. This optimizes the usual trade-off between the expense and difficulty of getting wide agreement, and the practicality of working in a smaller community. Putting Government Data online, 2009

Effort towards interoperability can then be spent where it is most needed. Nobody has to agree about everything before anybody can publish anything.

What happened since

  • The programmes launched, in the year the note names. Britain’s data portal opened in January 2010 and the American one in May 2009, and open data mandates spread internationally over the following decade.
  • The priority order was followed and then reversed. Raw data came first, as advised. Portals then invested heavily in interfaces, which the note ranks last, while the third priority of linking to other sources was rarely reached.
  • Bottom-up won by default. National data vocabularies were attempted and mostly did not complete, which is the outcome section 53.3 predicts.
  • Sustaining publication proved harder than starting it. Datasets published under a mandate frequently stopped being updated when attention moved, which is rule three of Chapter 49 failing slowly.

Connections
