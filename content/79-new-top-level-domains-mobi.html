The short version

A web address such as www.example.com is read from the right. The last part, .com, is the top-level domain; example is the name rented beneath it. ICANN, a non-profit body, runs the top of the tree; companies called registries run each top-level domain and charge fees.

In 2004 ICANN was considering .mobi (mobile content) and .xxx (adult content). Berners-Lee argues that every new top-level domain costs name-holders money, and that .mobi breaks links, because the same page would need a different address on a phone.

The question

Should a fact about the reader’s device, such as being a phone, be written into a site’s name? The note was written in April 2004 and, unusually for this collection, formally supported: at its meeting of 14 May 2004 the W3C Technical Architecture Group resolved to support the note, with one member abstaining and one recusing himself. Its objection to the mobile domain is architectural rather than commercial.

79.1 How the namespace stopped being hierarchical

The note begins with a history that explains the whole problem. The tree structure improved on a flat list of machine names. It let a university, say, create physics.uni.example itself, without asking a central registry. Names nearer the root were rarely handed out, so the central workload stayed small.

Then names moved out of protocols and into speech:

It then became valuable to own a short domain name. This turned domain name space into a limited commodity. New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful, 2004

He names the anomaly behind much of the instability:

An anomaly was that unclaimed names were deemed to have no owner and no value, and were allocated in a "first come first served" frenzy in which speculators made great profits and held to ransom those who may have been considered the more logical owner of a name. New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful, 2004

The cost of each new top-level domain

The first effect is a little like printing more money. New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful, 2004

The value of every existing registration drops, while the cost of protecting a brand rises from three domains to four, five and more. For a large company this is trivial; for a small business, a charity or a family it is not. The second effect is instability: a rush to reserve names that nobody can afford to miss, and a rash of attempts to take valuable ones.

79.2 The specific objection

Those costs apply to any new top-level domain. The objection to .mobi is different in kind: it is architectural.

The Web works by reference: an address is bookmarked, emailed and passed on, then looked up later somewhere else. The note’s example is a restaurant looked up and bookmarked on a laptop, and the bookmark used later, when only a phone is to hand, to check the evening menu. Or a travel itinerary read in the office on a large screen for the map, and at the airport on a phone for the gate number.

Dividing the Web into information destined for different devices, or different classes of user, or different classes of information, breaks the Web in a fundamental way. New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful, 2004

Chapter 10 established that one resource may be served in many forms, and Chapter 2 that the identifier names the thing rather than the rendering. Chapter 67 makes the same complaint about the s in https: a property of the delivery put into the name of the thing.

Both kinds of behaviour have been seen in practice: one shortly before the note was written, one after. This is the single point of control of Chapter 5, at the one place the Web cannot route around.

79.4 Why a chapter for a proposal that failed

The value of the note is the general form of the argument. Adding a distinction to the top of a namespace does three things: it multiplies defensive work for everyone already in it, it hands value to whoever operates the new division, and it invites people to encode in the name a property that does not belong there.

Suppose a software package registry added a top-level prefix for mobile builds: every author would claim each name twice, the prefix’s operator would collect, and a property of the build would sit in the package’s name. Chapter 4 makes the argument about schemes; this chapter makes it about names.

What happened since

  • The rumoured behaviours happened. In September 2003, months before the note, VeriSign, the .com and .net registry, redirected unregistered names to its own search page (Site Finder), withdrawing it in October 2003 after objections. In 2008 the registrar Network Solutions began briefly reserving names that customers had searched for on its site.
  • The mobile domain launched and faded. ICANN approved it in 2005; general registration opened in 2006. Separate mobile addresses gave way to responsive design (a term coined in 2010), one address for every device, as section 79.2 argues.
  • The namespace expanded enormously. The programme begun in 2012 added over a thousand new top-level domains, multiplying the burden of Figure 79.1.
  • The adult-content domain launched in 2011. Before it opened, trademark holders filed tens of thousands of paid “Sunrise B” applications simply to block their names from it.
