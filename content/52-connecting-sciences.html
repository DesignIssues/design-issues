The question

Written in January 2004, this is the shortest argument in Volume VI and the one with the clearest motive. Scientific problems increasingly require several fields at once, scientific information is naturally data rather than prose, and the two facts together make science the obvious first customer.

52.1 Fields have fuzzy edges

No scientific discipline is as island. The fields of study to which we give names have fuzzy edges, and overlap one another. They are in fact connected in a loose web which evolves with time, as new fields arise, and we change our perceptions of existing ones. Connecting the Sciences with the Semantic Web, 2004

The chain Berners-Lee offers runs from physics through physical chemistry, organic chemistry, cell biology, proteomics, genetics, epidemiology, medicine and pharmacology. One may be expert in one without being expert in all, and must know something of the neighbours.

52.2 The argument from serendipity

The second half makes a case for openness that does not depend on altruism, and it starts with a precedent the reader has lived through:

During the early years of the WWW, an element of reluctance was a hesitation by companies to allow information such as their catalogs or parts lists to be available to the general public. This hesitation evaporated when it became clear that only those companies about whose products information was freely available on the web were likely to be involved in any commerce at all. Connecting the Sciences with the Semantic Web, 2004

The reluctance ended because withholding stopped being viable, not because anyone was persuaded. Berners-Lee is implicitly predicting the same for scientific data, and notes in passing that funders had begun complaining about the disappearance of the original data behind published papers.

The argument proper is about what open information turned out to be for:

We discovered with the web of human-readable information that much of the benefit was serendipitous: information was used to advantage in ways that its publisher could never have imagined, and the enquirer who started off surfing for a particular solution often finds quite different solutions to that envisaged, not to mention solutions to quite different, but equally pressing problems. Connecting the Sciences with the Semantic Web, 2004

He then makes the move that gives the note its force, listing discoveries made by accident, from Archimedes through penicillin, and drawing the conclusion about machines:

If we are to make new discoveries using information on a huge scale, we will need to emulate the openness of the minds of these researchers by making scientific data available in a Semantic Web so that crazy hypotheses can be tested in a few moments harnessing data from many diverse fields. Connecting the Sciences with the Semantic Web, 2004

Serendipity is treated as a thing infrastructure can supply. If testing a strange idea is cheap enough, strange ideas get tested, and the accidents that used to require a bath or a contaminated culture dish can be arranged deliberately.

52.3 The closing widening

The note ends by refusing to let science be a special case. It observes that science itself is not an island either, since an epidemiological survey often yields results when joined with geographical and economic data, and the search for a disease outbreak leads outwards from the laboratory.

That is Chapter 51’s argument, arriving from the other end. A message carries terms at several scales because the questions people ask do not respect the boundaries of the datasets they are asked of.

What happened since

  • Science became the field where this worked. Life sciences adopted shared identifiers and cross-referenced datasets more thoroughly than any other domain, and the identifier-plus-mapping pattern of Figure 52.1 is how biomedical data is now integrated.
  • The data-behind-the-paper complaint became policy. Data availability statements and repository deposit requirements became standard conditions of publication and funding through the 2010s.
  • The serendipity claim was tested at scale. Large-scale cross-dataset inference in biology, from drug repurposing to variant interpretation, is the thing this note describes, and it works where the identifiers line up and not otherwise.
  • Agreement in advance is still attempted. Top-down standardisation efforts continue to be commissioned and to run long, which is the cost Figure 52.1 is designed to avoid.
