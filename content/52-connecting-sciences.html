The short version

Each science records its data in its own way, so a question that runs from chemistry through biology to medicine has to be translated at every border. Agreeing one common format for everyone would take years.

This note argues for a third way: leave each field’s data as it is, and publish small machine-readable links where fields overlap, so a program can follow a question across them. It also argues for open data, because much of the Web’s value came from uses nobody planned. If testing an odd idea against many sources takes moments, more odd ideas get tested, and more lucky discoveries follow.

The question

Written in January 2004, this note has a plain motive. Scientific problems increasingly need several fields at once, and scientific information is naturally data (tables, rules, measurements) rather than prose. That makes science the obvious first customer for the Semantic Web, the web of data that machines can read and combine.

52.1 Fields have fuzzy edges

No scientific discipline is as island. The fields of study to which we give names have fuzzy edges, and overlap one another. They are in fact connected in a loose web which evolves with time, as new fields arise, and we change our perceptions of existing ones. Connecting the Sciences with the Semantic Web, 2004

The chain Berners-Lee offers runs from physics through physical chemistry, organic chemistry, cell biology, proteomics (the study of all the proteins in a cell or organism), genetics, epidemiology (the study of how diseases spread through populations), medicine and pharmacology. One may be expert in one without being expert in all, and must know something of the neighbours.

The cost of crossing a boundary

The search for cures for AIDS or cancer, or for a new virus such as SARS, traces pathways through many of these fields at once. But each field models its data its own way, deciding what to record and how the pieces relate. So every idea that crosses fields means negotiating the conversion and transfer of data afresh, while getting groups to agree common formats and vocabularies first takes great time and effort.

The note’s answer is route (c), which Berners-Lee calls retrospective and incremental (Figure 52.2). Retrospective: each field’s models and data already exist and stay as they are. Incremental: links are built one interface at a time, and no data model is redesigned at once. The overlap is only partial, but linking it where it exists is still worth doing. The links are metadata, data about data. A hypothetical example: two databases record the same protein under different names. Neither changes; someone publishes one machine-readable statement that the names are equivalent, and a program can now follow a question from one into the other. That statement is a mapping.

52.2 The argument from serendipity

The catalogue precedent

The second half makes a case for openness that does not depend on altruism, and it starts with a precedent from the web’s own early years:

During the early years of the WWW, an element of reluctance was a hesitation by companies to allow information such as their catalogs or parts lists to be available to the general public. This hesitation evaporated when it became clear that only those companies about whose products information was freely available on the web were likely to be involved in any commerce at all. Connecting the Sciences with the Semantic Web, 2004

The reluctance ended because withholding stopped being viable. Berners-Lee implies the same for scientific data, noting that funders already complained of the original data behind papers disappearing.

Accidents on purpose

The argument proper is about what open information turned out to be for:

We discovered with the web of human-readable information that much of the benefit was serendipitous: information was used to advantage in ways that its publisher could never have imagined, and the enquirer who started off surfing for a particular solution often finds quite different solutions to that envisaged, not to mention solutions to quite different, but equally pressing problems. Connecting the Sciences with the Semantic Web, 2004

He then lists discoveries made by accident, from the bath of Archimedes through penicillin to the effect of Viagra, and draws the conclusion about machines:

If we are to make new discoveries using information on a huge scale, we will need to emulate the openness of the minds of these researchers by making scientific data available in a Semantic Web so that crazy hypotheses can be tested in a few moments harnessing data from many diverse fields. Connecting the Sciences with the Semantic Web, 2004

Serendipity is treated as a thing infrastructure can supply. If testing a strange idea is cheap enough, strange ideas get tested, and lucky accidents become more likely.

52.3 The closing widening

The note refuses to let science be a special case: science itself is not an island either (Figure 52.3).

That is Chapter 51’s argument from the other end: one piece of data mixes vocabularies from communities large and small, because questions do not respect the boundaries of datasets.

52.4 First steps

The basic standards existed: OWL, the Web Ontology Language, for writing vocabularies a machine can process, and RDF for publishing data that uses them. The note suggests how to start:

  • Basic ontologies for weights and measures, the periodic table, physical constants and simple molecules, useful to schools and to hazard, food and drug information.
  • Demonstrator datasets of general value, such as geophysical, meteorological or genome data.
  • Anyone can begin: researchers, funders, journals or academies. Publication could reasonably be a condition of funding.

He also flags what openness raises: the lab notebook, whose incidental details sometimes matter later, and privacy, clinical trial data and the copyright of compilations.

What happened since

  • The life sciences went furthest. Projects such as Bio2RDF, first published in 2008, republished many public biological databases as linked data, linked across databases.
  • The missing-data complaint became policy. PLOS required authors to share underlying data from 2014; the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) appeared in 2016; the US National Institutes of Health’s data-sharing policy took effect in January 2023.
