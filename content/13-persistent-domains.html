The short version

A web address has two parts: the domain name (example.com) and the path after it (/downloads). The publisher can keep the path stable. Nobody can keep the domain stable, because domain names are rented: they can lapse, be sold, or be lost in a lawsuit, and then the same address leads somewhere else.

Berners-Lee proposed new domains stamped with a year, such as .y2000, bought once and held forever. Each would come with a duty to keep its pages available, and that duty would pass to whoever bought the domain. If the owner disappeared, copies kept by other organisations would take over. It was never built.

The question

Chapter 3 concluded that persistence is a contract rather than a technology. This note, written in October 2000, is the one place in the collection where Berners-Lee tries to write the contract. He explains the threshold a thought has to cross to be written down here:

Things get written under Design Issues when I have expressed them several times in various contexts. Persistent Domains, 2000

He labels it a strawman: a rough proposal put up so that others can knock it down. Its first half diagnoses a problem still unsolved; its second shows what solving it would demand.

13.1 The problem, factored

The note begins by splitting URI persistence into two independent questions: the persistence of the opaque string after the domain name, and the persistence of the domain name itself. Figure 13.1 uses the note’s own example.

The first is the publisher’s to solve. Berners-Lee notes that the failure is usually thoughtlessness rather than malice, aggravated by a dearth of tools for running a server with persistent URIs. He had already written Cool URIs Don’t Change about it, and that covers only half the problem.

The second half is summarised in a line from Esther Dyson (the note spells her name Ester), then chair of ICANN, the body that oversees the domain name system. A meme is a phrase that spreads and sticks:

The second issue is summarized by ICANN chair Ester Dyson's line, You don't buy domain names, you rent them. This single meme instantly undermines any public expectation that domain names should be persistent. Persistent Domains, 2000

In principle, he goes on, whoever next holds example.com, by purchase, lawsuit or an owner’s accidental failure to renew, could make /downloads point to anything at all.

Why it might already be fine

He then argues against his own alarm. The investment in domain names as references is huge, the lookup technology sophisticated, and the legal weight behind a significant organisation’s ownership considerable. Nobody imagines a legal battle over microsoft.com or ietf.org being lost to a sneaky entrepreneur. The section ends with four words: but is this enough?

He also names a symptom: a constant stream of proposals for new URI schemes (URNs and Handles are examples), each with its own lookup system, usually made with less attention to the social issues than HTTP and DNS receive, and otherwise similar except in persistence.

13.2 The proposal: date-stamped top-level domains

The mechanism is a single trick, borrowed twice over. A top-level domain is the last part of a name, such as .com or .org. Create a series of top-level domains named for years. A name under .y2000 could be registered only during the year 2000, and once acquired would be held forever.

The trick is the same one as used in W3C's datespace URIs (such as http://www.w3.org/2000/01/sw) and British car registration plates: to date code them. Persistent Domains, 2000

The date code removes competition for a name over time. No one can apply for a name in a year that has passed, so the incentive to take it from its holder disappears, and the expectation that it will never be reused is set from the start.

Each of the sanity rules he attaches anticipates an abuse:

  • some trademark checking before granting an alphanumeric name, which stops people squatting on brand names;
  • a cap on how many persistent domains one organisation may hold, or at least a queue that favours those holding fewer, which stops hoarding;
  • immediate, automatic allocation for anyone content with a random numeric name such as 6872364.y2000, not transferable, so it is cheap to get and worthless to trade;
  • some constraint on sitting on a domain without using it, and possibly on transfer, which discourages speculation.

13.3 The obligations

The second half makes the proposal a contract rather than a naming scheme, and it is the hard part.

An organisation entering the scheme must join a mirroring arrangement, in which others keep live copies of its site under a contract that, if the publisher comes to a voluntary or involuntary end, the mirrors continue to serve. The documents then enter what Berners-Lee calls an archive state, in which the original publisher loses the authority to change the domain and the public gain rights of access.

For material so uninteresting that no one will pay to keep it, serious archival institutions, such as the major national libraries, should have the right to take over an archive for the public good. He also suggests mirroring confidential, access-controlled documents, with a 30-year timeout on the confidentiality.

The obligations run with the property:

The persistent domain would be property, with (like land) rights and obligations. Transfer of the domain name would carry within it both. Persistent Domains, 2000

The worked case is one bank acquiring another and being unable simply to drop support for the acquired bank’s documents without all of them falling into archive state. Archive state is also the penalty for cheating. A domain whose publisher “consistently flaunts the rules” would be declared dead and pass into archive state as though the organisation had ceased to exist; it could then ask for a new domain and start again, at least the first time.

Berners-Lee also floats, without committing to it, a limited redistribution licence for material in archive state, by analogy with an implicit right to photocopy an out-of-print book. A footnote records Joseph Reagle’s point that courts might not accept the parallel.

13.4 What the note gets right about what it is

The clarification about re-use is the part to carry away. Most URIs are generic in the sense of Chapter 10: they refer to a conceptual resource whose representation varies with time, technology and language, as one address for “today’s weather” shows different content each day. That is permitted inside a persistent domain. What is forbidden is reallocating a URI to something else. Ideally the generic resource and each specific version of it (one format, one exact sequence of bytes) would have separate URIs, and providing them could be one of the owner’s obligations.

He admits he does not know how to enforce this, and guesses:

Actually, I don't think it will be a long term problem, as the tools will be made such that it is not a function. (“Rename: command not known”!). Persistent Domains, 2000

The note ends with a summary list of rules. Two say who would hold the power: the registry would be a neutral non-profit cooperative, and ICANN’s delegation of the year domains would be irrevocable for all time. The list closes by asking one dollar from anyone who uses the scheme (“just kidding!”), and a footnote jokes about how this era will look to historians after the year 2100.

What happened since

  • Nothing was built. The expansion of the top-level namespace that did occur, beginning with ICANN’s new generic top-level domain programme in 2012, sold names as rentals on the existing model and added no persistence obligations.
  • The contract was written elsewhere, privately. The Handle System and the Digital Object Identifier (DOI, the identifiers printed on academic papers, such as doi.org/10.…) supply the undertaking this note describes, funded by registration fees rather than by a change to DNS. The obligation attaches to a registrant through a registration agency, not to a domain name.
  • Archive state arrived without rights. Web archives, such as the Internet Archive’s Wayback Machine (public since 2001), preserve material after publishers disappear, but as a voluntary third-party act, not a condition of holding a name, and with no contractual succession.
  • Dyson’s line held. Domain names are still rented. Lapsed domains are still re-registered and repointed: the worse failure the note names, a URI resolving to something other than what the linker meant.
