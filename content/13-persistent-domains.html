The question

Chapter 3 concluded that persistence is a contract rather than a technology. This note, written in October 2000, is the one place in the collection where Berners-Lee tries to write the contract. He explains the threshold a thought has to cross to be written down here:

Things get written under Design Issues when I have expressed them several times in various contexts. Persistent Domains, 2000

It is labelled a strawman, and nothing in it was built. It is included at full chapter length because the analysis in its first half is correct and still unaddressed, and because the proposal in its second half shows what taking persistence seriously would actually require.

13.1 The problem, factored

The note begins by splitting URI persistence into two independent questions: the persistence of the opaque string after the domain name, and the persistence of the domain name itself.

The first is the publisher’s to solve, and Berners-Lee notes that the failure is usually thoughtlessness rather than malice, aggravated by a dearth of tools that help run a server with persistent URIs. He had already written Cool URIs Don’t Change about it and had led the Consortium to a persistence policy. He is clear that this addresses only half the problem.

The second half is where the note earns its place, and it is summarised by quoting someone else:

The second issue is summarized by ICANN chair Ester Dyson's line, You don't buy domain names, you rent them. This single meme instantly undermines any public expectation that domain names should be persistent. Persistent Domains, 2000

He then makes the case against his own alarm, which is the mark of the better notes in this collection. The planet’s investment in domain names as references is huge, the dereferencing technology has become sophisticated, and the legal weight behind a significant organisation’s ownership is considerable. Nobody imagines a legal battle over a well-known domain being lost to a sneaky entrepreneur. The section ends with three words: but is this enough?

He also names a second-order symptom that Volume II has been circling since Chapter 3: the constant stream of proposals for new URI schemes with incompatible lookup technology, usually made with less attention to the social issues than HTTP and DNS receive, and proposing to be otherwise similar except in persistence.

13.2 The proposal: date-stamped top-level domains

The mechanism is a single trick, borrowed twice over. Create a series of top-level domains named for years. A name under .y2000 could be registered only during the year 2000, and once acquired would be held forever.

The trick is the same one as used in W3C's datespace URIs (such as http://www.w3.org/2000/01/sw) and British car registration plates: to date code them. Persistent Domains, 2000

The date code does the work because it removes contention over time. A name in a year that has passed cannot be applied for by anyone, so the incentive to take a name from its holder disappears, and the expectation that the name will never be reused can be set from the beginning.

The sanity rules Berners-Lee attaches are as interesting as the mechanism, because each one anticipates an abuse: trademark diligence before granting an alphanumeric name; a cap on how many persistent domains one organisation may hold, or at least a queue that favours those holding fewer; immediate, automatic, non-transferable allocation for anyone content with a random numeric name; constraints on sitting on an unused domain and on transfer.

13.3 The obligations

The second half of the proposal is the part that makes it a contract rather than a naming scheme, and it is the part that is genuinely hard.

An organisation entering the scheme must join a mirroring arrangement, so that other parties hold copies. There must be a contract under which, if the original publisher comes to a voluntary or involuntary end, the mirrors continue to serve. The documents then enter what Berners-Lee calls an archive state, in which the original publisher loses the authority to change the domain and the public gain rights of access.

The obligations run with the property:

The persistent domain would be property, with (like land) rights and obligations. Transfer of the domain name would carry within it both. Persistent Domains, 2000

The worked case is one bank acquiring another and being unable simply to drop support for the acquired bank’s documents without all of them falling into archive state. That is the whole idea in one example: the acquirer inherits the duty along with the asset.

Berners-Lee also floats, and explicitly declines to commit to, a limited redistribution licence attaching to material in archive state. He offers the analogy of an implicit right to photocopy a book that is out of print, and a footnote records Joseph Reagle pointing out that courts would not necessarily accept the parallel between Web and paper distribution.

13.4 What the note gets right about what it is

The clarification about re-use is the part practitioners should carry away. Most URIs are generic in the sense of Chapter 10: they refer to a conceptual resource whose representation varies with time, technology and language. That is permitted inside a persistent domain. What is forbidden is reallocating a URI to something else.

Berners-Lee is honest that he does not know how to enforce this, and the guess he offers is characteristic:

Actually, I don't think it will be a long term problem, as the tools will be made such that it is not a function. (“Rename: command not known”!). Persistent Domains, 2000

The note closes with a joke at its own expense, a note addressed to readers after the year 2100 with invented citations, and a summary list of rules ending with a request for one dollar from anyone who uses the scheme.

What happened since

  • Nothing was built. No date-stamped top-level domain exists. The expansion of the top-level namespace that did occur, beginning with ICANN’s new generic top-level domain programme in 2012, sold names as rentals on the existing model and added no persistence obligations.
  • The contract was written elsewhere, privately. The Handle System and the Digital Object Identifier supply the undertaking this note describes, funded by registration fees rather than by a change to DNS. The obligation attaches to a registrant, not to a domain, and does not transfer automatically on acquisition.
  • Archive state arrived without rights. Web archives preserve material after publishers disappear, at scale, but as a voluntary third-party act rather than a condition of holding a name. There is no contractual succession of the kind section 13.3 describes.
  • Dyson’s line held. Domain names are still rented. Lapsed domains are still re-registered and repointed, which is the second and worse failure the note names in its opening: not an error message, but a URI resolving to something entirely different from what the person linking to it meant.

Questions

13.1 The date code removes contention by making a name unobtainable once its year has passed. Explain why that also removes most of the commercial pressure that makes domain names change hands.

13.2 Section 13.3 requires obligations to transfer with the domain. Name the party who would have to enforce this and the mechanism by which they could.

13.3 A persistent domain holder wishes to serve a document in a new format ten years on. Under the clarification in section 13.4, is this permitted? What would not be?

13.4 The Digital Object Identifier system delivers persistence by contract without touching DNS. Say what it achieves that this proposal would have, and what it does not.
