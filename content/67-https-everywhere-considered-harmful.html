The short version

Web addresses begin http: or https:; the s means the connection is encrypted. Berners-Lee argues that encryption should happen on every connection, invisibly, as faster modems did. Changing the s changes the address, and old links to the other address break.

He is not arguing against encryption. He is arguing that the fact “this connection is secure” does not belong in the name of a page.

The question

http://x.org/report and https://x.org/report look like one page. To the Web they are two names, and every link ever written points at only one of them. In 2015, while the Web was being urged to move wholesale to the second kind, the designer of the first called that campaign, taken at face value, “arguably a greater threat to the integrity for the web than anything else in its history”.

67.1 Who was the s for?

Every address begins with a prefix, or scheme, naming the protocol (the rules browser and server follow to talk) used to fetch it. When a secure HTTP was designed in the mid-1990s, there was a choice: extend the protocol behind the existing http: prefix, or give it a prefix of its own, https:.

A link passes between two people: whoever writes it and whoever later clicks it. The choice was about which of them the Web should serve.

When you look at that design choice, you have to remember that the URL is being used to communicate between two people, for example, the person who writes the link containing the href with the link, and the person who later sees the link and clicks ion it. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

67.2 The reader took over

Three things changed, and the last is the plainest.

  1. Users came under attack individually, so protecting the person who follows a link became the browser’s job rather than the publisher’s.
  2. People learned to look not at the s in the address but at the padlock, and then at the name of the certificate holder, the organisation the browser has checked the site belongs to.
  3. Some browsers stopped showing the prefix at all.
The 'http:' has in fact (2015) relatively recently been removed from the browser bar altogether for some browsers. While this infuriates those of us who are actually interested in it, it certainly puts another nail in the coffin of the idea that the 's' in the URI is important part of the architecture. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

An unshown letter cannot do the job it was added for.

67.3 Two names for one page

The problem is of course that moving things from http: space into https space, whether or not you keep the rest of the URI the same, breaks any links to. Put simply, the HTTPS Everywhere campaign taken at face value completely breaks the web. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

A link written years ago keeps pointing at the old name.

No earlier change to the Web did this:

The underlying speeds of connection of increased from 300bps to 300Gbps, IPv4 has being moved to IpV6, but none of this breaks the web of links in so doing. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

The security level was put where the identity lives. Chapter 2’s opacity axiom says an address is a sealed label: it names a thing, and nothing else should be read from its letters. The s breaks that rule. (A footnote spares the EFF’s browser extension of the same name.)

67.4 A wall that never fits

Once https: was treated as a safe space, browsers ring-fenced it: in the spirit of the Same Origin Policy, everything loaded into an https: page must arrive securely too, which, the note says, “has led to a class of web apps being broken”. A parenthesis that follows draws the general lesson:

It has been more any more clear that we and our systems should not just trust things or not trust them, or even to trust them on a scale form 0 to 1. We trust different people for different things. We trust one person for recommendations on food, and another for movies, and to muddle these trusts could be disastrous. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

A wall between a secure region and an insecure one implements trust as a single value, and the note gives the symptom of that mistake in any system:

A symptom if that you can never find the perfect place to put that boundary. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

If a boundary keeps needing to be moved, the thing it separates was not two kinds of thing.

67.5 Encrypt the wire, not the name

The alternative is in the note’s own title. TLS (Transport Layer Security) is the encryption layer that https: uses. Use it everywhere, inside the protocol, and stop marking it in the address. A connection can be upgraded to TLS as it is made, an upgrade already described in 2000 in RFC 2817 (one of the Internet’s numbered standards documents), though that was “motivated apparently by the need to save low-numbered ports”; or the browser can simply try the encrypted version first. Show the assurance in the browser, where people already look, and let the prefix be phased out.

Users should then be taught to understand the level of assurance being given, which has three parts:

  1. authentication of the other party (is this really the bank?);
  2. encryption of the communication;
  3. how the user appears to the other party, which ranges from anonymous, through traceable, to strongly authenticated.

One letter can say only yes or no. The last of these questions alone has three answers, which is the single-valued trust mistake again.

What happened since

  • Free certificates, late 2015. Let’s Encrypt, named in the note, began issuing them.
  • The campaign won. Chrome 68 began marking plain http: pages “Not secure” in July 2018, and most of the Web migrated.
  • The breakage was absorbed, not avoided. Redirects, HSTS (RFC 6797, 2012, telling browsers to rewrite a site’s http: links) and upgrade-insecure-requests repaired most links: a permanent layer of rewriting.
  • The in-band upgrade lost; trying TLS first arrived. RFC 2817 was not adopted, but Firefox 83 added an HTTPS-Only Mode (2020) and Chrome 90 tried https: first for typed addresses (2021), after the names had changed.
  • The certificate holder’s name went too, removed from the address bar in 2019 (Chapter 6). In Chrome 117 (2023) Google replaced the padlock itself, saying many users read it as a sign that a site was trustworthy: the note’s “level of assurance” problem.
  • The extension was retired. In 2021 the EFF announced it would retire HTTPS Everywhere: browsers now had HTTPS-only modes.
