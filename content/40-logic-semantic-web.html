The question

Volume V opens with the note in which Berners-Lee reads the logic literature and reports back. He is candid about his standing:

A problem here is a that I am no logician, and so I am am having to step like a fascinated reporter into this world of which I do not possess intimate experience. The Semantic Web as a language of logic, 1999

The question is what kind of logical system the Web needs, and the answer turns on giving up something that every earlier system treated as essential.

40.1 Five requirements, and the one that has to go

The note quotes Crawford and Kuipers on what a knowledge representation system must have: a compact syntax, well-defined semantics, enough expressive power to represent human knowledge, an efficient and understandable reasoning mechanism, and usability for large knowledge bases. Then the observation that sets up everything:

It has proved difficult, however, to achieve the third and fourth properties simultaneously. Crawford and Kuipers, quoted in The Semantic Web as a language of logic, 1999

Expressive power and efficient reasoning pull against each other. Berners-Lee chooses expressive power, and says exactly what is surrendered: the parts of the fourth requirement that conflict, notably a single efficient reasoning system.

40.2 A validator, not a prover

He offers a precedent for the move, and the parallel is the whole argument of Volume I arriving in a new field:

(This fundamental change goals from KR systems to the semantic web is loosely analogous with the goal change from conventional hypertext systems to the original Web design dropping link consistency in favor of expressive flexibility and scalability. The Semantic Web as a language of logic, 1999

Hypertext gave up the guarantee that links resolve. Logic gives up the guarantee that questions are answerable. In each case the guarantee was what had kept the system small.

40.3 Tractability by subsetting

Nothing is efficient about the whole, so efficiency is arranged in parts. Berners-Lee lists five ways a workable subsystem can be carved out of an intractable whole: subset the language by removing operations and rules; limit the statements to those from particular documents or sites; constrain the form of formulae with schemas; make application design decisions that guarantee tractable results with common engines; or construct proofs with hand-built application-specific programs.

What the whole must guarantee is correspondingly small, and he states it as three requirements: consistency, so that a contradiction cannot be deduced unless one was given; strength, so that all applications are subsets; and grounding in reality.

40.4 Grounding, which is not a logical matter

The section that separates this note from a logic paper is the one on grounding. It is not enough, Berners-Lee argues, to show that the system can be constrained so as to be isomorphic to some algebra. A particular mapping must be defined so that the representation conveys its semantics in a way that can meaningfully be combined with everything else.

He then gives the reason for urgency, dated 1999 and aimed at a specific future:

Electronic commerce needs a solid foundation in this way, and the development of the semantic web is (in 1999) essential to provide a rigid framework in which to define electronic commerce terms, before electronic commerce expands as a mass of vaguely defined semantics and ad hoc syntax which leaves no room for automatic treatment, and in which the court of law rather than a logical derivation settles arguments. The Semantic Web as a language of logic, 1999

Electronic commerce did expand as a mass of vaguely defined semantics and ad hoc syntax, and arguments are settled in court. The prediction was right and the intervention did not happen.

Where meaning actually bottoms out is stated practically in the next paragraph: the meaning of data is grounded in the non-Semantic-Web applications interfaced to it. A currency transfer application that accepts such input defines, for practical purposes, what the terms in the instrument mean. That is Chapter 37's closed loop, seen from the logic side.

40.5 Rules cannot be outside the language

One requirement is derived that later chapters depend on. Most knowledge representation systems keep their inference rules outside the language, because rules like substitution cannot be written in it. Berners-Lee observes that such rule sets are often not only redundant but arbitrary, and that a universal design must be minimalist.

The consequence is that different machines will use different algorithms and different sets of inference rules, and that what must be shared is not the algorithm but the check:

The essential this is that the results of either must be provably correct against the same basic minimalist rules. The Semantic Web as a language of logic, 1999

Chapter 41 takes this apart properly, showing that a rule holds two separable things: a fact that anyone can use, and a procedural hint that only its own engine can.

What happened since

  • The subsetting strategy was adopted formally. The Web Ontology Language shipped in 2004 with named profiles of deliberately different expressive power, and OWL 2 in October 2009 added three more, each chosen so that reasoning stays tractable. That is section 40.3 turned into a standard.
  • The proof layer was never built. No interoperable format for exchanging proofs was standardised, so the asymmetry of Figure 40.1, which the whole design rests on, was never cashed in practice.
  • Grounding happened in applications, as predicted. What terms mean in practice is still fixed by the programs that consume them, which is the note's own fallback rather than its ambition.
  • Checkability returned from another direction. The distinction between generating an answer by any means and verifying it cheaply is now the central design question in systems that use language models, which reach conclusions unverifiably and are increasingly paired with checkers that do not.
