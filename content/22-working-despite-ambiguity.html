The short version

No word is perfectly unambiguous, and Berners-Lee agrees. Work carries on anyway, just as physics keeps the solid billiard ball after learning about atoms. People have always built systems, such as dictionaries, courts and standards committees, that make words definite enough to use. The Semantic Web adds one thing: for any term, anyone can look up who owns it and how its meaning is fixed.

The question

In October 2012 an argument broke out on the mailing list of the Technical Architecture Group (the W3C’s small panel on Web architecture), not for the first time, over whether a symbol can ever have an unambiguous meaning. It began as background reading for a TAG meeting on httpRange-14, the long-disputed rule for what an http URI denotes (Chapters 8 and 9). Berners-Lee’s reply, lightly edited into this note, concedes the point at once and then asks how work is to continue. It was titled “Working without being ambushed by Ambiguity”, and ambush is the real subject: not whether the pedants are right, but what to do when they are right at an inconvenient moment.

22.1 Keeping the billiard ball

No symbol is ever completely unambiguous, so, the note says, some might call the Web’s architecture useless, since it asks that each URI denote one thing (stand for a particular thing). Berners-Lee answers with an analogy.

The naively pedantic response, he says, would be to throw away classical physics because the ball’s atoms could in principle all move three inches east at once. He states the parallel exactly:

So it is with denotation by names. We need to keep the models of ambiguity in our back pocket and bring them out when we need them, but not use them to ambush any discussion in the classical form. Working despite Ambiguity, 2012

The analogy lacks one thing. In physics, mathematics shows that the quantum model gives rise, to a high degree of approximation, to the classical one. For meaning there is no such derivation. The note offers something else: a set of working systems that make words definite enough.

22.2 Five ways of pinning a word down

Berners-Lee treats disambiguation not as a philosophical problem but as a catalogue of working systems, and lists five.

ModelHow meaning is fixedExample or remark
1. Authoritative dictionaryThe editor of the Oxford English Dictionary knows best how words are used; if a use is missing, send him a note.Looking up a word in the OED; perhaps, the note says, “the model we have in kindergarden”
2. Meaning as useA speaker may use words however they like; since meaning is use, no use is ever wrong.“Sometimes blamed on Wittgenstein”; slang, as when “sick” means good
3. ExpertiseLawyers train for years to agree tighter terms; if needed, a court adds definition.A contract
4. Areas of expertiseSpecialists in a field write what is needed and testify to it in court.An expert witness
5. Standards committeeDefines local terms until they are tight enough; others can challenge it while the group exists.A technical specification

The committee model gets the most careful description. A committee works until its terms are tight enough to make a standard people will use without undesirable consequence through misunderstanding, and Berners-Lee adds: not a standard which everyone will understand unambiguously in exactly the same way. He also notes, wryly, that a group sharing its work is sometimes horrified to find the larger community needs much longer discussion.

22.3 Dictionaries and courts are engineered

He then says what the five models are:

The initial philosophers seemed to treat language as a natural or god-made thing to be investigated not engineered invented things, but in fact dictionaries and court procedure and standards bodies are all engineered systems. So we can design the ones we need. Working despite Ambiguity, 2012

Disambiguation stops being something that happens to language and becomes something one builds. And all of them share one property:

A common facet of all these models is that they do not give complete unambiguity at all, just a good enough definition. Working despite Ambiguity, 2012

Since, he suggests, much of the world’s violence might stem from misunderstandings of some sort, improving them may be worth the effort.

22.4 Looking up who answers for a word

The Semantic Web is not a sixth model but sits above the five: each vocabulary (a published set of terms) fixes its meaning however it likes.

How a term is looked up

The Domain Name System (DNS) lets a person or organisation own a term. HTTP, the Web’s fetching protocol, lets a machine look the term up. What comes back explains it in natural language and in logic (Figure 22.2), and says, openly or by implication, which model, or mix of models, fixes its meaning.

Even here he hedges. What comes back would be called the term’s definition, he says, except that “we would be ambushed by people who want to use the word ‘definition’ specifically for a definition using one or other particular model”.

A sender then picks terms whose curation makes them, in Berners-Lee’s phrase, good enough for the situation. People still share understandings over beers in bars, he notes, but the machine version has two advantages: it lets anyone look up a meaning without pretending meaning is unambiguous, and it yields logic that programs can use.

A worked example: the seminar form

Both of the note’s examples concern an online seminar form.

Someone filling in the form can enter the date and times without likely misunderstanding. Choosing whether the event is a talk, a class, a seminar or a concert is harder, but a good application pulls in the vocabulary’s comment when the user hovers.

Someone building the form must pick a property, a published term for one kind of fact such as a start time, for each field, ideally from a widely used vocabulary, so that the events reach people’s calendars. They will find some standard terms, a few from other vocabularies, and then have to make some up. At that point, Berners-Lee says, the system should show the cost of getting others to agree, with buttons to invite comments, form a group, or ask another group to collaborate.

Pat Hayes’s warning

The note ends by quoting Pat Hayes on what happens to English when meanings really must be pinned down. In contracts, diplomatic messages and patent applications, he observes, almost every word is given an explicit definition, often with “guard” language denying its ordinary reading.

This is not normal language: to even use it requires specialized training, and it is dangerous for non-experts to attempt, exactly because it has hard unambiguous meanings which are independent of context. Pat Hayes, quoted in Working despite Ambiguity, 2012

Berners-Lee’s own closing advice is more practical: some of the rat-holes around ambiguity “need to be charted and left rarely visited”.

What happened since

  • October 2012: Wikidata. Launched on 29 October, two weeks after the note, Wikidata names things with http URIs that return data when looked up, and creates a new property only after public proposal and discussion, much like the form-builder’s buttons in section 22.4.
  • February 2014: the lookup became standard. RDF 1.1 Concepts and Abstract Syntax became a W3C Recommendation. It says that an IRI’s owner can help establish what the IRI refers to, and that dereferencing it can return information about it: much of section 22.4, in a standard.
