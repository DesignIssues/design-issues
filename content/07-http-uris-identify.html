The question

This is the longest argument in the collection and the only one written from a declared minority position. The status line is unusually exposed:

This was a result of my being in a minority with this opinion on the Technical Architecture Group, and yet finding it the only one I could accept. What do HTTP URIs Identify?, 2002

The question is what an HTTP URI denotes. For a decade nobody had needed to ask. Berners-Lee notes with some amusement that the ambiguity of natural language had concealed the problem so well that the original author of HTTP and its later co-author, who wrote his doctoral thesis analysing the Web, held conflicting ideas of what the terms meant without either noticing.

What forced the question was RDF. A language with well-defined logical properties cannot leave denotation vague, and once people began using HTTP URIs to name properties and classes rather than pages, two parties could use the same identifier for different things and never discover it.

7.1 The position being defended

Berners-Lee’s answer is that an HTTP URI without a fragment identifies a document, in a sense of “document” deliberately stretched to breaking point. His own list of what counts includes a poem, an order for ball bearings, a painting, a film, a review of a film, a sound clip, a record of the temperature of a furnace, and an array of a million integers all zero. He is explicit that the word is a placeholder:

I'm going to refer to this as a document, because it needs a term and that is the best I have to date, but the reader should be sure to realize that this does not mean a conventional office document What do HTTP URIs Identify?, 2002

These documents are generic in the sense of Chapter 10: abstractions that may have many bit representations varying by time, content type, natural language, and machine language. What holds them together is that the representations have a great deal in common, and that the publisher is the authority on how much variation is permitted.

7.2 Why the distinction must be kept

The argument against collapsing the document into the thing it describes is made twice, once on logical grounds and once on social ones. The logical version is short: protocols can now talk about cars as well as about pages, so a reader must either distinguish the car from the page about the car or be hopelessly fuzzy.

The social version is the one Berners-Lee cares about more:

The process of publishing and reading is a real social process between social entities, not mechanical agents. To be socially responsible, to be able to handle trust, and so on, we must be aware of these operations. The difference between a car and what some web page says about it is crucial - not only when you are buying a car. What do HTTP URIs Identify?, 2002

He then answers the opposing view directly. Some had argued that the abstract document is a fiction, and that all that exists when a page describes a car is the car and some bit streams. His reply is that the bit streams have far more in common with each other than any of them has with the car, and that the relationship between a page and a car can be any of home page, picture, catalogue entry, invoice, remote control panel or weblog. To dismiss the document is to lose the ability to say which.

7.3 Eight alternatives, tried and rejected

The bulk of the note works through the positions Berners-Lee does not hold. The method is worth more than the verdicts: each alternative is stated in its proponents’ terms, granted whatever it has going for it, and then failed on a specific consequence.

AlternativeWhy it is rejected
The URI identifies the abstract thing, not a documentLeaves no way to refer to the page itself
The owner may define it to mean whatever they likeConsistent, but nothing can be used before consulting the owner
Default to a page until told otherwiseRequires closed-world reasoning, which the Web forbids
Decide by a protocol operation such as GETWould work logically, but cannot be retrofitted to deployed servers
Let logic disambiguateRequires every consumer to carry the disambiguating rules
Let different properties imply different levelsSame objection, and fails when a property is new
Carry extra information alongside the URIA reference then needs more than a URI, breaking universality
Abandon abstract identification entirelyConsistent, but bars RDF from talking about documents

The rejection of the owner-defines position is the most instructive, because Berners-Lee concedes almost everything before refusing it. He calls it tempting and logically consistent, and notes that it is how uuid URIs already work. His objection is engineering rather than philosophy: he cannot use a URI until he has definitive information from the publisher about what it identifies, and there is no moment at which that information reliably arrives.

The consequence is put in a sentence that became the standard way of stating the problem:

Before I recommend the Vietnam War page, I have to be careful I am not recommending the Vietnam War. What do HTTP URIs Identify?, 2002

7.4 The redirect he proposed and did not get

Having rejected the alternatives, Berners-Lee sketches the change to HTTP that would make the owner-defines position workable. A new kind of redirect, which he numbers 343 and names “Abstract Object, please see”, would tell a client that the thing requested is abstract and point at a document about it.

He does not adopt it. He notes only that the avenue remains open and that the community could take it if it wished. Chapter 8 records what the community actually did, which is the same mechanism with a different number.

7.5 The defence of the hash

The note closes with questions, and one answer is worth keeping because it states the value of a single character:

The hash is not a minor appendage to the URI: It is the most significant piece of punctuation in the whole URIref. The hash adds a whole new level of abstraction and specification! What do HTTP URIs Identify?, 2002

The hash is what lets a document-naming system name things that are not documents, without giving up the guarantee that the part before the hash names a document. That is the whole of Berners-Lee’s position in one line, and it is why he preferred hash URIs to the alternative that eventually won alongside them.

What happened since

  • The author lost and the mechanism won. In June 2005 the Technical Architecture Group resolved the issue it had recorded as httpRange-14, adopting a runtime test rather than a rule fixed in the specification, which is the position section 7.3 rejects. It did so using a redirect closely resembling the one proposed here. Chapter 8 is Berners-Lee’s account of the resolution.
  • A note of concession was added to the page itself. By 2006 the page carried a header saying the question had been decided by the Group “in a compromise which I think works quite well”. The argument was left standing and the verdict was overtaken.
  • Both patterns survive. Hash URIs and redirect-based URIs are both in wide use, and the split has never closed. Vocabularies that predate the resolution, including Dublin Core and Friend of a Friend, use hashless HTTP URIs for properties, which is the practice this note argues against.
  • The recommendation problem persists outside RDF. Social platforms that resolve a link to a preview of the thing it describes rather than the page itself reproduce the Vietnam War ambiguity at consumer scale, without any of the machinery for detecting it.

Questions

7.2 The owner-defines position is called both tempting and consistent, then rejected on engineering grounds. Construct the smallest change to HTTP that would make it workable, and say what it would cost deployed clients.

7.3 Take a vocabulary you use that names properties with hashless HTTP URIs. Determine what its server returns for one of them, and say whether a client can tell a property from a page about it.
