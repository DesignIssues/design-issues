The short version

A web address (an HTTP URI) names a document, not the thing the document is about. The page about a car is not the car. Berners-Lee held this view in 2002 while in a minority on the W3C’s architecture group. He took the rival views one at a time and turned each one down.

His answer for naming things that are not documents is the hash (#): page#car names something described in page. In 2005 the group settled on a compromise that kept his distinction but used a redirect instead of the hash.

The question

This is the longest argument in the collection and the only one written from a declared minority position. The status line is unusually exposed:

This was a result of my being in a minority with this opinion on the Technical Architecture Group, and yet finding it the only one I could accept. What do HTTP URIs Identify?, 2002

The Technical Architecture Group is the small W3C committee charged with deciding questions about the Web’s overall design. The question put to it was what an HTTP URI denotes, meaning what thing in the world it is a name for. For a decade nobody had needed to ask. Berners-Lee notes with some amusement that the ambiguity of natural language had concealed the problem so well that the original author of HTTP and its later co-author, who wrote his doctoral thesis analysing the Web, held conflicting ideas of what the terms meant without either noticing.

What forced the question was RDF, a language for machine-readable statements of the form subject, property, value, in which every name is a URI. A language with well-defined logical properties cannot leave denotation vague. Once people began using HTTP URIs to name ideas such as “colour” or “is a kind of car” rather than pages, two parties could use the same identifier for different things and never discover it.

7.1 The position being defended

Berners-Lee’s answer is that an HTTP URI without a fragment (the part after a # sign) identifies a document, in a sense of “document” deliberately stretched to breaking point. His own list of what counts includes a poem, an order for ball bearings, a painting, a film, a review of a film, a sound clip, a record of the temperature of a furnace, and an array of a million integers all zero. He is explicit that the word is a placeholder:

I'm going to refer to this as a document, because it needs a term and that is the best I have to date, but the reader should be sure to realize that this does not mean a conventional office document What do HTTP URIs Identify?, 2002

Each such document is one abstract work that can be delivered as many different files, varying by date, format and language (Chapter 10 develops this). What holds the files together is that they have a great deal in common, and that the publisher is the authority on how much variation is permitted.

7.2 Why the distinction must be kept

The argument against collapsing the document into the thing it describes is made twice, once on logical grounds and once on social ones. The logical version is short: protocols can now talk about cars as well as about pages, so a reader must either distinguish the car from the page about the car or be hopelessly fuzzy.

The social version is the one Berners-Lee cares about more:

The process of publishing and reading is a real social process between social entities, not mechanical agents. To be socially responsible, to be able to handle trust, and so on, we must be aware of these operations. The difference between a car and what some web page says about it is crucial - not only when you are buying a car. What do HTTP URIs Identify?, 2002

The test of a shared link

The note’s most concrete passage turns this into a test anyone can run. Berners-Lee sees a web page with a photograph of a car. He copies the address from his browser and emails it to a friend: look, the car is a Toyota. The friend clicks the link. The same address comes up, but the page is a table of the car’s weight, length, colour and registration number.

Both pages are about the same car, yet the Web has failed. Every link carries the expectation that the person who follows it gets basically the same information as the person who made it. Some differences would not have mattered. A JPEG instead of a GIF, a lower resolution, a black-and-white copy, or for a reader who cannot see the picture a well-written description of it: each of these still carries the same information. A table of measurements does not. The test fixes what the address names:

The sameness we are after is the sameness of information content. What do HTTP URIs Identify?, 2002

If an address named the car, both pages would pass, since both are about the car. Only if it names the document does the test explain why one of them counts as failure.

7.3 Eight alternatives, tried and rejected

The bulk of the note works through the positions Berners-Lee does not hold. The method is worth more than the verdicts: each alternative is stated in its proponents’ terms, granted whatever it has going for it, and then failed on a specific consequence. One term needs explaining first. Closed-world reasoning means assuming that anything not stated is false. The Web is open, so new information can always arrive, and conclusions drawn that way can be overturned at any moment.

AlternativeWhy it is rejected
The URI identifies the abstract thing, not a documentMany systems already use URIs to name documents, and a URI can name only one thing; this leaves no way to refer to the page itself
The owner may define it to mean whatever they likeConsistent, but nothing can be used before consulting the owner
 … with a page as the default until told otherwiseRequires closed-world reasoning, which an open Web forbids
 … settled by a protocol operation such as GETWould work logically, but cannot be retrofitted to the millions of servers already deployed

The rejection of the owner-defines position is the most instructive, because Berners-Lee concedes almost everything before refusing it. He calls it tempting and logically consistent, and notes that it is how uuid URIs already work. His objection is engineering rather than philosophy: he cannot use a URI until he has definitive information from the publisher about what it identifies, and there is no moment at which that information reliably arrives.

The consequence is put in a sentence that became the standard way of stating the problem:

Before I recommend the Vietnam War page, I have to be careful I am not recommending the Vietnam War. What do HTTP URIs Identify?, 2002

7.4 The redirect he proposed and did not get

When a browser asks a server for an address, the reply starts with a three-digit status code: 200 means “here it is”, 404 “not found”, and codes in the 300s mean “look elsewhere”. A reply of that last kind is a redirect.

The note gives a real case of the problem. The RDF test suite used the address http://www.w3.org/2000/10/rdf-tests/TestSchema/NegativeParserTest to name a kind of software test, not a page. Asked for that address, the server returned 404. It had little choice: HTTP has no way to return a class of tests, only a document. And had it returned a document, nobody could refer to that document without accidentally referring to the class of tests too.

Having rejected the alternatives, Berners-Lee sketches the change to HTTP that would make the owner-defines position workable. A new kind of redirect, which he numbers 343 and names “Abstract Object, please see”, would tell a client that the thing requested is abstract and point at a document about it.

He does not adopt it. He notes only that the avenue remains open and that the community could take it if it wished. Chapter 8 records what the community actually did: in 2005 it adopted the same mechanism using an existing code, 303 “See Other”, in place of a new 343.

7.5 The defence of the hash

The note closes with questions, and one answer is worth keeping because it states the value of a single character:

The hash is not a minor appendage to the URI: It is the most significant piece of punctuation in the whole URIref. The hash adds a whole new level of abstraction and specification! What do HTTP URIs Identify?, 2002

The hash is what lets a document-naming system name things that are not documents, without giving up the guarantee that the part before the hash names a document. That is the whole of Berners-Lee’s position in one line, and it is why he preferred hash URIs to the redirect approach, which was adopted in 2005 and now coexists with them. What happened since

  • The author lost and the mechanism won. In June 2005 the Technical Architecture Group resolved the issue it had recorded as httpRange-14, adopting a runtime test rather than a rule fixed in the specification, which is the protocol-operation option in section 7.3’s table. It did so using a redirect closely resembling the one proposed here. Chapter 8 is Berners-Lee’s account of the resolution.
  • A note of concession was added to the page itself. By 2006 the page carried a header saying the question had been decided by the Group “in a compromise which I think works quite well”. The argument was left standing and the verdict was overtaken.
  • Both patterns survive. Hash URIs and redirect-based URIs are both in wide use, and the split has never closed. Vocabularies that predate the resolution, including Dublin Core and Friend of a Friend, use hashless HTTP URIs for properties, which is the practice this note argues against.
