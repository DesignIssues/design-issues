The short version

Two people who both call themselves supporters of “open” can talk past each other, because the word names several separate ideas. Asked to give a talk on the subject, Berners-Lee said the word was used in “at least eight” ways. The note itself names nine, and two more were added in 2024.

Separating the senses settles no argument, but it shows which argument is actually under way.

The question

Open source is about freedom. Open access is about price. Net neutrality is about what an internet provider may do with the traffic it carries. All three are called “open”, and confusing them, Berners-Lee wrote, “leads to a lot of misunderstanding”. A January 2013 keynote on the theme of “Open”, which he found underspecified, made him count:

I was recently asked to talk about the idea of “open”, and I realized the term is used in at least eight different ways. The Many Meanings of Open, 2013

The senses are independent. A system can be open in several and closed in the rest. The sections below take up the senses the note argues about; the tables summarise the rest.

25.2 Universality, and what it was designed against

The note puts first the sense the Web was built for, and explains it by what it was designed to avoid:

When I designed the Web protocols, I had already seen many networked information systems fail because they made some assumptions about the users – that they were using a particular type of computer for instance – or constrained the way they worked, such as forcing them to organize their data in a particular way, or to use a particular data format. The Many Meanings of Open, 2013

The note pairs it with decentralisation (not one of the eleven): no central authority’s permission is needed to post, and no single node can take everything down by failing. The aim was that “anyone should be able to publish anything on the Web”, independent of technical constraints “as well as language, character sets, and culture”.

25.3 The sense he argues hardest for

A native app is written for one operating system, such as a phone’s, and installed on it rather than run in a browser. The Open Web Platform section turns on one short sentence:

Native apps are not on the Web, they're not part of the Web. The Many Meanings of Open, 2013

So he encourages everyone to build web apps.

25.4 The one he declines to settle

Openness with personal data is the shortest section, and Berners-Lee leaves it open:

The word “open” is often used in the sense “I wouldn't be that open with my personal life”. We are as a society learning how to draw the right boundaries in this new age. The Many Meanings of Open, 2013

A site that helps people share may also profit from their data in unforeseen ways. Volume VII picks up the thread.

25.5 Where law enters

An open platform means the right to install and write software on one’s own device. One motive for closing it, the note says, is a manufacturer wanting users to experience its content without storing it or passing it on.

In the US, he writes in 2013, two laws aggravate this: the Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA), which forbids getting round copy protection, and the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA), which punishes unauthorised access. Both can send a person who uses a computer improperly to prison for a long time. Their effect on the argument is the point:

These unjust laws colour the debate so much in the US that some people react by saying that all platforms should be completely open, so that no one can be said to use them improperly. The Many Meanings of Open, 2013

An unjust law, on this account, produces an absolutist opposition. He hopes instead for laws that balance paying creative people against consumers’ need to “rip, mix, quote and archive”.

25.6 The two additions

In May 2024 came open science, after many talks at CERN’s seventieth anniversary “were about the importance of Open Science”. In September came open process, in the words of the essayist Hamish Campbell:

Open process refers to the transparent and participatory decision-making processes that govern [tech] projects. The Many Meanings of Open, 2024

His examples are W3C, the IETF and open source projects, but he adds that there is no reason it has to be a tech project.

25.7 The one rule: net neutrality

Net neutrality is the sense the note defines by a rule: non-discrimination by the internet service provider (ISP) that carries a user’s traffic. The alternative is packets delivered in a way that discriminates “for commercial or political reasons”. The stakes he names are not only economic: “It's essential to an open, fair democracy.”

25.8 Open data: the sense he campaigned for

Here Berners-Lee was an actor, not an observer. The problem that moved him in 2009:

Too many websites could generate nice reports as documents, but had no way to access the data behind it to check and build on the results. The Many Meanings of Open, 2013

In February 2009 he asked a TED audience for their data and got them to chant “raw data now”. In April he began open government data work with Gordon Brown, then UK Prime Minister. In 2010 he returned to TED to show what had been achieved, “including Open Street Map's role in relief efforts in Haiti”, and in 2012 he helped launch the Open Data Institute in London.

The note ends: “we must all continue to push for these various forms of openness in the appropriate places.” The qualifier matters: personal data (section 25.4) is one place where more openness is not simply better.

What happened since

  • The platform argument was lost and partly regained. The EU’s Digital Markets Act, applicable from March 2024, requires large platforms to permit alternative app distribution: the right of section 25.5, as law.
  • Open access became policy. The US National Institutes of Health made its public-access policy mandatory in April 2008, and other funders followed. Plan S, launched in September 2018, required immediate open publication of funded work from 2021.
  • Net neutrality did not settle. US rules were adopted in 2015, repealed in 2017, readopted in 2024 and struck down by a federal appeals court in January 2025. The EU’s Open Internet Regulation applied from 2016. Chapter 78 has the detail.
  • The CFAA was narrowed, not repealed. Aaron Swartz, prosecuted under it for bulk-downloading JSTOR articles, died in January 2013, the month of the keynote. In June 2021 the Supreme Court, in Van Buren v. United States, read its “exceeds authorized access” clause narrowly.
  • Open data lost its novelty. The US data.gov (May 2009) and the UK’s data.gov.uk (January 2010) still operate.

Connections

Universality and decentralisation are principles of Chapter 1, implemented by the axioms of Chapter 2. Open standards are written as in Chapter 21 and stacked as in Chapter 20. Open data is Chapter 53, personal data is Volume VII, net neutrality is Chapter 78, and digital rights management is Chapter 80.
