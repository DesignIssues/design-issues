The short version

Social networks suggest new friends who are friends of existing friends. The suggestions are usually welcome, but they close people into tight groups where everyone knows everyone, and the Web’s power to connect distant people goes unused.

This note proposes the stretch friend: now and then, suggest someone like the user in every way but one, such as race, religion or home town. The shared ground makes the one difference easier to cross. People who have never known anyone of another race find outsiders easier to treat as enemies, so this matters. Rather than model the effect, the note suggests simply trying it.

The question

Dated 2011, presented in talks including the 2012 WWW conference keynote in Lyon, and left as a first draft, this note asks whether the thing the Web is praised for is actually being used.

We celebrate the web as a space which allows you to discover all kinds of new things people and places. We admire it as something which breaks down geographical barriers, allowing me to interact with someone on the other side of the world as easily as with the person next door. But are we really using that aspect, of it? Stretch friends, 2011

89.1 What recommendation does

Social networking sites suggest people a user might know, usually like this:

The simple way xdoing this is just to pick people who are friend s of our existing friends. We are almost sure to like them, especialy if we have a lot of friends in common. Stretch friends, 2011

It works. That is the problem:

So what happens if we accept these new proposed friends? Out social network becomes a solid mass of mutually connected people. We make cliques. Stretch friends, 2011

A clique is a closed group in which everyone knows everyone else. Each accepted suggestion closes a triangle (Figure 89.1), and the result, in the note’s words, “doesn’t in fact add new social experience”.

The note cites Eli Pariser’s The Filter Bubble (2011): a filter bubble is a feed that shows a person only people and views like their own. Children able to play an online game with anyone in the world end up playing with the child next door, or the one at the other end of the sofa.

This is good for the cohesion of the neighborhood, but is it good for the planet? Stretch friends, 2011

89.2 The proposal

Now and then, instead of a friend of a friend, the system would suggest someone almost identical to the user who differs in one respect, such as race or home town. The note’s own example:

You are a white male liberal geek in New York and you are offered a black male liberal geek in New York or maybe a white male liberal geek in Saudi Arabia. Stretch friends, 2011

Only one boundary is crossed at a time:

Just one at a time. Maybe more later, but for now, let's see how you get on. Stretch friends, 2011

If lots of people had stretch friends, the note asks, would the world be “an incrementaly better place, or maybe after a while a dramatically different place?”

89.3 The evidence he cites

The note rests on one analysis, flagged as needing checking. In a TED talk, Nate Silver described his puzzlement that in the election which first elected Barack Obama, almost the whole country shifted to the liberal side except one group, people who could not imagine a black person being president. Who were they?

Well, they were by strong correlation the people who answered "no" to the question, "Have you ever lived or worked with someone of a different race". Stretch friends, 2011

Berners-Lee generalises from the election to human beings:

So human beings have an ability to mark people outside their normal zone as being strange, or foreign, or enemy. We have evolved the neurology to become tribal at the drop of a hat Stretch friends, 2011

He calls “rather horrid” the experiments in which school children wore badges showing their eye colour; the reference is left as a placeholder. (Editor’s note: this is presumably Jane Elliott’s 1968 blue-eyes/brown-eyes classroom exercise.) Breaking down such single differences, which otherwise let people switch into a tribal mode, therefore becomes a matter of urgency.

89.4 The invitation

The note ends without a worked-out scheme. It links the idea to twinning projects, in which towns or schools pair up with partners in very different parts of the world, noting that church-arranged ones do not always cross religious boundaries. The closing line is an argument about method:

Web scientists, people who analyze these things, could do lots of math to try work out what the effect would be. Or we could just try it. Stretch friends, 2011

Chapter 87 admits it has no mathematics for a healthy society; this note treats that as no reason to wait. One reading is that the intervention is cheap and easy to decline, so it can be tried first and measured afterwards.

What happened since

  • No major platform is known to have built it. The incentive runs the other way: similar people engage more.
  • The diagnosis was studied at scale. Homophily, people’s tendency to connect with people like themselves, became a central topic; in 2015 Bakshy, Messing and Adamic (Science) measured how far Facebook users’ friends and choices narrowed their exposure to opposing news.
  • The contact hypothesis held up. Gordon Allport’s 1954 idea that contact across a difference reduces prejudice, the mechanism this proposal relies on, was supported by Pettigrew and Tropp’s 2006 review of over 500 studies, with conditions attached.
  • Exposure to difference can backfire. Bail and colleagues (PNAS, 2018) paid Twitter users to follow a bot retweeting the other political side; Republicans in particular became more polarised. Such encounters are adversarial rather than acquainted, a distinction the note does not draw.
