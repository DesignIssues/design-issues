The short version

In 2008 a company proposed to inspect everything passing through people’s internet connections at home, work out every page each household visited, and sell advertising against the result. Berners-Lee attended a discussion about it at the House of Lords. These are his notes.

The argument is not the expected one. The harm he identifies is not to the individual whose browsing is read, but to the act of reading itself, and therefore to a society that decides things by reading.

The question

These are notes Berners-Lee made for a meeting at the House of Lords on 11 February 2009, and he says why a note about the network layer belongs in a collection about the Web:

Most of these notes are about architecture at the web layer. However, a healthy web for society places requirements also on the Internet layer. No Snooping, 2009

The occasion was a proposal by the company Phorm to inspect the contents of network traffic at the provider, build a profile of every address a household visited, and use it to target advertising.

71.1 The equivalence

The argument opens by refusing to treat this as new:

The Internet in general has and deserves the same protection as paper mail and telephone. No Snooping, 2009

It then claims more protection, not the same:

If fact you could argue that it needs it more, as it carries more or our lives and is more revealing than our phone calls or our mail. No Snooping, 2009

An internet service provider (ISP) is the company that carries a customer’s traffic. Every message is cut into pieces called packets. The outside of each packet carries the delivery address; the inside carries the content. The provider needs only the outside to do its job. Reading the inside on the way past is called deep packet inspection, the technique Phorm proposed to use. The note says exactly where the line falls:

The access by an ISP of information within an internet packet, other than that information used for routing, is equivalent to wirtetapping a phone or opening sealed postal mail. No Snooping, 2009

71.2 What an address discloses

A web address (URL) names the exact page someone asked for, not just the site. A list of them is therefore a diary of what a person wanted to know, kept before they had told anyone.

The URLs which people use reveal a huge amount about their lives, loves, hates, and fears. This is extremely sensitive material. No Snooping, 2009

Forms make it worse. A search can produce an address such as https://example.org/search?q=chest+pain+left+arm: the words typed are now part of the address, visible to anyone who reads the payload.

71.3 The argument about democracy

The passage that gives the note its weight is not about privacy as a personal good:

We use the internet to inform ourselves as voters in a democracy. We use the internet to decide what is true and what is not. We use the internet for healthcare and social interaction and so on. These things will all have a completely different light cast on then if the users know that the click will be monitored and the data will be shared with third parties. No Snooping, 2009

The harm is to the activity rather than to the person. Reading in order to decide changes when reading is observed, and a society that decides things by reading has therefore lost something even if no individual is harmed.

71.4 The argument nobody expected

One passage predicts a consequence that had not yet happened and reads now as an account of what did:

If people really want privacy, then many users and sites may switch to using SSL encryption: to doing theior actual web surfing thorugh an encrypted tunnel. This takes a lot of server CPU cycles, making server farms more expensive. It would slow the user's computer. It would effectively slow down the whole net. It also prevents the use of HTTP proxies, which currently help the efficiency of web access. No Snooping, 2009

SSL (now TLS) scrambles traffic so that only the two ends can read it. A proxy or shared cache is a machine in the middle that keeps copies of popular pages, and it cannot do that with traffic it cannot read. This was a warning against surveillance on efficiency grounds, six years before Chapter 67. It was right about the mechanism and wrong about the cost: encryption got cheap, and caching moved to the site’s side (Figure 71.3).

71.5 Why it must be illegal rather than discouraged

The conclusion is about incentives, and it explains why the note asks for law rather than for norms:

The power of this information is so great that the commercial incentive for companies or individuals misuse it will be huge, so it is essential to have absolute clarity that it is illegal. No Snooping, 2009

Where a thing is enormously valuable and only norms forbid it, someone will do it. Berners-Lee’s demand is for clarity rather than severity: not that the penalty be large, but that nobody be able to argue about whether the rule applies.

The note closes with a statement of principle:

The act of reading, like the act of writing, is a pure, fundamendal, human act. It must be available without interference or spying. No Snooping, 2009

What happened since

  • Europe objected. In April 2009 the European Commission began infringement proceedings against the United Kingdom over its handling of the interception rules.
  • The specific proposal failed. BT, which had trialled Phorm’s system, dropped its plans in July 2009; the other large British providers that had signed up made no commitment to deploy it.
  • The encryption prediction came true and the costs did not. Let’s Encrypt, a free certificate authority, opened to the public in December 2015, and from July 2018 Chrome labelled every unencrypted page as not secure.
  • The surveillance moved to where encryption does not reach. Profiling moved into pages and apps, through third-party cookies and tracking scripts. Firefox began blocking known trackers’ cookies by default in September 2019, and Safari blocked all third-party cookies from March 2020.
  • The principle entered law unevenly. The EU’s General Data Protection Regulation, applying from May 2018, treats browsing data linked to a person as personal data requiring a legal basis.
