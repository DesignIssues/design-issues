The short version

In 2008 the company Phorm proposed to read the traffic on British home internet connections, list every page each household visited, and sell advertising against the result. Berners-Lee attended a meeting about it at the House of Lords on 11 February 2009. These are his notes. He had already spoken against it on BBC News in March 2008.

He argues two harms. One falls on the person whose browsing is read: a profile could be bought, used against them in hiring or insurance, or used to stalk them. The other falls on reading itself, and so on a society that decides things by reading.

The question

His notes open by saying why a note about the network layer belongs here:

Most of these notes are about architecture at the web layer. However, a healthy web for society places requirements also on the Internet layer. No Snooping, 2009

71.1 Opening the envelope

The argument begins by refusing to treat this as new:

The Internet in general has and deserves the same protection as paper mail and telephone. No Snooping, 2009

It then claims more protection, not the same:

If fact you could argue that it needs it more, as it carries more or our lives and is more revealing than our phone calls or our mail. No Snooping, 2009

An internet service provider (ISP) carries a customer’s traffic in pieces called packets. The outside of each packet carries the delivery address, the inside the content, and the provider needs only the outside. Reading the inside on the way past is deep packet inspection, Phorm’s technique. The note draws the line exactly:

The access by an ISP of information within an internet packet, other than that information used for routing, is equivalent to wirtetapping a phone or opening sealed postal mail. No Snooping, 2009

71.2 What an address discloses

The note puts the scale in one comparison:

To put his in perspective, it is like the company having a video camera inside your house, except that it gives them actually much more information about you. No Snooping, 2009

The reason is that a web address (URL) names the exact page someone asked for, not just the site. A list of them is therefore a diary of what a person wanted to know, kept before they had told anyone.

The URLs which people use reveal a huge amount about their lives, loves, hates, and fears. This is extremely sensitive material. No Snooping, 2009

Forms make it worse. A search can produce an address such as https://example.org/search?q=chest+pain+left+arm: the words typed are now part of the address, visible to anyone who reads the payload. That is the note’s third harm: an insurer could discriminate against whoever looked up cardiac symptoms.

71.3 Watched reading is different reading

A voter checking a candidate’s claim, someone wondering whether they are homosexual, a patient reading about symptoms: each reads differently once the click is known to be logged and shared. The note’s central passage:

We use the internet to inform ourselves as voters in a democracy. We use the internet to decide what is true and what is not. We use the internet for healthcare and social interaction and so on. These things will all have a completely different light cast on then if the users know that the click will be monitored and the data will be shared with third parties. No Snooping, 2009

The book’s reading of that passage: even if no profile were ever misused, people who know they are watched read less freely, and a democracy that decides by reading decides worse.

71.4 A prediction that came true at a different price

One passage predicted a consequence that had not yet happened:

If people really want privacy, then many users and sites may switch to using SSL encryption: to doing theior actual web surfing thorugh an encrypted tunnel. This takes a lot of server CPU cycles, making server farms more expensive. It would slow the user's computer. It would effectively slow down the whole net. It also prevents the use of HTTP proxies, which currently help the efficiency of web access. No Snooping, 2009

SSL (now TLS) lets only the two ends read the traffic, so a proxy cache in the middle, which keeps copies of popular pages, can no longer work. This was a warning against surveillance on efficiency grounds, six years before his 2015 note on moving the Web to encryption (Chapter 67). It was right about the mechanism and wrong about the cost: encryption got cheap, and caching moved to the site’s side (Figure 71.3).

71.5 Why it must be illegal rather than discouraged

The note asks for law rather than norms, because of incentives:

The power of this information is so great that the commercial incentive for companies or individuals misuse it will be huge, so it is essential to have absolute clarity that it is illegal. No Snooping, 2009

When something is this valuable and only good manners forbid it, someone will do it. So the law must leave no room to argue. The note closes on principle:

The act of reading, like the act of writing, is a pure, fundamendal, human act. It must be available without interference or spying. No Snooping, 2009

What happened since

  • Europe objected, and British law changed. BT’s secret trials of the system on customers in 2006 and 2007 came out in 2008. In April 2009 the European Commission began infringement proceedings against the United Kingdom over its interception rules, and in September 2010 referred it to the EU Court of Justice. The UK amended its interception law in 2011, and the Commission closed the case in January 2012.
  • The specific proposal failed. BT dropped its plans in July 2009; the other large British providers that had signed up made no commitment to deploy it.
  • The encryption prediction came true and the costs did not. Let’s Encrypt, a free certificate authority, opened to the public in December 2015, and from July 2018 Chrome labelled every unencrypted page as not secure.
  • The surveillance moved to where encryption does not reach. Profiling moved into pages and apps, through third-party cookies and tracking scripts. Safari blocked all third-party cookies from March 2020.

Questions

71.1 Besides harm to the person, the note names a harm to the activity of reading. Name another activity that changes when observed, and say what society loses.

71.2 Encryption took the contents from the provider. Where did the watching go, and does the envelope analogy still apply there?

71.3 A company says its profiling is anonymous and opt-out. Why would that not meet the note’s “absolute clarity”?
