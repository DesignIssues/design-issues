The short version

Tags are quick but mean different things to different people. Well-defined data is reusable but slow to enter. Berners-Lee proposes a label, made once from shared terms such as place, and attached to a folder. Every item in the folder carries that meaning; the folder keeps its private name.

The question

Written in November 2006, at the height of enthusiasm for tagging, this note tries to reconcile the ease of typing a word with the reusability of a well-defined term.

Berners-Lee states the worry:

On the other hand, there is the sinking feeling that tags are headed the same way as keywords of Information Retrieval in the 1980s: initial hope, and then being stranded between the unbearable constraints of a controlled vocabulary and the hopeless ambiguity of uncontrolled user-generated keywords. Using labels to give semantics to tags, 2006

Information Retrieval is the field of document search. A controlled vocabulary is a fixed list of approved terms. The note looks for a way between that and free keywords.

36.1 The two worlds

On one side, tagging: quick, personal, and hard to reuse, because the same word means different things when used by another person or on another site. On the other, well-defined data: locations, relationships, authorship, each recorded with a global identifier (a name that means the same everywhere). Such data is extremely reusable, but not as quick as tagging with a single word off the top of one’s head.

He mentions a community considering a way to declare that one uses a tag the same way as another person, and his comment states the design goal:

That would be a very interesting feature, as it would allow a useful definition to gain growing acceptance, to progressively move from being a private idea to being a group global standard. Using labels to give semantics to tags, 2006

36.2 The luggage label

The proposal takes its name from a physical object. A luggage label is tied to a bag by its owner and can be read by anyone. In software, a label is a set of common facts, built once from menus of shared terms and dropped on a folder.

36.3 The example, which is a complaint about software

Berners-Lee photographs a local soccer team twice, against Wayland and against Arlington, loads the pictures into iPhoto, and wants to burn a CD for the team and find the photographs later.

On the first day he took no other photographs, so a folder defined by date was enough (iPhoto’s smart album, whose contents are chosen by a rule). On the second day he took others too, so he picked the right ones by hand into a plain folder. Then the wish: a folder Soccer with the two as subfolders. Another smart album combining them works, but is not as intuitive and lacks the hierarchical interface he wants.

The note observes, in separate places, that these folders are classes in RDF (a class is a named set of things) and that the folder names are his local tags. Nothing new is needed in the model, only a way to say what a folder means.

The software’s workaround is to apply keywords to every photograph in a folder as a batch. The data is then stored in each photograph, but the fact that it was applied to the whole folder is lost. A label keeps that fact.

36.4 Writing a label down

The note gives three ways to write the same label, and says they are “more or less inter-convertible”:

A label is not a photo

The note warns of one trap. The label is a class, not a photograph: who made the label, and when, has nothing to do with who took the photographs, or when.

Sharing and smart labels

Tags stay local, but they can be shared, and then a rule applies: tagging things with another person’s tag gives them the properties of that tag’s label. Using someone’s tag means taking on its meaning, which is the mechanism behind the hope quoted in 36.1.

Combine a smart album’s selection rule with a label and the result is a smart label, a rule with a single variable: any photo that matches X also has Y, such as: any photo taken on the day of the Wayland game shows our team. Berners-Lee suggests the two metaphors together could let lay users write rules.

What happened since

  • The labelling language arrived. The content-labelling incubator group mentioned in the note (W3C’s Content Label Incubator Group) led to POWDER (Protocol for Web Description Resources), which became a W3C Recommendation in September 2009.
  • The software in the example was retired. Apple replaced iPhoto with its Photos application in April 2015.
  • The interface complaint stands. Nested collections, where membership of a child implies membership of the parent, remain unusual in consumer software.
