The short version

Tags are quick but mean different things to different people. Well-defined data is reusable but slow to enter. Berners-Lee proposes a label, made once from shared terms such as place, and attached to a folder. Every item in the folder carries that meaning; the folder keeps its private name.

The question

Berners-Lee photographed a local soccer team at two games and put the pictures into folders named Soccer vs Wayland and Soccer vs Arlington. The names were, in his words, “essentially my local tags”, and “Exporting them doesn’t help much.” Saying where the photographs were taken, and who is in them, in terms another person or program could use is slower than typing a word. This November 2006 note asks whether one can have both, and states the worry:

On the other hand, there is the sinking feeling that tags are headed the same way as keywords of Information Retrieval in the 1980s: initial hope, and then being stranded between the unbearable constraints of a controlled vocabulary and the hopeless ambiguity of uncontrolled user-generated keywords. Using labels to give semantics to tags, 2006

Information Retrieval is the field of document search; a controlled vocabulary is a fixed list of approved terms.

36.1 Quick to type, or reusable: pick one

On one side, tagging: quick, personal, and hard to reuse, because the same word means different things when used by another person or on another site. On the other, well-defined data: locations, relationships, authorship, each recorded with a global identifier (a name that means the same everywhere). Such data is extremely reusable, but slower to enter.

He mentions a community considering a way to declare that one uses a tag the same way as another person, and his comment states the design goal:

That would be a very interesting feature, as it would allow a useful definition to gain growing acceptance, to progressively move from being a private idea to being a group global standard. Using labels to give semantics to tags, 2006

36.2 The luggage label

The proposal takes its name from a luggage label, which the note would draw on screen as “a box with a hole in the end with a bit of string”. A label is a set of common facts, built once from menus of shared terms. The user should be able to “drop a label on a folder, and let all the things in the folder inherit the labeled properties”, so the soccer folders keep their private names while saying something anyone can read.

36.3 Two games, two folders, no parent

The example has a person behind it: “I face these problems day to day, and like many geeks, am driven by the urge to make the boring things in life happen automatically”. He loaded the soccer photographs into iPhoto, wanting to burn a CD for the team and find the pictures later.

The first folder was a smart album, chosen by a rule (that day’s photographs); the second, a plain folder picked by hand. Then the wish: a folder Soccer with the two as subfolders. A smart album combining them works, but lacks the hierarchical interface he wants.

In RDF these folders are classes (named sets of things). Nothing new is needed in the model, only a way to say what a folder means.

The software’s workaround is to apply keywords to every photograph in a folder as a batch. The data is then stored in each photograph, but the fact that it was applied to the whole folder is lost. A label keeps that fact.

36.4 Writing a label down

Under the covers the note writes the same label three ways: as a rule in N3 (Chapter 42), as OWL restrictions, and with a special label vocabulary. It calls them “more or less inter-convertible”.

A label is not a photo

The note warns of one trap. The label is a class, not a photograph: who made the label, and when, has nothing to do with who took the photographs, or when. So the location and subject cannot be written on the class itself, as if the category stood in the field (Figure 36.4).

Using someone’s tag means taking on its meaning

Tags stay local, but they can be shared, and then a rule applies: tagging things with another person’s tag gives them the properties of that tag’s label. That is the mechanism behind the hope quoted in 36.1.

A smart album’s selection rule combined with a label gives a smart label, a one-variable rule: any photo taken on the day of the Wayland game shows our team. Here the note makes its largest claim:

A lot of information can be expressed as rules, but finding an intuitive user interface to allow lay users to express their needs with rules has been a stumbling block. These smart folder and label metaphors, combined, could be a route to solving this problem Using labels to give semantics to tags, 2006

What happened since

  • The labelling language arrived. The note’s Web Content Labeling (WCL) Incubator Group fed into POWDER (Protocol for Web Description Resources), a W3C Recommendation in September 2009.
  • Tags with defined properties appeared. In January 2007 Flickr launched “machine tags” of the form namespace:predicate=value, such as geo:lat=…: a tag that carries a defined property rather than a private word.
  • The software in the example was retired. Apple replaced iPhoto with its Photos application in April 2015.
