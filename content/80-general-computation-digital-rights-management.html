The short version

Digital rights management (DRM) is software that lets a film play on a viewer’s machine while preventing copying. Around 2013 the W3C was split over whether HTML should offer a standard slot for it. Berners-Lee separates four things people want, shows they cannot all hold at once, and leaves the choice open. In 2017 the W3C, with Berners-Lee as its Director, published the slot as Encrypted Media Extensions (EME).

The question

Should the open Web’s standard include a slot for software the user cannot inspect or control? Berners-Lee put the choice like this:

So should W3C just say "DRM is evil we should not collaborate with it in any way" and end up driving people to native apps where the whole app is locked down on a locked down platform, or should we open up a slot in an open system to allow a locked-down system to be accessed? General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS, 2013

The note, written in 2013 and revised in 2023, first separates the issues, because:

In the fervent discussion, very extreme positions have been taken, which has led to the debate becoming acrimonious, to the extent that much more heat than light tends to be available. Here we tease out some separate issues which have become entangled. General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS, 2013

80.1 Four rights that cannot all hold

A film streamed to a browser plays on the screen, but the viewer cannot clip a scene to quote it, keep it if the service closes, or read the code of the decoder that unlocks it. Each loss is one of the rights the note names.

Berners-Lee pulls four wants out of the argument (Figure 80.1). Three belong to the user: the open-source right to have and change the code one runs; the right to have root, meaning full administrator control, over one’s own computer; and the right to copy what one watches. The fourth belongs to the seller.

80.2 Locked decoder or locked platform

The Electronic Frontier Foundation and the author Cory Doctorow argued that HTML should not allow DRM at all, because it moved computing platforms towards big-company control. The reply was tactical. The case for allowing a hook in the standard, a standard slot through which a page hands encrypted video to a closed decoder, is not that the mechanism is good, but where people end up otherwise:

there are companies who will never put their movies on the net without DRM, so basically if we don't put DRM hooks in HTML they will just stick with flash and force you install an app, or a completely closed platform like a set-top box - in other words use a completely locked down platform. General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS, 2013

Flash was Adobe’s proprietary video plug-in. The conclusion:

So it isn't as though making DRM more difficult in HTML5 will make DRM go away: it will just force users off the web. General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS, 2013

80.3 Four people, four objections

The note sets out what different people believe, labelling none of them wrong.

PositionWhat it rests on
Copyright itself is wrongArtists should be supported by live gigs and donations
I judge when to copyCopyright has been twisted and “applies e.g. to 30 year old movies when it should not”; protection blocks fair use, backups and archives
Protection is worth it for a thriving industryNot fussed about archives: they “don't really watch movies they have bought a long time ago, or they are too young to have experienced the problem”; no answer for bands without a DRM channel
I object to hardware I do not controlIndependent of any wish to copy anything

The second position holds the prediction that has aged best: protected files fail when the supporting system changes. The fourth has nothing to do with copyright.

80.4 Ten years later: the laptop survived, the artist did not

The 2023 revision on what was standardised:

This allows the web site to get access to one of a small set of DRM gadget on the device -- gadgets which the user has no control of. General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS, 2023

Streaming happened at enormous scale, on the Web and off, and the platforms still push people towards their apps. Yet sharing a clip from an app “typically generates a link which will take the recipient back to the web version”: even the apps lean on the Web.

What survived

As a developer, I can still develop code on my Apple laptop and still install open source code and random apps written by other people on it. General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS, 2023

One machine, but the very thing the objectors feared losing: control of a general-purpose computer.

What did not

As an artist, there is no way for me to make my own DRM platform if a person wants to protect their material. So they have to find a route to the big platforms. General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS, 2023

The big platforms, the revision adds, “are infamous for returning very little funds to the original artists”. The open route remains: “I can make my music or video available on my website, and give it away for free, or charge for it without protecting it.” The 2013 note’s question, how to pay those who write or perform music “with no 3rd party gatekeeper”, is still open.

What happened since

  • The objectors split. In May 2014 Mozilla reluctantly agreed to support EME in Firefox so its users would not have to switch browsers: the tactical argument, accepted by an objector.
  • The specification was published. EME became a W3C Recommendation on 18 September 2017, over formal objection; the Electronic Frontier Foundation resigned from the Consortium in protest.
  • Flash ended. Adobe stopped supporting Flash Player at the end of 2020.
  • The archive prediction came true. In 2019 Microsoft closed the books section of its store, and the protected e-books customers had bought stopped working; Microsoft refunded them.
  • Implementation stayed concentrated. In the 2020s browsers supported a handful of protection systems, chiefly Google’s Widevine, Microsoft’s PlayReady and Apple’s FairPlay, so the barrier in Figure 80.3 remains.
