The short version

A specification is a document that says exactly what counts as, say, “a conforming HTTP 1.1 server”. It has to do two jobs: name the thing, and define it. Where it can, it should say what things mean rather than what software must do about them. And a program should be able to test whether something conforms.

The question

Berners-Lee opens by calling this note motherhood and apple pie and closes by calling it folklore. He credits Peggie Rimmer at CERN and years of work with the IEEE, the IETF and W3C, and assumes others have written books on it. They have, but the note makes one structural point that longer treatments bury.

21.1 A specification has two halves

A technical specification defines something. The document must specify the thing being defined as well as give its definition: a “left hand side” as well as a “right hand side”. Both must be done quite precisely. The essentials of a specification, 1999

On the Web the thing defined is usually a language or a protocol. In the note’s terms, a language is a set of symbols, rules for combining them, and what they mean; a protocol is a language for messages plus constraints on the order in which messages may be sent. Something conforms when it meets the definition. Text that is normative is binding on what conforms; non-normative text is explanation only. Standards writers use a capitalised MUST for a requirement and MAY for something allowed but optional, following a 1997 Internet convention (BCP 14).

Most specifications spend their length on the right-hand side and leave the left implicit. The note insists that the thing defined gets a precise name: a conformance term.

Wherever possible, the note adds, conformance phrases should be grounded in the Web by being identified with a URI.

The name then governs the wording of the definition. If the term is “foo-valid document”, a sentence saying something “is invalid” clearly applies to it. A sentence saying something “is wrong” or “produces an error” does not, unless the text explicitly ties it to the conformance term. Only words linked to the term carry weight.

21.2 Meaning, not behaviour

The sharpest rule in the note is about the grammatical mood a specification should be written in. When defining a language, say what a construct means rather than what software should do when it meets one. His example is a cheque:

“x” indicated that the check is void The essentials of a specification, 1999

is better than the version that says the cheque should be rejected with a fatal error.

The justification is a claim about what an author is entitled to do:

You can tell people what something means, you can't tell them what to do about it, unless you are defining a protocol. The essentials of a specification, 1999

Typical behaviours may still be described, and Berners-Lee says they can be very useful for explaining intent, provided they are marked non-normative.

Protocols are the exception, and for them he asks for something stricter still: constraints given as a state transition diagram or table, so that they are totally clear.

21.3 Two paragraphs nobody expects

Two short passages lift this note above a style guide.

The first is about contracts. When a message binds people or organisations, the specification must say so, or its authors could end up in court explaining it. The note sketches the kind of prose required:

The MMTP protocol defines the meaning of a message sent by or on behalf of a party herein referred to as the “debtor” to a party referred to as the “creditor”. The creditor is identified by the foo-email-address... The essentials of a specification, 1999

MMTP is invented. This is Chapter 20’s argument, that defined fields are statements people can be held to, arriving as drafting advice.

The second is a checklist for borrowing. When part of a specification is deliberately made similar to another, the note asks the author to:

  1. say that the similarity has been noticed;
  2. say whether it is exact, and if not, where not and why;
  3. make clear that the other document is definitive and this copy is for information only;
  4. say whether this use will track new versions of the other document;
  5. think about whether such changes could break this system;
  6. if necessary, negotiate constraints with whoever controls changes to the other document.

21.4 The four test questions

The note ends with questions to ask of a draft.

QuestionWhat a failure means
Does the spec decide, for any arbitrary object, whether the conformance term applies?The left-hand side is undefined
Could a program be written to test conformance?Nobody can build a checker, so arguments about conformance cannot be settled
Is conformance alone enough to make systems work as intended?Something necessary is unstated
Can important properties be proved from the state transition tables?Two correct implementations can still deadlock or disagree; Figure 21.3 should let one prove, say, that a reply never arrives unasked

The third is the easiest to overlook, because a specification can be perfectly decidable and still describe something that does not work, because the requirement that made it work was left in somebody’s head.

What happened since

  • The vocabulary became universal. The requirement keywords this note points at, from Bradner’s BCP 14 of March 1997, are now cited by nearly every protocol specification, and were refined by RFC 8174 in May 2017 to apply only when written in capitals.
  • Conformance terms became formal. Media types and namespace URIs are now the ordinary way to name what conforms. A namespace URI needs no registration: anyone who controls a URI can mint one.
  • Testability became a gate. The W3C Process requires a specification to show implementation experience before it can leave the Candidate Recommendation stage, close kin to the second test question.
  • The meaning-not-behaviour rule lost ground. HTML5 and its successors specify algorithms rather than meanings, deliberately, because interoperability on error handling mattered more than portability of the definition. Chapter 23 is where that choice is argued.

Questions

21.1 Pick a household standard, such as a plug or a paper size, and write its conformance term in one sentence.

21.2 Find an instruction in a manual or standard that tells you what to do, and rewrite it so that it states a meaning instead. What is lost?

21.3 The third test question asks whether conformance alone makes a system work. Give a case where a conforming implementation is nonetheless unusable.

21.4 A specification copies a definition from another. Apply the six-point checklist of section 21.3 to a case you know, and say which points were missed.
