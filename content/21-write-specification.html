The short version

A specification says exactly what counts as, for example, a conforming HTTP 1.1 server. It has to do two jobs: name the thing, and define it. Where it can, it should say what things mean rather than what software must do about them. And a program should be able to test whether something conforms.

The question

Write that an “x” on a cheque means the cheque is void, and a bank, an auditor and a court can all use the statement. Write that such a cheque should be rejected with a fatal error, and only one program has been described. This 1999 note, “a little motherhood and apple pie” in Berners-Lee’s words, is about that difference, and about a prior step: naming exactly what is being defined.

21.1 Name it before defining it

A technical specification defines something. The document must specify the thing being defined as well as give its definition: a “left hand side” as well as a “right hand side”. Both must be done quite precisely. The essentials of a specification, 1999

Is a file valid XML? Until the question names a term it has no answer: a “well-formed XML 1.0 document” and an “xml-schema-valid XML document” are different things, and a file can be the first without being the second. So the note insists that the thing defined gets a precise name, a conformance term; whatever meets the definition conforms. On the Web the thing is usually a language (symbols, rules for combining them, and what they mean) or a protocol (a language for messages plus constraints on their order).

A specification may define more than one term, such as a strictly conforming and a loosely conforming widget, but Berners-Lee warns against diluting the brand. Wherever possible, the note adds, conformance phrases should be grounded in the Web by being identified with a URI.

The name also governs the wording: one careless word can put a sentence outside the specification. If the term is “foo-valid document”, a sentence saying something “is invalid” can be assumed to apply to it. A sentence saying something “is wrong” or “produces an error” does not, unless the text explicitly ties it to the conformance term.

21.2 Say what the cheque means, not what to do with it

When defining a language, the note says, state what a construct means rather than what software should do when it meets one. Its example sets two versions of a cheque rule side by side:

“x” indicated that the check is void
is better than
“x” indicates that the check should be rejected with a fatal error. The essentials of a specification, 1999

The reason given:

You can tell people what something means, you can't tell them what to do about it, unless you are defining a protocol. The essentials of a specification, 1999

Typical behaviours may still be described, as non-normative explanation of intent. For protocols, the exception, he asks for constraints given as a state transition diagram or table.

HTML’s editors later broke this rule on purpose: they judged that browsers agreeing on what to do with a broken page mattered more than a definition of what the page means (Chapter 23).

21.3 Messages that bind people, and definitions borrowed from elsewhere

Two pieces of drafting advice follow. The first is about contracts:

When defining a message which in fact binds to human social entities, then this must be clear. You could end up in court explaining it if not. The essentials of a specification, 1999

Its sketch of the prose required:

The MMTP protocol defines the meaning of a message sent by or on behalf of a party herein referred to as the “debtor” to a party referred to as the “creditor”. The creditor is identified by the foo-email-address... The essentials of a specification, 1999

MMTP is invented for the example; section 20.2 argues the legal point at length.

The second is a checklist for borrowing. When part of a specification is deliberately made similar to another, the note asks the author to:

  1. say that the similarity has been noticed;
  2. say whether it is exact, and if not, where not and why;
  3. ideally, make clear that the other document is definitive and this copy is for information only;
  4. say whether this use will track new versions of the other document;
  5. think about whether such changes could break this system;
  6. if necessary, negotiate constraints with whoever controls changes to the other document.

HTML shows why the third point matters. W3C long published its own edition derived from the WHATWG’s text; the two drifted apart until, in May 2019, both agreed the WHATWG Living Standard is definitive (Chapter 23).

21.4 Four questions to ask of any draft

QuestionWhat a failure means
Given anything at all, does the spec say yes or no to whether the term applies?The left-hand side is undefined
Could a program be written to test conformance?Nobody can build a checker, so arguments about conformance cannot be settled
Is conformance alone enough to make systems work as intended?Something necessary is unstated
Can important properties be proved from the state transition tables?Two correct implementations can still deadlock or disagree; Figure 21.3 should let one prove, say, that a reply never arrives unasked

Berners-Lee calls it all folklore, learnt from Peggie Rimmer at CERN and from years of IEEE, IETF and W3C work. All four questions come back to the cheque: given any cheque at all, can anyone say for certain whether it is void?

What happened since

  • The vocabulary became standard. The requirement keywords this note points at, from Bradner’s BCP 14 of March 1997, are cited by most IETF and W3C specifications, and were refined by RFC 8174 in May 2017 to apply only when written in capitals.
  • Conformance terms became formal. Media types and namespace URIs are now the ordinary way to name what conforms.
  • Testability became a gate. The W3C Process requires a specification to show implementation experience before it can leave the Candidate Recommendation stage, close kin to the second test question.
  • The meaning-not-behaviour rule lost ground. The WHATWG’s HTML, from 2004, specifies detailed processing algorithms alongside meanings, deliberately, because interoperability on error handling mattered more than portability of the definition. Chapter 23 is where that choice is argued.

Questions

21.1 Pick a household standard, such as a plug or a paper size, and write its conformance term in one sentence.

21.2 Find an instruction in a manual or standard that tells you what to do, and rewrite it so that it states a meaning instead. What is lost?

21.3 The third test question asks whether conformance alone makes a system work. Give a case where a conforming implementation is nonetheless unusable.
