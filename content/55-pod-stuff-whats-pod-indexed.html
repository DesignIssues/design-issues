The question

Thirteen years after Chapter 54 proposed that storage become a commodity, this note from December 2022 asks the question that turns out to be harder than the protocol: given a space of your own, how should what is in it be arranged?

The answer begins by refusing to narrow the question:

What should be in your Solid Pod? Anything, of course. Any data in your life, across the data spectrum from public to private and everything shared with communities in between. Stuff in your Pod, 2022

55.2 The organising decision

The note's substantive claim is about what determines where something goes, and it is stated as a preference between two obvious candidates:

We do this by principally initially keying data by its RDFS Class, not by the particular app which wrote it. Stuff in your Pod, 2022

Filing by application is what every operating system does, and it recreates the silo inside the space that was meant to abolish it. Filing by kind means that two applications dealing in events find each other's events, which is the cross-linking Chapter 54 promised.

The expectation that makes this workable is stated immediately: the classes of data used by different applications are expected to overlap a lot. Most applications deal in people, events, places, messages and documents, which is Chapter 48's argument arriving as a filing system.

55.3 Why activities are kept together anyway

Having filed by kind, the note immediately allows an exception, and the reason is about permissions rather than about data:

Keeping stuff related to the same activity together in a pod, even though it is of many different types, is valuable, because the same access and trust conditions apply and it is much simpler to get those right. Stuff in your Pod, 2022

This is the most practical sentence in Volume VI. Access control is applied to places, and people reason about places. If everything touching one matter lives together, a person can decide once who may see it. If it is scattered by type, they must make the same decision many times and will eventually get one wrong.

The two principles pull against each other, and the note does not pretend otherwise. Filing by kind makes data findable across applications. Filing by activity makes permissions comprehensible to the person granting them.

55.4 Who configures the layout

The resolution offered is to let applications arrange things:

To be extensible across these dimensions, we must give applications the power to configure this data in the users pod in a way which meets these requirements, and also works well for that particular app. Stuff in your Pod, 2022

Applications decide the arrangement, common patterns emerge because the classes overlap, and the mechanism for describing where new data should go is the subject of Chapter 57. That chapter's third technology exists precisely because this one leaves the question open.

55.5 Provenance as a first-class concern

One sentence names an obligation that the rest of Volume VI mostly assumes:

We track and use its provenance carefully and carefully control is access, to be able to model the way we really trust data, people and bots in this very rich, varied, and valuable system. Stuff in your Pod, 2022

Two things are worth noticing. Trust is described as something to be modelled as it really is rather than as a permission flag, which is Chapter 66's argument. And bots are listed alongside people and data as things one trusts, in a note written in 2022, before that became the ordinary way to talk.

What happened since

  • Type indexes became the mechanism. Solid's type index, which maps a class to the places instances of it are kept, is section 55.2 implemented, and it is how applications find each other's data in a pod.
  • The tension was not resolved. Arranging by kind and arranging by activity remain in conflict, and applications in practice do both, which is why finding things in a pod is still harder than it should be.
  • Immutability was put to work elsewhere. Content addressing and verifiable credentials both exploit the property this note names, storing what never changes by what it contains rather than where it sits.
  • The bots arrived. Reasoning about what an automated agent may read from a personal store became an urgent question within two years of this note, and is the subject of Chapters 92 to 96.
