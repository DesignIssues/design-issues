Chapter 69

Client-Side Certificates

2015

Source Client-Side Certificates, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2015-03-28. read the original

The question

The last of the four notes of March 2015 asks why the Web authenticates one side of a conversation well and the other side badly. Berners-Lee grants the case for anonymity before making his argument:

When you are browsing the news and interacting with incoming information, you may be happy not to be identified or even keen to be anonymous. There are times, though, when you want to be well identified yourself, whether moving money around at the bank or chatting with friends. Web Security - Client side certs, 2015

The question is why, in those cases, we still use passwords.

69.2 Six things a browser would have to do

The note is unusual in the collection for being a feature specification. The list is short and the absence of any of it explains why the mechanism failed.

FunctionWhat it means for a person
Create a key pair and keep it locallyOn the laptop, phone or a hardware device
Choose which identity to use for a site, and remember itWith enough shown to tell one from another
Show who you are logged in as, near the addressBeside where the site’s own identity is shown
Log outDrop the credentials, optionally choose another identity
Manage those preferencesEditable when you get muddled
Back up and synchronise keys, selectivelyMarked for extra credit; some keys should never leave one machine

The third is the one that reveals the asymmetry most sharply. A browser shows who the site is, prominently, and shows nothing about who you are being. The conversation is displayed from one side only.

The complaint about how identities are presented is specific and is the kind of detail that decides whether a feature is usable:

Make sure the certificates are labelled in a way that makes them distinct -- not all just labelled with my name (duh!). Web Security - Client side certs, 2015

69.3 The refusal

The note ends with a section titled with two words, and it is directed at a habit rather than at a technology:

Many of the things which I hear people wish for, mentioned in this article or not, are often put out into the conversation but with a sigh and the caveat that "It would be nice, but that's not how browsers work". Web Security - Client side certs, 2015

His answer is an observation about the material rather than an appeal:

The bowsers are code, in many cases open source code. They are being changed all the time. Unlike a decade ago, they are also being upgraded all the time, because of the need for security-related bug fixes, and so a new idea can be tried out in browsers really fast. Web Security - Client side certs, 2015

The closing instruction is four words: let us just change it. Coming from someone who had spent thirty years unable to change how browsers work, it is either optimism or exasperation, and possibly both.

69.4 Why this closes the set

The four notes together make one argument. Chapter 66 says the system models one kind of trust. Chapter 67 says it marks security in the wrong place. Chapter 68 says it draws the party boundary in the wrong place. This one says it authenticates the wrong side.

Each is a case of the same thing: the machine’s model of a social arrangement is narrower than the arrangement, and the narrowing is invisible because it has been there since the beginning.

What happened since

  • The mechanism was replaced rather than fixed. WebAuthn, a W3C Recommendation of March 2019, gives exactly the properties of the right column of Figure 69.1: a key generated locally, never sent, never stored by the other party.
  • The interface requirements were met. Passkeys, from 2022, supply the creation, selection, synchronisation and per-site memory that section 69.2 lists, with the operating system rather than the browser doing most of it.
  • The key element the note asks for is still missing. No browser shows, beside the site’s identity, which identity you are currently presenting. The conversation is still displayed from one side.
  • The old mechanism was removed. The element for generating a key pair in a page, which the note mentions, was removed from browsers around 2019, before its replacement was widely usable.

Connections
