Chapter 16

Evolvability

1998

Source Evolvability, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 1998-05-01. read the original

The short version

Between 1994 and 1998, web pages gained tables, colours, frames and a dozen other features, none of which anybody had agreed on. Companies simply invented them, because of a rule inherited from electronic mail: if you meet something you do not understand, ignore it.

That rule is why the Web grew so fast. It is also why nothing could ever be relied on. This note describes the cycle that resulted, explains why it had to end, and sets out the thought experiment its author used to test every design he made.

The question

This is the keystone of Volume III and the longest note in it. Berners-Lee wrote it in March 1998, following a keynote at the seventh World Wide Web conference, and the status line says it is an incomplete first draft. It is also the note where the Consortium’s founding mandate gets examined:

The World Wide Web Consortium was founded in 1994 on the mandate to lead the Evolution of the Web while maintaining its Interoperability as a universal space. Evolvability, 1998

Interoperability was well understood. Evolvability was a word without a technique, and this note is the attempt to give it one.

16.1 The old rule, and what it bought

The Web inherited one rule from Internet mail, and Berners-Lee states it as he found it:

A rule explicit or implcit in all the email-like Internet protocols has always been that if you found a mail header (or something) which you did not understand, you should ignore it. Evolvability, 1998

The companion note of Chapter 17, written with Dan Connolly in the same year, states it more tersely still: if you find a language element you do not understand, ignore it.

He calls this free extension, records that he adopted it for HTML and used it constantly himself, and is clear that it is why HTML grew as fast as it did. He is equally clear about the bill:

It has its advatage of rapid prototyping and incremental deployment, and the disadvantage of ambiguity, confusion, and an inability to add a mandatory feature to an existing protocol. Evolvability, 1998

That last clause is the one that matters. Under free extension there is no way to say that something must be understood, which means no feature can ever be relied upon.

16.2 The HTML cycle

The note’s best passage is an anthropology of how a standard actually got made between 1994 and 1998. It is a cycle with a motor, and the motor is the tension between wanting to beat a competitor and needing to interoperate with one.

The worked example is the table tag. A browser meeting one had no way to know which table extension it was, so the situation could become ambiguous, and if the table mattered for safe interpretation, the server would never learn whether it had been handled: an unaware client would ignore it blithely.

16.3 The other extreme, and why it also fails

The note then looks at how extension works in distributed object systems, where interfaces are declared in machine-processable form and checked at build time. The verdict is even-handed:

With well-defined interfaces, either everything works, or nothing. Evolvability, 1998

The advantage is certainty. There is no wondering whether a table is being displayed: if the module was called, it is there. The disadvantage is that changing an interface forces a change on the other side, which is why such systems never spread, in Berners-Lee’s phrase, in a web-like or virus-like manner.

The requirement is therefore stated as a demand for both:

One must be able to freely extend a language, but do so with an extension language which is itself well defined. Evolvability, 1998

That sentence is the design brief for XML namespaces, and Chapter 17 is the specification of it.

16.4 Partial understanding

The mechanism that replaces free extension is partial understanding, and the note is careful to define it better than the word “optional” can. Marking a section optional in a document fails because optionality is subjective: the same invoice that an accounts system may strip down to a valid invoice, a warehouse may strip down to a delivery note, and different parts are dispensable in each case.

The precise version is a transformation. A processor needs to convert a document containing things it does not understand into one it does understand, preserving validity in some sense. Berners-Lee notes that the transformation language could range from the crudest to the Turing complete, and applies the rule from Chapter 1:

When expressing something, use the least powerful language you can. Evolvability, 1998

He gives the smallest possible case, a stream editor deletion rule, and observes that if the rule is allowed then the deleted element was optional. The section then breaks off mid-argument, marked to be continued. It never was.

16.5 The test of independent invention

The most memorable part of the note is a thought experiment that Chapter 1 introduced in one line and that gets its full form here.

Suppose another group, unknown to you, designs the same sort of thing. Being as smart as you, they make the same important architectural decisions, since those follow from logic. But they make every arbitrary decision differently. Berners-Lee’s list of arbitrary decisions is the part people remember: they complement bit 7, they drive on the other side of the road, they use red buoys to starboard, and 575 lines per screen on their televisions.

The reason the Web survives the meeting is the third axiom of Chapter 2: URI space is universal but need not be unique, so the rival space can be folded in. The counterfactual is the sharpest sentence in the note. Had the Web required all servers to synchronise through one master lock server, the Mesh would have required one too, and the encounter would have failed.

The cost of failing is not hypothetical, and the examples are the reason the section persuades:

Sweden actually changed from driving on the left to driving on the right. Evolvability, 1998
The test of independent invention is not just a thought experiment, it is happening all the time. Evolvability, 1998

16.6 Two assumptions

The note closes by deriving its requirements from two premises stated as plainly as possible. The first is that we will be smarter in the future, which yields the requirement that version 4 software read a version 5 file. The second is more unusual, and he flags the difficulty:

The philosophical assumption that we may not be smarter than everyone else (a huge step for some!) leads us to realise that others will have gret ideas too, and will independently invent the same things. Evolvability, 1998

Humility, here, is a technical requirement.

What happened since

  • The mechanism shipped and was then declined. XML Namespaces became a W3C Recommendation in January 1999. HTML5, whose first Recommendation came in October 2014, does not use them, which is the fork described in Chapter 23.
  • Partial understanding was solved by other means. Versioned wire formats such as Protocol Buffers, first released publicly in July 2008, make forward compatibility a property of the encoding: unknown fields are preserved rather than merely ignored, which is closer to what section 16.4 asked for than anything the Web adopted.
  • The cycle did not stop. Vendor-prefixed CSS properties through the late 2000s reproduced it exactly, ending the same way, in standardisation and the deprecation of the prefixes.
  • The test was passed again, more than once. Every successful bridging of two independently designed systems since, from character encodings converging on Unicode to the coexistence of competing package registries behind one client, is an instance of the third outcome in Figure 16.2.

Connections

The test of independent invention and the principle of least power are both stated in Chapter 1, and the axiom that makes the Web pass the test is Chapter 2. The namespace requirements this note calls for are Chapter 17, and the mandatory flag it says is missing is Chapter 18. The fork that ended the namespace approach for HTML is Chapter 23.

Questions 4

16.1 Free extension makes it impossible to add a mandatory feature. Explain why, using the table tag example, and say what a publisher can never learn.

16.2 Section 16.4 rejects “optional” as subjective and replaces it with a transformation. Give one document and two processors for which different parts are dispensable.

16.3 Apply the test of independent invention to a system you work on. Name one arbitrary decision in it and say what happens when it meets a twin that chose the opposite.

16.4 The Web passes the test because URI space need not be unique. Name a part of the modern Web that would fail it, and say whether the failure is structural or contingent.
