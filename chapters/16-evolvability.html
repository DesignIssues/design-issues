Chapter 16

Evolvability

1998

Source Evolvability, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 1998-05-01. read the original

The short version

Between 1994 and 1998, web pages gained tables and many other features, added before anyone had agreed on them. Companies simply invented them, protected by a rule inherited from electronic mail: anything not understood is to be ignored rather than rejected.

That rule is why the Web grew so fast. It is also why no feature could ever be made mandatory. The note traces the cycle that resulted, why it had to end, and the thought experiment Berners-Lee tried to make the Web pass.

The question

Someone in a group saves a shared document with version 5 of a word processor; a colleague’s version 4 refuses to open it, and everyone must upgrade at once. This note, dated March 1998 and the basis of Berners-Lee’s keynote at the seventh World Wide Web conference (WWW7), asks how a language can change without that flag day, and without the chaos HTML had fallen into. It starts from the founding mandate of the World Wide Web Consortium (W3C), the body that coordinates the Web’s standards:

The World Wide Web Consortium was founded in 1994 on the mandate to lead the Evolution of the Web while maintaining its Interoperability as a universal space. Evolvability, 1998

Interoperability was well understood. Evolvability was a word without a technique, and the note distrusts anyone claiming one: of technologies dubbed “futureproof” it says, “I expect you the reader to share my cynicism when encountering any such claim.”

16.1 The old rule, and what it bought

The Web inherited one rule from Internet mail. A mail header is one of the labelled lines, such as From: or Subject:, at the top of a message, and Berners-Lee states the rule as he found it:

A rule explicit or implcit in all the email-like Internet protocols has always been that if you found a mail header (or something) which you did not understand, you should ignore it. Evolvability, 1998

He calls this free extension and credits it with HTML’s fast growth. He is equally clear about the bill:

It has its advatage of rapid prototyping and incremental deployment, and the disadvantage of ambiguity, confusion, and an inability to add a mandatory feature to an existing protocol. Evolvability, 1998

Nothing can be marked as having to be understood, so no feature can be relied upon.

16.2 The HTML cycle

Between 1994 and 1998 HTML grew as browser makers added new tags (a tag is the bracketed label, such as <table>, that marks out part of a page). Anyone could think up an extension, and free extension let it ship. Successful features became product differentiators, their originators “loth to discuss the technology with the competition”, until rivals shipped equivalents and engineers everywhere were writing three or four versions of the same thing. Then the companies met at the W3C, agreed a common design, and went away “making public promises to implement it”, while their engineers were pressed to start the next cycle.

The worked example is the table tag (step 3 of Figure 16.1). Rival browsers each added tables their own way, so a browser meeting <table> could not tell which the author meant; and a browser that did not know the tag silently ignored it, so the server never learned whether a table that mattered had been handled.

One sign that the cycle had to end: MathML became a W3C Recommendation in April 1998, yet there was no defined way to combine it with HTML: “MathML was already waiting for XML namespaces.”

16.3 The other extreme, and why it also fails

At the other extreme are distributed object systems, such as remote procedure call (RPC), where every interface is declared in machine-readable form and checked when the system is compiled. A program that has called the table module need not wonder whether a table is being displayed. The catch:

With well-defined interfaces, either everything works, or nothing. Evolvability, 1998

Change an interface and the other side must change too, which is why such systems never spread, in Berners-Lee’s phrase, in a web-like or virus-like manner. The requirement is both:

One must be able to freely extend a language, but do so with an extension language which is itself well defined. Evolvability, 1998

That sentence is the design brief for XML namespaces, a way of labelling each set of tags with the URI of the vocabulary it comes from. Chapter 17 specifies them.

16.4 Reading half a document

Paper invoices once came as coloured copies (packing list, restocking sheet, invoice, delivery note), each department reading its own part. Berners-Lee adds:

I don't understand half the information in the tax form - but I know enough to know what applies to me. Evolvability, 1998

The note’s test case is an electronic invoice. Most of it follows a well-known invoice vocabulary, but each item also carries a part description in the supplier’s own private vocabulary. An invoice program should be able to process it without understanding the part description. The obvious fix, marking that part “optional”, fails because optionality depends on who is reading.

Conversion rules could be written in anything from the crudest language to one that is Turing complete, able to express any computation. Berners-Lee applies the rule from Chapter 1:

When expressing something, use the least powerful language you can. Evolvability, 1998

His smallest case is a one-line rule that says, in effect, delete this part. If the rule is allowed, the deleted part was optional. The section then breaks off, “to be continued”.

16.5 The test of independent invention

Suppose another group, unknown to the first, designs the same thing. Being just as smart, they make the same important architectural decisions, since those follow from logic.

But they make every arbitrary decision differently: every convention that could have gone either way but must match on both sides. In Berners-Lee’s list, they drive on the other side, flip one bit of every character code, put red buoys on the other side of the channel, and use a different number of lines on their television screens. Then the two systems meet.

The Web survives the meeting because of the third axiom of Chapter 2: URI space is universal but need not be unique, so the rival space can be folded in (Figure 16.4).

Without the test, worldwide interoperability needs either a worldwide committee designing top down before anyone starts, or one system to be dropped. The first works for a new idea, the note says, but not for practices centuries old: the library community has had endless trouble agreeing on a single library card format, the MARC record. As for the second, when Europe abandoned its ISO networking protocols (in the late 1980s, as the note dates it) because they could not interwork with the Internet, much work was lost, and problems Europe had solved, “including network addresses of more than 32 bits”, had to be solved again at great cost (an IPv4 address has 32). Arbitrary choices can be reversed, but at national cost:

Sweden actually changed from driving on the left to driving on the right. Evolvability, 1998

It did so on 3 September 1967, Dagen H. The note’s verdict: “The test of independent invention is not just a thought experiment, it is happening all the time.”

16.6 Smarter later, and not the smartest

The note derives its requirements from two premises. The first, that we will be smarter in the future, demands that version 4 read the version 5 file, at least in part, with no flag day. The second he flags as hard:

The philosophical assumption that we may not be smarter than everyone else (a huge step for some!) leads us to realise that others will have gret ideas too, and will independently invent the same things. Evolvability, 1998

That demands moving between parallel versions invented independently: the test of section 16.5.

16.7 From a bare name to a logic: six kinds of schema

A schema is the document that describes a language; an XML document points to its schema by URI. The note ranks six kinds by how much a machine can do with them.

Even the bottom step lets software check whether it knows the language, which is all most RPC systems do. The second reflects the note’s view that what a document commits to must in the end be defined in human-readable legal language. The fourth is the one section 16.4 was waiting for:

All you need to do is to mark in the schema of a new version of the language which elements of the langauge can be ignored if you don't understand them. Evolvability, 1998

Old software can then filter a new document down to one it understands: the elusive version 2 to version 1 conversion. A Turing-complete converter can do more, but nobody can see its rules, so it must be trusted. What excites him is the top step: stating, in machine-processable form, how terms in one language relate to terms in another. His example is a calendar language whose schema declares its INDIVIDUAL a superset of the HR department’s concept of employee. Links like that let inference “conclude things which we knew as whole but no one person knew”; that is the programme of RDF and the Semantic Web.

The note ends with Lao-Tse, whom it had already cited for least power: “Usefulness from what is not there.”

What happened since

  • The mechanism shipped and was then declined. XML Namespaces became a W3C Recommendation in January 1999. HTML5 (a Recommendation in October 2014) does not use them: the fork of Chapter 23.
  • Partial understanding was solved by other means. Versioned wire formats such as Protocol Buffers (public from 2008) make forward compatibility a property of the encoding: unknown fields are kept rather than merely ignored (proto3 at first discarded them, until version 3.5 in 2017). That is closer to what section 16.4 asked for than anything the Web adopted.
  • The cycle did not stop. Vendor-prefixed CSS properties (-webkit-, -moz-), common from the mid-2000s to the early 2010s, repeated steps 1 to 3 of the cycle, until browser makers moved experimental features behind settings flags instead, as Chrome’s Blink engine did from its launch in 2013.

Connections
