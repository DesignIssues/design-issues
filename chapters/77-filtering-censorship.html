Chapter 77

Filtering and censorship

1997

Source Filtering and censorship, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 1997-12-19. read the original

The short version

Governments wanted one rule for what counts as indecent. Berners-Lee argued instead for letting anyone publish ratings and letting each reader choose whose ratings to trust. The technology should provide the mechanism and leave the policy to society.

The question

From 1994, governments worried about what children might find online were contemplating laws against transmitting “indecent” material. In the United States one passed, the Communications Decency Act of 1996, and in June 1997 the Supreme Court struck down its indecency provisions in Reno v. ACLU. Berners-Lee wrote this note that December. The question: who should decide what a reader is shown, a government or the reader?

77.1 Does he regret what the Web is used for?

People have asked whether I regret that the Web has been used for some uses, but I have to reply that if somehow it had been built to control the material which was placed in it, then that would be the technology controlling society, rather than the other way around as it should be. Filtering and Censorship, 1997

Paper and ink, he notes, can serve truth or lies; metal makes ploughshares or swords. Some fear society is not strong enough to be trusted with such a tool. He disagrees, and marks it as belief: in the western developed world, at least, democratic process checks governments and the courts check criminals.

77.2 Three things wrong with the legislation

First, indecency, which society judges subjectively and in many places, was being enforced as one central concept. Second, the problem was children reading such material, yet the rule addressed its transmission. Third, “transmission” swept in intermediate parties who neither wrote nor chose the content. Behind the first objection is a claim about how the Web is built:

The Web works as a decentralized system, with no hierarchical or other structure to force society into a shape imposed by technology. This works. Filtering and Censorship, 1997

Centralising such an idea, he adds, would stop the Web mirroring society accurately.

77.3 Quality, made subjective on purpose

The underlying problem is more general than indecency:

The basic problem being addressed was that of subjective information "quality". Filtering and Censorship, 1997

It is, he says, the same problem newcomers to the Web report when a search engine returns too much “junk”. Protecting children and filtering search results are one problem, and it cannot be solved centrally because quality cannot be defined centrally:

It is unreasonable to ask for information delivered from the web to be of consistently high "quality" if you can't define what "quality" is. There is a need, then, to be able to represent "quality" in a completely subjective way. Filtering and Censorship, 1997

The answer, PICS, took three documents.

77.4 Three documents in place of one rule

PICS, the Platform for Internet Content Selection, was a W3C standard for attaching ratings to Web pages. Berners-Lee says it was meant to show that readers could hold their own notion of quality without a government enforcing one, and states the stake: “Politically, PICS is a system necessary for the preservation of free speech on the Internet.” It needed three kinds of document:

  • a rating system defines a scale;
  • a label places a page on that scale;
  • a profile says which scales, and which levels, a given person will accept.

A parents’ group might rate a page 3 for violence; a parent who accepts up to 2 never sees it, and a neighbour who accepts 4 does.

Passing a profile to family, organisation or friend lets a community form around a shared standard. How large that community should be is the most durable point in the note:

Agreement over a large community enhances global harmony, but threatens diversity. Agreement over a small community does the reverse. Filtering and Censorship, 1997

The design must allow both; Chapter 87 returns to this.

77.5 Engineers know first

The last part answers a charge that PICS should be suppressed because governments might abuse it. Berners-Lee notes the stronger version, that the whole scheme was a government plot to promote censorship, and denies it: not in the idea, the funding or the intent. His general answer is one he gave closing the first International World Wide Web conference, to “a mere 350” enthusiasts in an auditorium at CERN, whose particle physicists “trace their roots through nuclear physics”:

I firmly believe it is the task of scientists and technologists to be aware of and responsible for the social implications of their work. This cannot just be left to "professional socially responsible people", as each engineer and scientist is often best aware of the potential of the work. Filtering and Censorship, 1997

Engineers are not wiser, but they know sooner. So he asks of his own work what can be asked of nuclear fission or firearms: is there anything about PICS, or metadata in general, “which makes it more of a danger than a boon”?

77.6 Mechanism, not policy

His answer is no, because responsibility does not mean building policy into the protocol. Protocol specifications, Berners-Lee writes, enable but do not enforce what software will do, so the technology can leave policy to others, though PICS by its nature leans towards diversity and free speech.

Metadata, information about information, enables every kind of use at once:

  • finding information
  • talking about information
  • making laws about information
  • breaking laws about information

Limiting those uses belongs to the democratic process, not to the metadata system. Engineers can explain it to lawmakers, as this note does. And metadata, he ends, is “the start of a machine-understandable web”, the subject of Chapter 30. “We must understand it as we build it.”

What happened since

  • The law went his way; the tool did not. The note expected PICS to succeed in reducing the risk of government censorship. Instead civil libertarians attacked PICS itself as a means of censorship, among them Lawrence Lessig in Wired in 1997. It was never widely adopted, and W3C superseded it with POWDER in September 2009.
  • Responsibility moved to the platforms, not to readers. The EU’s Digital Services Act (2022) and the UK’s Online Safety Act (2023) require platforms to act against illegal content and to protect children.
  • The transferable profile arrived only in niches. Ad-blocker filter lists such as EasyList (mid-2000s) and Bluesky’s labelling services (2024) let a user take a standard from someone trusted.
