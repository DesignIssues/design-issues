Chapter 77

Filtering and censorship

1997

Source Filtering and censorship, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 1997-12-19. read the original

The question

Begun in December 1997, when governments faced with the question of what children might read were contemplating legislation against the transmission of indecent material. The note is Berners-Lee’s defence of an alternative, and also his answer to people who asked whether he regretted what the Web was used for.

77.1 The answer to the question he is always asked

People have asked whether I regret that the Web has been used for some uses, but I have to reply that if somehow it had been built to control the material which was placed in it, then that would be the technology controlling society, rather than the other way around as it should be. Filtering and Censorship, 1997

The answer is not that the uses do not matter. It is about which of the two things should be subordinate. He then states the opposing view fairly, in the form someone might actually hold it: that our society is not strong enough to be trusted with a powerful information system, and that the pain might exceed the gain. His reply is a belief rather than a proof, and he marks it as one: that the democratic process and the judicial process are sufficient to defend the health of an evolving society.

77.2 Four things wrong with the legislation

The objections are specific, and each is a different kind of error.

ObjectionThe error
Indecency was being enforced as one central conceptA subjective, distributed judgement made singular
The problem was reading, and the rule addressed transmissionThe wrong act was targeted
Transmission included parties with no editorial roleResponsibility placed where there is no control
The structure was imposed by technology on societyThe relation of the two reversed

The first is developed into an architectural claim:

The Web works as a decentralized system, with no hierarchical or other structure to force society into a shape imposed by technology. This works. Centralization of such an idea would [prevent the Web from being an accurate mirror of society itself. Filtering and Censorship, 1997

77.3 Quality, made subjective on purpose

The underlying problem is identified as something more general than indecency, and the connection Berners-Lee draws is unexpected:

The basic problem being addressed was that of subjective information "quality". This is the same problem reported by newcomers to teh web who find (typically after a search engine search) too much "junk". Filtering and Censorship, 1997

Protecting children and filtering search results are the same problem, and it cannot be solved centrally because quality cannot be defined centrally:

It is unreasonable to ask for information delivered from the web to be of consistently high "quality" if you can't define what "quality" is. There is a need, then, to be able to represent "quality" in a completely subjective way. Filtering and Censorship, 1997

77.4 The three documents

The sentence about community size is the most durable thing in the note. Agreement at scale buys harmony and costs diversity; agreement in small groups does the reverse. Neither is right, and the design must allow both. That is Chapter 87’s argument, arriving eighteen months early.

77.5 On the responsibility of the people who build things

The last section answers a charge that the technology should be suppressed because governments might abuse it. Berners-Lee notes the stronger version, that the whole scheme was a plot to promote censorship, and denies it flatly as to idea, funding and intent.

Then the general answer, which is the reason this chapter belongs in a book about architecture:

I firmly believe it is the task of scientists and technologists to be aware of and responsible for the social implications of their work. This cannot just be left to "professional socially responsible people", as each engineer and scientist is often best aware of the potential of the work. Filtering and Censorship, 1997

The argument is not that engineers are wiser but that they know sooner. The person who built a thing understands what it could be used for before anybody else is in a position to ask.

What happened since

  • The legislation was struck down and the mechanism failed too. The American indecency provisions were held unconstitutional in 1997, and the labelling system this note defends was never widely adopted and was formally obsoleted.
  • Filtering was centralised after all. What arrived instead was platform moderation and national blocking, both of which are the single central definition of acceptability that section 77.2 objects to.
  • Subjective quality became ranking. The junk problem was answered by search and feed ranking, which is a profile chosen by the platform rather than by the reader.
  • The transferable profile did not arrive. Being able to adopt someone else’s standards, which the note calls essential for building communities of common trust, is still unavailable in any mainstream system.
