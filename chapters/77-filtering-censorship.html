Chapter 77

Filtering and censorship

1997

Source Filtering and censorship, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 1997-12-19. read the original

The short version

Governments wanted one rule for what counts as indecent. Berners-Lee argued instead for letting anyone publish ratings and letting each reader choose whose ratings to trust. The technology should provide the mechanism and leave the policy to society.

The question

Written in December 1997, looking back at a threat that arose from 1994 onwards, when governments worried about what children might read were contemplating laws against transmitting “indecent” material. It defends an alternative, and answers those who asked if Berners-Lee regretted the Web’s uses.

77.1 The answer to the question he is always asked

People have asked whether I regret that the Web has been used for some uses, but I have to reply that if somehow it had been built to control the material which was placed in it, then that would be the technology controlling society, rather than the other way around as it should be. Filtering and Censorship, 1997

The question is which of technology and society should shape the other. He states the opposing view fairly: that our society is not strong enough to be trusted with a powerful information system, and that the pain might exceed the gain. His reply is a belief, and he marks it as one: that the democratic process and the judicial process are sufficient to defend the health of an evolving society.

77.2 Three things wrong with the legislation

ObjectionThe error
Indecency was being enforced as one central conceptA subjective, distributed judgement made singular
The problem was reading, and the rule addressed transmissionThe wrong act was targeted
Transmission included parties who neither wrote nor chose the contentResponsibility placed where there is no control

The first objection is developed into an architectural claim:

The Web works as a decentralized system, with no hierarchical or other structure to force society into a shape imposed by technology. This works. Centralization of such an idea would [prevent the Web from being an accurate mirror of society itself. Filtering and Censorship, 1997

77.3 Quality, made subjective on purpose

The underlying problem is more general than indecency, and the connection Berners-Lee draws is unexpected:

The basic problem being addressed was that of subjective information "quality". This is the same problem reported by newcomers to teh web who find (typically after a search engine search) too much "junk". Filtering and Censorship, 1997

Protecting children and filtering search results are the same problem, and it cannot be solved centrally because quality cannot be defined centrally:

It is unreasonable to ask for information delivered from the web to be of consistently high "quality" if you can't define what "quality" is. There is a need, then, to be able to represent "quality" in a completely subjective way. Filtering and Censorship, 1997

77.4 The three documents

The alternative was PICS, the Platform for Internet Content Selection, a W3C standard for attaching ratings to Web pages. Berners-Lee says it was aimed at showing that individuals could obtain their own subjective notion of quality without a government enforcing a centralised one. It needed three kinds of document. A rating system defines a scale. A label places something on that scale. A profile says which scales, and which levels, a given person will accept. For example, a parents’ group publishes a violence scale from 0 to 4; a reviewer labels page X “violence 3”; a parent’s profile allows up to 2, so the browser hides page X.

Being able to pass a profile to someone else, whether family, organisation or friend, is what lets a community form around a shared standard. How large that community should be is the most durable point in the note:

Agreement over a large community enhances global harmony, but threatens diversity. Agreement over a small community does the reverse. Filtering and Censorship, 1997

The design must allow both, as Chapter 87 argues later.

77.5 On the responsibility of the people who build things

The last part answers a charge that PICS should be suppressed because governments might abuse it. Berners-Lee notes the stronger version, that the whole scheme was a plot to promote censorship, and denies it flatly as to idea, funding and intent. Then the general answer:

I firmly believe it is the task of scientists and technologists to be aware of and responsible for the social implications of their work. This cannot just be left to "professional socially responsible people", as each engineer and scientist is often best aware of the potential of the work. Filtering and Censorship, 1997

The argument is not that engineers are wiser but that they know sooner.

77.6 Mechanism, not policy

Responsibility does not mean building policy into the protocol. Protocol specifications, Berners-Lee writes, enable but do not enforce what software will do, so the technology can leave policy to others, though PICS by its nature leans towards diversity and free speech.

Metadata, information about information, enables every kind of use at once:

  • finding information
  • talking about information
  • making laws about information
  • breaking laws about information

Limiting those uses belongs to the democratic process, not to the metadata system. Engineers can explain to lawmakers what the technology does, as this note attempts.

What happened since

  • The law fell, and so did PICS. The American indecency provisions were held unconstitutional in June 1997 (Reno v. ACLU). PICS was never widely adopted, and W3C superseded it with POWDER, a Recommendation of September 2009.
  • Filtering was centralised after all. What arrived instead was platform moderation and national blocking, a few central definitions of acceptability, chosen by platforms and states rather than readers.
  • Subjective quality became ranking. Search and feed ranking answered the junk problem, with a profile chosen by the platform, not the reader.
  • The transferable profile arrived only in niches. Ad-blocker filter-list subscriptions such as EasyList (from the mid-2000s) and Bluesky’s subscribable labelling services (2024) let a user take a standard from someone they trust.
