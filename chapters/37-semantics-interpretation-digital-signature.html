Chapter 37

Semantics and Interpretation (and digital signature)

1999

Source Semantics and Interpretation (and digital signature), a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 1999-12-01. read the original

The short version

A digital signature is a mark that only the holder of a secret key can make, and that anyone can check against the matching public key. A signed document can name other keys, so one key can vouch for the next.

So a computer can follow a chain of keys vouching for keys without knowing who anybody is. Berners-Lee’s advice is to let the machine run that chain on its own, and to connect it to people and law at only a few edges.

Documents have meaning; signatures only carry trust.

The question

What does a digital signature mean? The note calls this “the much-asked question”, and argues that it is the wrong one:

Documents have meaning. Signatures by themselves do not. Interpretation and Semantics on the Semantic Web, 1999

The evidence is four rules that, in Berners-Lee’s words, “can happily run controlling our web site”, and a warning that every attempt to tie those rules to people at each step leads into what he calls rat holes. The note was written in 1999 (its header says 1998), and apologises that its terms may not match philosophy’s.

37.1 A cheque the bank never has to understand

The system, Berners-Lee argues, should form a loop that closes on itself. Definitions are given in terms of other things in the system, and the system reaches outside only at a few places: the physical delivery of goods, or the presentation of a document to a person for signature.

His first example is a cheque, defined only as something which, fed into the bank’s black box, makes it do a certain thing. Dollars and transfers can then be defined in terms of the cheque, and trade can run on electronic currency, invoices and delivery notes, falling back on a paper cheque only when necessary. The analogy he then reaches for is monetary too:

This is a similar relationship with reality that coins originally had with gold, and bills with coin. (A UK pound used to read “I promise to pay the bearer on demand the sum of one Pound signed, signed: Bank of England”). From then on a pound note became what people thought of as a pound, and the notion of what exactly the “sum of one pound” was originally defined by becomes irrelevant and the paper money is self-sufficient. Interpretation and Semantics on the Semantic Web, 1999

Bank of England notes still carry that promise, although the pound stopped being convertible to gold in 1931: the words outlived the thing they once referred to.

Why touch reality at all

He gives three reasons:

  1. an interface is needed, because that is how the system will work, at least at first;
  2. the social system has legislative backing and public understanding worth exploiting;
  3. it works, so change should be gradual.

None claims the existing definitions are fundamental or beautiful; many existing systems, he admits, are “really crufty”.

37.2 Two loops, and the mistake of joining them

The worked example is access control on the W3C’s own member web site, where Hugo is deemed responsible for deciding who has member access.

Of the left column he says: “This little system can happily run controlling our web site.” As a loop, it needs no person:

His diagnosis (SW is the Semantic Web):

Security has always been plagued by people trying to connect the SW steps (such as a-d) at every stage to the social machine (A-D). Interpretation and Semantics on the Semantic Web, 1999

Joining them at rung b means identifying a person with a key. That brings in a directory system (he names X.500, a planned worldwide directory standard) that is not part of the trust loop, becomes a security hole and adds unnecessary third parties, and it raises endless questions about what identity is.

Is key K3 Hugo’s, or the webmaster’s? Before that is settled, the argument turns to whether Hugo the person and Hugo the webmaster believe different things about who is a member: “maybe Hugo does not have to but in his webmaster role he does! These are rat holes.” People, he adds, do not simply believe things; they trust certain sources for certain purposes.

He suggests a separate word, “interpretation”, for the mapping between the machine world and the real one. In practice, a signature specification should give only the link between a signature and a string of bits, and a semantic web toolkit (the software for writing rules) only a language for writing a–d. Neither should try to link a–d to A–D.

37.3 When the list becomes the definition

First the server is installed with its signature-based access rules; then other things are related to it, such as giving invited experts keys on a given list. From then on the direction of definition reverses:

The semantic web becomes the definitive machine, and we just have rules at the edges about how it related to things like membership payments. An invited expert becomes defined as someone whose key is on a given list. Interpretation and Semantics on the Semantic Web, 1999

The system can then work without anyone answering the questions “‘invited’ in what sense? by whom?” or “Does this mean an invitation which has been accepted?” Next comes a warning that reaches past signatures:

We must be careful not to confuse what is said with where it is stored Interpretation and Semantics on the Semantic Web, 1999

Four rules define the access machine. Sent in an HTTP request or etched in marble, to take two of the note’s examples, they say the same thing, so the design must not care where they are kept.

37.4 Where a signature’s meaning lies

His own answer is that the semantics lie in the whole loop a–d, which must eventually be tied down to a key. Asking for the semantics of a signature alone is therefore not useful. That is the ground for the eight words quoted at the start of this chapter.

Signatures convey trust, and only through statements about keys and documents. Society’s many rules about what trust a signature conveys are to be left alone when building the infrastructure.

What happened since

  • Token-based authorisation became normal. OAuth 2.0 (RFC 6749) and its bearer tokens (RFC 6750), both from October 2012, have a server check a token, not who is holding it.
  • The global directory never arrived; its certificates did. The X.500 global directory never materialised, but X.509 certificates, which come from it, underpin every HTTPS connection. The certificate authorities they rely on are trusted third parties, and their compromise became a recurring problem, as with DigiNotar in 2011.
  • Signing self-contained documents. Verifiable Credentials Data Model 1.0 became a W3C Recommendation in November 2019 (1.1 in March 2022, 2.0 in 2025). It signs a self-contained document rather than a graph scattered across the Web, respecting Chapter 28’s point that a signature does not alter what a document means.

Questions 4

37.1 A hotel key card opens your room. Write its machine loop: whose key, on whose list, trusted by what? Where does it touch a person?

37.2 Berners-Lee calls each join between the two columns a rat hole. Name a system that joins them at every rung (for example, a bank branch checking photo ID), and say what it gains.

37.3 An invited expert becomes someone whose key is on a list. Give a case where that definition and the social one come apart, and say which should win.
