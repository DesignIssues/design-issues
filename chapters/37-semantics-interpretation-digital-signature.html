Chapter 37

Semantics and Interpretation (and digital signature)

1999

Source Semantics and Interpretation (and digital signature), a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 1999-12-01. read the original

The question

Written in December 1999 with an apology in the status line that its terms probably do not match those used in philosophy, this note asks what a digital signature means, and answers by arguing that the question is the wrong one.

Its opening states the ambition plainly:

We need some philosophy as a basis for the architecture of digital signature and the semantic web. Interpretation and Semantics on the Semantic Web, 1999

What follows is the most useful piece of engineering advice in Volume IV: keep the machine’s loop closed, and touch the social world only at the edges.

37.1 The self-sufficient loop

The system, Berners-Lee argues, should form a loop that closes on itself. Definitions are given in terms of other things in the system, and the system reaches outside only at a few places: the physical delivery of goods, or the presentation of a document to a person for signature.

The analogy he uses is monetary, and it does a great deal of work:

This is a similar relationship with reality that coins originally had with gold, and bills with coin. (A UK pound used to read “I promise to pay the bearer on demand the sum of one Pound signed, signed: Bank of England”). From then on a pound note became what people thought of as a pound, and the notion of what exactly the “sum of one pound” was originally defined by becomes irrelevant and the paper money is self-sufficient. Interpretation and Semantics on the Semantic Web, 1999

The reasons for touching reality at all are given as three, and none of them is that the existing definitions are correct. An interface is needed because that is how the system will work initially; the social machine has legislative backing and public understanding worth exploiting; and the existing system works, so change should be incremental. He adds the disclaimer directly: this is not because the current definitions are fundamental or beautiful, and indeed many existing systems are really crufty.

37.2 Two loops, and the mistake of joining them

The worked example is access control, and it is the clearest illustration in the book of a distinction people routinely collapse.

His diagnosis of why security work goes wrong is direct:

Security has always been plagued by people trying to connect the SW steps (such as a-d) at every stage to the social machine (A-D). Interpretation and Semantics on the Semantic Web, 1999

He follows the consequences. Identifying a person with a key introduces a directory system that is not part of the trust loop and becomes a security hole, bringing in unnecessary third parties. It raises endless questions about what identity is. Before those are settled one is arguing about belief, and whether someone really believes a claim or merely certifies it in an official role. He calls these rat holes and adds a distinction worth keeping: people do not simply believe things, they trust certain sources for certain purposes.

37.3 The inversion

What happens once the loop is closed is the part that reads as prophecy:

The semantic web becomes the definitive machine, and we just have rules at the edges about how it related to things like membership payments. An invited expert becomes defined as someone whose key is on a given list. Interpretation and Semantics on the Semantic Web, 1999

The status stops being a fact about the world that the system records and becomes a fact about the system. Berners-Lee presents this as a feature, and it is: the questions of invited in what sense, by whom, and whether the invitation was accepted never have to be answered.

It is also the mechanism by which administrative systems come to define the categories they were built to track, which is a theme Volume X returns to.

37.4 What a signature is not

The conclusion is stated twice, and the second time in six words:

Documents have meaning. Signatures by themselves do not. Interpretation and Semantics on the Semantic Web, 1999

Asking for the semantics of a signature is therefore not useful. Signatures convey trust, and even that only because of a set of statements about keys and documents. Society has many rules about what trust a signature conveys in what circumstances, and Berners-Lee’s instruction is to leave them alone when building the infrastructure.

A separate warning closes the argument and generalises past signatures:

We must be careful not to confuse what is said with where it is stored Interpretation and Semantics on the Semantic Web, 1999

The four rules of the access machine could be sent in a request, kept on several sites, carried on a card, sent by mail, or etched in marble. The design must not constrain where things are stored.

What happened since

  • The architecture won on the Web. Capability-style and token-based authorisation, where a bearer presents a chain that a server verifies without identifying a person, is now the normal shape. OAuth 2.0, published in October 2012, checks a chain rather than a person.
  • The directory approach faded. The identity-first model Berners-Lee warns against, with hierarchical directories binding people to keys, did not become the general mechanism, and the trusted third parties it requires have been a recurring source of compromise.
  • The inversion happened. Access lists now define categories of person in many organisations, rather than recording them, which is section 37.3 realised without anyone deciding to.
  • Signing graphs remained hard. The separation of what is said from where it is stored is a requirement that verifiable credentials, a W3C Recommendation of March 2022, meets by signing a self-contained document, which is Chapter 28’s constraint respected.

Questions 4

37.1 Write the machine loop for a system you use. How many rungs are there, and where does it touch the social world?

37.2 Berners-Lee calls each join between the two columns a rat hole. Name a system that joins them at every rung, and say what it gains.

37.3 An invited expert becomes someone whose key is on a list. Give a case where that definition and the social one come apart, and say which should win.

37.4 “Do not confuse what is said with where it is stored.” Find a design you know that confuses them, and say what it costs.
