Chapter 87

Fractal web, fractal society

1998

Source Fractal web, fractal society, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 1998-12-31. read the original

The short version

A society can fail in two ways: everybody shares the same ideas, so nothing new arises, or people split into sealed groups that cannot talk. Avoiding both needs groups of every size, a scale-free pattern that looks much the same at any zoom. In 1999 Jon Kleinberg proved that, on a grid, fast delivery needs links at every scale of distance.

The question

This note of 1998 names “the two opposite social deaths of a global monoculture and a set of isolated cults”. It claims the escape is a particular shape, groups of every size from one person to everyone, and adds:

This isn't an observation so much as a requirement. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

He had no proof. In 2000 the note reported a result that supplied part of one.

87.1 Two ways for a society to die

In a monoculture communication is easy, but nothing different is left to say. A cult, in the note’s sense, is a group that talks only to itself.

Groups, he says from experience, are stable when they have peers and parts; since no group can manage very many of either, there must be groups at every size between one person and everyone. He admits he lacks even a metric for “the resilience of society and its effectiveness as an organism”.

87.2 The same tension, in language

Local languages are easy to create, since only a few people need to agree on meaning; global ones are “so much more effective at communication, reaching the parts that local languages cannot”.

The tension is plainest in standards work, where ideas meet ever larger groups “with friction and hard work at each stage”.

87.3 Groups that no longer nest

Geography made communities nest: town, region, country. The Web allows groups that do not; how far society has already left that nesting, he leaves open.

Mixing helps up to a point: someone (he asks who) suggested the bicycle improved health in the Welsh valleys by widening the gene pool, but global travel could reduce diversity. Other things to model passing through such a web: viruses spreading through schools and business travel, or a problem travelling until it reaches someone with a solution.

Zipf happens

Early measurements at DEC’s firewall found its staff visiting sites with a Zipf (1/n) distribution of popularity: the top site gets twice the visits of the second, three times the third’s, and so on (Figure 87.2). The shape matters for engineering, in “planning network bandwidth between long-range and short-range communication, planning for cache usage”.

Hence the note’s new title. It began as “The Fractal nature of the web”, until he was told that fractal “should really only be used in spaces of finite dimension”; the Web’s term is scale-free.

87.4 What would “effective” mean?

One answer: to combine scientists’ knowledge “to find a cure for AIDS”. Another sets two ambitions in one breath:

We would like to preserve world peace by allowing xenophobia to disperse in a web of understanding, while at the same time preserving the diversity of culture which gives the human race its richness. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

Too little global understanding costs war; too little family communication, broken families. In general:

At any level of scale, missing social structure at that scale will prevent problems at that scale being addressed, and also prevent resources at that scale being used. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

The usual “small world” measure, the shortest path between two points, captures global effectiveness but not what he calls the “chewiness”, the structure at every scale in between.

As a personal check, the note suggests eleven jars, one per power of ten, and a marble for each period spent at that scale. The global jar, he observes, gets the least money: US international aid is far below federal, state and town spending. He adds:

People who stick at one scale all their lives feel very uncomfortable. Maybe our preferences have evolved to form naturally a fractal society. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 1998

87.5 Kleinberg’s answer, 2000

A “Stop Press” of March 2000 reports Jon Kleinberg’s result, whose logic shows in a postcard sent to a stranger in Massachusetts through friends. Local friends must find someone who knows someone there; then:

The person they find in Massachusetts must be able to pass it to people successively closer and closer to the target. this only works if there is connectivity on each scale. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 2000

Kleinberg proved it for a grid of cells with links of various lengths. For hop counts to grow only very slowly with the grid:

each cell must have the same number of links (on average) to cells 1-10 squares away as to cells 10-100 away, etc. Anything more local or more global leads to less of a small-world phenomenon: this is the only scalable solution. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 2000

Nobody has shown that hops are the right measure, the note concedes, “but on the other hand there is a clear analogy with the number of hops between a problem and a solution in a large organization”.

87.6 What shared vocabularies cost each person

In 2005 the note priced ontologies, the shared vocabularies of the Semantic Web, assuming (naively, he says) one per order of magnitude of community, a committee three larger per tenfold growth, time growing as the committee’s square, and cost shared across the community. Six of eleven rows:

Most of each person’s cost is at team and group scale; summed across the scales, the note puts it at 3.2 weeks, and a “Serious project: 30 ontologies, TCO = 10 weeks.” The lesson:

Do your bit. Others will do theirs. The Scale-free nature of the Web, 2005

And: “Thank those who do working groups.”

In 2007 he added: think of communities, not one domain of discourse. Tagging a photo of his car, using it in a car pool and registering it with the Registry of Motor Vehicles may use three ontologies. A message mixes global terms such as geo:longitude with local ones (Chapter 53); how much another agent can reuse depends on the communities they share.

The note closes the point: “one global ontology is not a solution to the problem, and a local subdomain is not a solution either.” A mix of a few ontologies at different scales, he argues, is.

What happened since

  • The Web’s link pattern was measured. Albert, Jeong and Barabási (Nature, 1999), cited in the note, found a power law: a few pages have vast numbers of links.
  • The argument reached the Semantic Web. Berners-Lee and Lalana Kagal published The Fractal Nature of the Semantic Web in AI Magazine in 2008; the note lists it as 2007.

Questions 4

87.1 The claim is a requirement rather than an observation. What would count as evidence against it?

87.2 Keep the note’s jars for a week, a marble per hour at each scale. How balanced are they?

87.3 The global jar gets the least money. By the note’s own argument, is that a failure?
