Chapter 25

The Many Meanings of Open

2013

Source The Many Meanings of Open, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2013-01-15. read the original

The short version

Two people arguing about whether something is “open” are usually arguing about different things, and neither has noticed. Asked to give a talk on the subject, Berners-Lee said the word was used in “at least eight” ways. The note itself names nine, and two more were added in 2024.

This is the list. Separating the senses settles no argument by itself. It does show which argument is actually under way, which is generally the thing missing.

The question

Berners-Lee was asked in January 2013 to give a keynote at a conference on the theme of “Open”, and found the theme underspecified. The note is the result, written up later for a magazine and added to twice since, in 2024.

I was recently asked to talk about the idea of “open”, and I realized the term is used in at least eight different ways. The Many Meanings of Open, 2013

It sits in Volume III, on how the Web changes without breaking, because openness makes such change possible. Outsiders can extend a system only if they can read its specification: a browser maker can add a feature to HTML only because the HTML specification is public.

25.1 Eleven senses, and what each protects

The note is a list of kinds, and its usefulness lies in the fact that the senses are independent. A system can be open in several and closed in the rest, and arguments become unwinnable when two people have different ones in mind.

Note the rows on open source and open access: one is about freedom, the other about price.

25.2 Universality, and what it was designed against

The first sense is given first because it is the one the Web was built for, and Berners-Lee explains it by naming what he was avoiding:

When I designed the Web protocols, I had already seen many networked information systems fail because they made some assumptions about the users – that they were using a particular type of computer for instance – or constrained the way they worked, such as forcing them to organize their data in a particular way, or to use a particular data format. The Many Meanings of Open, 2013

Decentralisation sits next to it: no central authority’s permission is needed to post, and no controlling node whose failure would take everything down. Chapter 1 lists these separately; here they are one requirement.

25.3 The sense he argues hardest for

The Open Web Platform section is the only one that makes a demand of the reader. A native app is one written for a particular operating system, such as a phone’s, and installed on it, rather than run inside a browser. The sentence that carries the demand is short:

Native apps are not on the Web, they're not part of the Web. The Many Meanings of Open, 2013

He encourages everyone to build web applications, and expects the browser to reach the hardware, such as the camera, that native applications reach.

25.4 The one he declines to settle

Openness with personal data is the shortest section and the only one where Berners-Lee explicitly stops:

The word “open” is often used in the sense “I wouldn't be that open with my personal life”. We are as a society learning how to draw the right boundaries in this new age. The Many Meanings of Open, 2013

He names the connected issues: a site that helps people share also profits from their data in unforeseen ways, and what such data should be used for is unsettled. Volume VII picks up the thread.

25.5 Where law enters

The open platform section is the most forceful in the note. The right at stake is the right to install and write software on one’s own device. Berners-Lee identifies the motive for closing a platform as a manufacturer wanting users to experience its content without being able to store it or pass it on.

On the American statutes he is direct, and the judgement is dated to 2013. The Digital Millennium Copyright Act (DMCA) forbids getting round copy protection; the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act (CFAA) punishes unauthorised access to a computer. He says they allow a person who uses a computer improperly to be sent to prison for a long time, and calls them unjust laws.

The more interesting part is their effect on the argument. They colour the debate so much that some people insist all platforms be completely open, so that nobody can be said to use them improperly.

25.6 The two additions

Two sections were added long after the original. In May 2024, following CERN’s seventieth anniversary, came open science. In September 2024 came open process, for which Berners-Lee quotes the essayist Hamish Campbell:

Open process refers to the transparent and participatory decision-making processes that govern [tech] projects. The Many Meanings of Open, 2024

His examples are W3C, the IETF and open source projects. He then widens the idea: Campbell mentions tech projects, but there is no reason it has to be one.

25.7 The one mechanism: net neutrality

The open internet section is the only one that describes a mechanism. Net neutrality is non-discrimination by the internet service provider (ISP) that carries a user’s traffic. The alternative is packets delivered in a way that discriminates “for commercial or political reasons”.

What happened since

  • The platform argument was lost and partly regained. Native applications remained dominant on mobile. The European Union’s Digital Markets Act, applicable from March 2024, requires large platforms to permit alternative app distribution: the right named in section 25.5, arriving as law.
  • Open access became policy. Funder mandates spread from the United States and the European Commission to most major research funders. Plan S, launched in September 2018, required immediate open publication of funded work from 2021.
  • Net neutrality did not settle. United States rules were adopted in 2015, repealed in 2017 and readopted in 2024; in January 2025 a federal appeals court (the Sixth Circuit) struck the 2024 rules down. The European Union adopted its Open Internet Regulation in 2015, applying from 2016. Chapter 78 treats this at length.
  • Open data lost its novelty. Government data portals are now ordinary.

Questions 4

25.1 Take a system you use and score it against all eleven senses, using the table in section 25.1. Which ones does it satisfy, and does the pattern look deliberate?

25.2 Berners-Lee says an unjust law produces an absolutist opposition. Give another instance of that pattern, in or out of computing.

25.3 Name an app you use only as a native app. What would you lose if it ran in the browser instead?

25.4 Open process was added eleven years after the 2013 list. Name a sense of “open” that is still missing from it.
