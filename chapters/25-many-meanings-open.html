Chapter 25

The Many Meanings of Open

2013

Source

This chapter is about The Many Meanings of Open, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, dated 2013-01-15.

Read the original at https://www.w3.org/DesignIssues/Open.html. The Design Issues notes are Tim Berners-Lee's personal notes and are not endorsed by W3C. This book is commentary; where it quotes, it attributes.

The question

Berners-Lee was asked in January 2013 to give a keynote at a conference on the theme of “Open”, and found the theme underspecified. The note is the result, written up later for a magazine and added to twice since, in 2024.

I was recently asked to talk about the idea of “open”, and I realized the term is used in at least eight different ways. The Many Meanings of Open, 2013

It belongs at the end of Volume III because openness is the precondition for everything the volume describes. An evolvable system requires that anyone may extend it, which requires that the specification be readable, which requires that the process be joinable.

25.1 Ten senses, and what each protects

The note is a taxonomy, and its usefulness lies in the fact that the senses are independent. A system can be open in several and closed in the rest, and arguments become unwinnable when two people have different ones in mind.

25.2 Universality, and what it was designed against

The first sense is given first because it is the one the Web was built for, and Berners-Lee explains it by naming what he was avoiding:

When I designed the Web protocols, I had already seen many networked information systems fail because they made some assumptions about the users – that they were using a particular type of computer for instance – or constrained the way they worked, such as forcing them to organize their data in a particular way, or to use a particular data format. The Many Meanings of Open, 2013

Decentralisation is placed immediately next to it, in the sense that no permission is needed from a central authority to post anything, there is no controlling node, and therefore no single point of failure. Chapter 1 lists these as separate principles; here they are treated as two faces of the same requirement.

25.3 The sense he argues hardest for

The Open Web Platform section is the only one that makes a demand of the reader, and the sentence that carries it is short:

Native apps are not on the Web, they're not part of the Web. The Many Meanings of Open, 2013

The argument is the universality argument applied to applications. A programme that runs only on one platform, or one device, is more limited than one that runs wherever there is a browser, whatever its other merits. He encourages everyone to build web applications, and expects the browser to reach the hardware that native applications reach.

25.4 The one he declines to settle

Openness with personal data is the shortest section and the only one where Berners-Lee explicitly stops:

The word “open” is often used in the sense “I wouldn't be that open with my personal life”. We are as a society learning how to draw the right boundaries in this new age. The Many Meanings of Open, 2013

He names the connected issues, that a site which helps people share also benefits from the data in unforeseen ways, and that what different sorts of data about people should be used for is unsettled, and says these may lead to cultural norms as well as new technical architectures. Volume VII is where that thread is picked up.

25.5 Where law enters

The open platform section is the angriest passage in the note. The right at stake is the right to install and write software on your own device, and Berners-Lee identifies the motive for closing a platform as a manufacturer wanting you to experience content without being able to store or pass it on.

On the American statutes he is direct, and the judgement is dated to 2013: the Digital Millennium Copyright Act and the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act allow a person who uses a computer improperly to be jailed as a felon for a long time, and he calls them unjust laws. His observation about their effect on the argument is the more interesting part: they colour the debate so much that some people react by insisting all platforms be completely open, so that nobody can be said to use them improperly. A bad law produces an absolutist opposition.

25.6 The two additions

Two sections were added long after the original. In May 2024, following CERN's seventieth anniversary, open science. In September 2024, open process, where Berners-Lee quotes Hamish Campbell on transparent and participatory decision-making, and then widens it past technology in a single line: Campbell mentions technology projects, but there is no reason it has to be one.

What happened since

  • The platform argument was lost and partly regained. Native applications remained dominant on mobile. Regulation rather than technology moved the platform question: the European Union's Digital Markets Act, applicable from March 2024, requires large platforms to permit alternative application distribution, which is the right named in section 25.5 arriving as law.
  • Open access became policy. Funder mandates spread from the United States and the European Commission to most major research funders, and Plan S, launched in September 2018, required immediate open publication of funded work.
  • Net neutrality did not settle. United States rules were adopted in 2015, repealed in 2017, and readopted in 2024, while the European Union's regulation has held since 2015. Chapter 78 treats this at length.
  • Open data lost its novelty and kept its problem. Government portals are now ordinary, and the complaint that opened the section, that sites publish reports rather than the data behind them, describes most published analysis still.

Connections

Universality and decentralisation are principles one and four of Chapter 1, and the axioms that implement them are Chapter 2. The open standards this note describes are written according to Chapter 21 and stacked as in Chapter 20. Open data is Chapter 53, personal data is Volume VII, net neutrality is Chapter 78, and digital rights management is Chapter 80.

Questions 4

25.1 Take a system you use and score it against all ten senses. Which ones does it satisfy, and does the pattern look deliberate?

25.2 Berners-Lee says an unjust law produces an absolutist opposition. Give another instance of that pattern, in or out of computing.

25.3 The claim that native applications are not part of the Web rests on universality. State the strongest case for a native application on grounds this note would accept.

25.4 Open process was added eleven years after the original eight. Name a sense of “open” that is still missing from the list.
