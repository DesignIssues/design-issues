Chapter 49

Linked Data

2006

Source Linked Data, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2006-07-27. read the original

The short version

Putting data online is not the same as making it usable. A spreadsheet on a website is online. It is also a dead end: nothing in it points anywhere, and nothing anywhere points into it.

This note is four rules for publishing data so that a person or a program arriving at one piece can find the rest. It is the most quoted thing its author ever wrote, and short enough to hold in your head.

The question

This is the most widely read note in the collection and the only one whose content most practitioners can recite. Written in July 2006, it opens Volume VI because everything after it is either an application of its four rules or an argument about what they left out.

The first sentence does the work of a whole volume:

The Semantic Web isn't just about putting data on the web. It is about making links, so that a person or machine can explore the web of data. With linked data, when you have some of it, you can find other, related, data. Linked Data, 2006

49.2 Where each rule fails

The diagnosis by rule is the useful part, because the failures are different in kind.

The first rule is understood. Berners-Lee makes it definitional: if it does not use the universal set of symbols, we do not call it Semantic Web.

The second rule is understood and constantly violated, and he names the motive rather than the mechanism:

Typically, these involve not wanting to commit to the established Domain Name System (DNS) for delegation of authority but to construct something under separate control. Linked Data, 2006

Every rival identifier scheme is, on this reading, an attempt to avoid someone else’s authority. Chapter 3 explains why that attempt keeps recurring and Chapter 13 explains what it would take to satisfy it honestly.

The third rule is where the failure is most frustrating, because it is accidental. Vocabularies are served; datasets frequently are not.

Many research and evaluation projects in the few years of the Semantic Web technologies produced ontologies, and significant data stores, but the data, if available at all, is buried in a zip archive somewhere, rather than being accessible on the web as linked data. Linked Data, 2006

The fourth rule is argued by analogy with manners rather than mechanism. On the hypertext Web it is considered bad etiquette not to link to related material, and the value of one’s own information is very much a function of what it links to.

49.3 Two ways to make the link

The basic mechanism is the one from Chapter 11. Put local names in a document, and the architecture supplies global ones by prefixing the document’s address. Anyone who meets such an identifier can truncate at the hash, fetch the document, and learn about the thing.

The alternative, for identifiers without a hash, is the redirect of Chapter 8, and Berners-Lee gives the trade in one sentence each. It allows URIs of all forms, and it requires a request for every single one. His example is exact: several terms in one vocabulary are served by the same document, and one does not know that until each has been fetched and redirected separately.

49.4 The convention that worked and was wrong

A third pattern gets its own section because it was, in 2006, the dominant form of linked data on the Web. The social vocabulary of the day identified a person by two properties: a mailbox to say who, and a pointer to a document for more. To protect the address, publishers commonly gave a one-way hash of it instead.

This clever trick allows people who know their email address already to work out that it is the same person, without giving the email away to others. Linked Data, 2006

Berners-Lee credits the success and then names the defect:

However, the system has the snag that it does not give URIs to people, and so basic links to them cannot be made. Linked Data, 2006

A network of people that cannot link to a person is the failure of rule one inside a system that obeys the other three. Chapter 34 is the earlier argument that this pattern implements, and this is the moment its cost became visible.

What happened since

  • The rules became the standard statement. They are quoted in the specification of every subsequent publishing effort, and the five-star scheme of Chapter 51 is their popular form.
  • Linking open data grew and then thinned. The interlinked public dataset cloud grew rapidly from 2007, and many of its members later stopped being served, which is rule three failing after the fact rather than at publication.
  • Rule two lost ground where it mattered. Persistent identifier schemes under separate control, exactly the kind named in section 49.2, became the norm in scholarly and cultural publishing.
  • The people problem was solved elsewhere. Giving people their own identifiers happened through Solid and decentralised identifiers rather than through social vocabularies, which are described in Chapter 54.

Questions 4

49.1 Take a dataset you publish or use. Which of the four rules does it satisfy, and which failure is easiest to fix?

49.2 Every rival identifier scheme is described as avoiding someone else’s authority. Name one, and say what authority it avoids and what it substitutes.

49.3 The social vocabulary identified people without naming them. Write the same information in a way that gives the person an identifier, and say what changes.

49.4 Rule four is argued from etiquette rather than mechanism. Is that sufficient? Give a case where linking out is against the publisher’s interest.
