Chapter 74

The Intimacy Gradient

2023

Source The Intimacy Gradient, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2023-04-14. read the original

The short version

Life happens in groups of many sizes, but software offers only public and private. Buildings solve this with a sequence of rooms that can be told apart at a glance. Berners-Lee proposes doing the same for data in Solid, starting with colour, and designing the private end as carefully as the public one.

The question

Volume VII closes with a note from 2022 that takes its central idea from architecture, in the ordinary sense, and applies it to systems that have only ever offered two settings.

If in our systems we cater for only the public and only the private extremes, we miss out most of our lives! Most of our lives involve doing things with combinations of different sized groups of people. The Intimacy Gradient, 2022

74.1 The building

A city has public places where I can do all kinds of things, and also a private house with a private room which may be by myself. In that house there are spaces where I do things with family, friends, colleagues. The Intimacy Gradient, 2022

Christopher Alexander was an architect whose book A Pattern Language catalogues recurring design solutions; pattern 125 is the one Berners-Lee cites. Its resolution is a building laid out as a sequence: from the entrance and the most public parts, through slightly more private areas, to the most private ones.

The web, he says, must do the same, so that I can easily recognise the difference, easily know which I am in, and easily welcome people into the more intimate areas.

74.2 The mechanism he proposes is colour

A simple way to start making an intimacy gradient on the web is with style. The Intimacy Gradient, 2022

W3C pages are public, member-only or team-only, and by convention their backgrounds are white, pale blue and straw; the mailing-list archives use the same colours. The reason is safety, not looks:

For one thing, for security, anyone looking at a page will know who they can and cannot share things with. The Intimacy Gradient, 2022

People still mailed non-public material to public lists, so W3C put the warning word “public-” in every public list’s address. Then the gap:

Mail clients don't color the background of a message you are composing as a function of how large the audience is your are addressing - bit perhaps they could! The Intimacy Gradient, 2022

74.3 Both ends of the gradient deserve design

The note’s real subject is Solid, where each person keeps their data in a pod: a personal store on the web that they control (Chapter 54). A pod holds public things such as a profile, private things such as settings and preferences, and things shared with groups and combinations of groups.

Profiles are styled because people want to be proud of them. Then Berners-Lee turns the argument around:

However, thinking about both intimacy gradients and the different styles of the W3C web site, surely we should not stop there but make sure we think about the style of the private places too. The Intimacy Gradient, 2022

His instruction has two sides: make the default experience in intimate places one that respects that intimacy, and also let people control it.

A home, not a showroom

The private parts of a pod, he says, should feel like a home. The owner feels secure there and is in charge, arranging things their own way, in their own styles, filed alphabetically or by date. And because Solid lets people choose their apps, they can adopt a developer’s ideas about home they would not have had.

74.4 Why this closes the volume

Most chapters in Volume VII are about a system that models a social arrangement too narrowly. Trust is treated as a single yes-or-no value, so a padlock either shows or it does not (Chapter 67). Every application on one domain is treated as one party (Chapter 68). Sharing is a switch between nobody and the whole Web (Chapter 72).

This chapter names what they all miss, borrowing from an architect: people live in the rooms between.

74.5 What varies along the gradient

The note ends by asking what the gradient is a gradient in, and names the four rows below. The cells are this book’s illustration, not the author’s claims.

DimensionPublic endIntimate end
Colour and themeA clear warning that many can seeThe owner’s choice, restful
FontsThe community’s house styleWhatever the owner finds easy to read
Protection from mistakesConfirm before anything goes outFew interruptions
Fact-checkingClaims checked before they spreadNotes to oneself left as written

Because the gradient runs through the whole Solid ecosystem, everyone who codes a piece of it has to know where each interaction falls, and code accordingly. A start is to pick up key colours from the user’s preferences and from their communities’.

What happened since

  • Audience selection stayed coarse. Most platforms still offer a small fixed set of audiences, which is the three-option model of Chapter 64, and overlapping groups of different sizes remain hard to express.
  • The composing window is still uncoloured. Some mail systems warn before sending to a large or external audience, but the warning is a dialog box, not a change in how the composing window looks.
  • The pattern language kept being borrowed. Alexander’s patterns continued to be applied to software, and this is among the more literal applications: a gradient of rooms, for data.

Questions 4

74.1 Name the rooms of your own data. How many does your main tool let you distinguish?

74.2 Colour the composing window by audience size. What are the levels, and where are the boundaries?

74.3 Private places have been left undesigned. Describe what you would change about a tool you use only by yourself (notes, photos, a password manager).

74.4 Berners-Lee suggests that protection from mistakes could vary along the gradient. Where should a tool be most cautious, and where least?
