Chapter 45

The RDF-diff problem

2001

Source The RDF-diff problem, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2001-01-01. read the original

The question

This is the most paper-shaped note in the collection, written with an abstract and references, and it asks a question that sounds like plumbing and is not: what is the difference between two graphs, and how do you send it?

Berners-Lee starts from what already works elsewhere. Text files have differences and version control. Databases have insert and update statements. In both cases the states are represented explicitly and so are the transitions between them.

Since the transitions can be represented much more compactly than the pairs of states, and the sigma function is straightforward to compute, the deltas are useful for efficiently updating data distributed among two or more peers. Delta: an ontology for the distribution of differences between RDF graphs, 2001

45.1 The three problems hiding in one

The motivating example is chosen for being universally annoying: synchronising calendars and address books between devices, which Berners-Lee calls one of the most stubborn problems in practical computing, producing very strange results on not-so-rare occasions.

He then separates it into three problems that are usually confused.

ProblemQuestion
SyntacticHow do I get the data out of the device at all?
SemanticWhat do the fields mean? Can one have two home numbers?
SynchronisationGiven two changed copies, what actually happened?

Most synchronisation failures people experience are failures of the second, dressed as failures of the third. The rest of the note addresses the third and assumes the first two are solved.

45.2 Why a line-oriented diff will not do

A graph has no lines. Two files may serialise the same graph with statements in a different order, with different abbreviations, and with different arbitrary names for the nodes that have no names. A textual difference between them reports enormous change where nothing has changed.

A difference between graphs must therefore be computed over the statements themselves, and expressed as a pattern to match rather than a position to edit.

45.3 Weak and strong

The note’s contribution is a distinction that turns out to govern every distributed update system.

The property that makes a patch strong is the one Chapter 34 introduced for identifying people: a property no two things can share. That earlier chapter used it to name someone without giving them an address; here the same property lets a change travel between stores without going astray.

45.4 What a patch is, besides an instruction

Two short sections turn the mechanism into something larger, and they are why the note is in this volume rather than filed under tooling.

Patches as knowledge. A difference between two graphs is itself expressible as statements, so it can be published, described, signed and reasoned about like anything else. The record of a change is data.

Patches as news. If the record of a change is data, it can be distributed to interested parties as it happens. A feed becomes a stream of differences rather than a sequence of whole states, which is both smaller and more informative: it says what changed, not merely what is now true.

45.5 The follow-up, and what it concedes

A section added in 2015 looks back at fourteen years, and grants that the ideas became common property:

In the several years since this was originally written, the general concepts of diffs and patches, delta and sigma, differentiation and integration, been constant themes in distributed systems. Delta: an ontology for the distribution of differences between RDF graphs, 2001

He discusses an architecture in which differences travel in loops, so that a party receives back both the other side’s edits and, in his phrase, their apologies for not being able to apply a patch. The observation he draws from it is an architectural preference:

Such systems assume they must watch for a user editing with other tools and then compute a difference to discover what changed. The alternative is for the editing tool to send the difference explicitly, which is the style adopted in the later work described in Volume VI. Computing a difference is a way of recovering information that was thrown away; sending one is a way of not throwing it away.

What happened since

  • The strong patch problem was solved by canonicalisation. RDF Dataset Canonicalization, a W3C Recommendation of May 2024, gives nameless nodes deterministic labels, which makes comparing and patching graphs tractable without relying on an identifying property.
  • Patch formats arrived. SPARQL Update became a Recommendation in March 2013, and later work produced patch formats used for synchronising stores, which is section 45.3’s proposal in standard form.
  • Explicit change won over computed change. Conflict-free replicated data types and operation-based synchronisation became the standard approach in collaborative editing, and they work by sending operations rather than diffing states, which is the preference expressed in the 2015 follow-up.
  • The semantic problem remains the hard one. Contact and calendar synchronisation still fails, and still mostly fails on the second of the three problems in section 45.1 rather than the third.

Connections

Questions 4

45.3 A feed of changes says more than a feed of states. Give a question that the first can answer and the second cannot.
