Chapter 45

The RDF-diff problem

2001

Source The RDF-diff problem, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2001-01-01. read the original

The short version

A delta lists what to remove and what to add; sigma (sum) means applying it. Sending deltas between copies is cheaper than sending whole copies, but only safe if the delta names things that cannot be confused.

The question

This is the most paper-shaped note in the collection, written by Berners-Lee with Dan Connolly. It asks a question that sounds like plumbing and is not: what is the difference between two graphs, and how is it sent?

Text files have differences and version control; databases have insert and update statements. In both, the states and the transitions between them are represented explicitly.

Since the transitions can be represented much more compactly than the pairs of states, and the sigma function is straightforward to compute, the deltas are useful for efficiently updating data distributed among two or more peers. Delta: an ontology for the distribution of differences between RDF graphs, 2001

Delta (Δ) is the difference between two versions; sigma (Σ) applies a difference to a version to get the next one.

45.1 The three problems hiding in one

The motivating example is universally annoying: synchronising calendars and address books between devices, one of the most stubborn problems in practical computing. The note separates it into three problems that are usually confused.

ProblemQuestion
SyntacticHow do I get the data out of the device at all?
SemanticWhat do the fields mean? Can one have two home numbers?
SynchronisationGiven two changed copies, what actually happened?

The note suspects the semantic problem, or the mixing of the three, explains why so many packages break, and proposes keeping them separate. It then addresses only the third.

45.2 Why a line-oriented diff will not do

A graph has no lines. Two files may write the same graph with statements in a different order, and with different labels for its blank nodes: nodes the data mentions but has not named, such as Bob’s pet in Figure 45.2. Any label will do, so a textual comparison reports change where there is none.

Line diffs worked for hand-edited files, but failed badly for machine-written test results: every slight change to the printing program produced false test failures. A graph difference must be computed over the statements, and expressed as a pattern to match rather than a position to edit.

45.3 Weak and strong

The note’s patch format, in Notation3, is a pattern that finds the node, then what to remove and what to add. This is the form implemented by cwm, Berners-Lee’s command-line RDF tool: 

{ ?x bank:accountNo "1234578"}
  diff:deletion  { ?x bank:balance 4000};
  diff:insertion { ?x bank:balance 3575}.

?x is a variable, standing for whatever matches, and each pair of braces holds a quoted group of statements. So: find the thing whose account number is 1234578; remove “balance 4000”; add “balance 3575”. A single alternative property, diff:replacement, can express any change. Applied to a copy that has itself changed, a patch can fail in three ways.

FailureWhat happensCan it be prevented?
Inconsistent changesOne person moves a meeting while another deletes it.No; a person must decide.
No matchThe patch cannot find where to apply.Yes, for certain graph deltas.
MisappliedThe patch applies at the wrong place; erroneous data results.It must be: “This is unacceptable.”

Whether a patch can be misapplied depends on what its pattern names.

Account numbers exist precisely to avoid this confusion. The property that makes a patch strong is the one Chapter 34 used to identify people: one no two things can share. Here it lets a change travel between stores without going astray.

45.4 What a patch is, besides an instruction

Patches as knowledge. A strong patch has meaning in itself. A phone number changing from 1234 to 5678 suggests the person moved or changed jobs. For houses for sale, the change is the news; for currency rates, listening to the changes builds a working knowledge.

Patches as news. When the state is held in RDF, a difference between graphs is news. The W3C RSS feed was in effect partly a list of changes to the W3C’s technical reports list, and publishing RDF differences could make that formal.

45.5 The follow-up, and what it concedes

A section added in 2015 grants that the ideas became common property:

In the several years since this was originally written, the general concepts of diffs and patches, delta and sigma, differentiation and integration, been constant themes in distributed systems. Delta: an ontology for the distribution of differences between RDF graphs, 2001

It cites Neil Fraser’s 2008 paper Differential Synchronization, in which differences travel in loops, carrying edits and apologies for patches that could not be applied. That design watches a user editing with other tools, then computes a difference to discover what changed.

The alternative is for the editing tool to send the difference itself. In the Solid work of Volume VI, the patch is sent just before the interface shows the change, so it can be reverted if the patch fails.

Computing a difference recovers information that was thrown away; sending one avoids throwing it away.

What happened since

  • Canonicalisation was standardised. RDF Dataset Canonicalization, a W3C Recommendation of May 2024, gives blank nodes deterministic labels, making graph comparison deterministic. It does not make patches strong: one added triple can still relabel many blank nodes.
  • Patch formats arrived. SPARQL Update became a Recommendation in March 2013, and LD Patch was published as a W3C Working Group Note in 2015: section 45.3’s proposal in standard form.
  • Explicit change won over computed change. Collaborative editors came to rely on operational transformation and on operation-based designs of conflict-free replicated data types, which send operations rather than diffing states, as the 2015 follow-up preferred.
