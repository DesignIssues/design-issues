Chapter 15

Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs

2002

Source Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2002-07-29. read the original

The question

This chapter closes Volume II with a document unlike any other in the collection. It is not a note but an interview, conducted by Harry Halpin and Alexandre Monnin for a volume of writings from the PhiloWeb workshops, dated March 2013. Berners-Lee is being asked what kind of activity Web design is, and the answer explains why a book of architecture notes contains so many arguments about meaning.

It is placed last in the volume on naming because it states, more directly than anywhere else, what the identity axiom of Chapter 2 amounts to: that somebody owns a name and therefore gets to say what it means.

15.1 Where the phrase came from

The origin is an Oxford joke that turned out to be serious. Berners-Lee recalls being amused that the physics degree at Oxford is formally called experimental philosophy, a philosophy done by dropping things and seeing whether they continue to drop. The phrase returned when he tried to explain what writing a protocol is:

effectively when you create a protocol you get the right to “play God” and define what words mean. You can define a philosophy, to define a new world. So when people use your system, when they run the protocol, to a certain extent they have to leave their previous philosophy at the door and they have to join in and agree they will work with your system. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

He adds the qualification immediately: that is exciting, and a source of responsibility as well.

15.2 Specification as an agreement to stop arguing

Asked whether writing standards is philosophy in progress, Berners-Lee gives an account of specification that anyone who has sat in a working group will recognise. People argue about what words mean until the concepts in different heads are sufficiently aligned, and then it is written down in language everyone can live with.

What makes it work is a refusal to reopen the question:

You can if you want to philosophically argue that a word is in fact ambiguous, but nobody bothers. Understand when you play the game [of specification-writing], you're not going to argue about that. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

The illustration is a refusal of payment. Nobody may send HTTP headers to buy something and then argue afterwards that headers do not actually mean anything. He quotes a spammer saying that a form field can contain anything and need not be the person sending the email, and answers: it does if you are playing the game.

From which he derives a gap he says is missing:

I think one of the things we're missing is the relationship between the law of the land and protocols. It should be easier to establish that when someone disobeys a protocol that they've broken the law via a straight-forward path. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

15.3 What is special about a URI

Asked how URIs differ from words, Berners-Lee gives the answer this volume has been building towards, and it is about property:

one thing that is nifty about HTTP URIs is that they have domain names in them. So they're hierarchical, and a domain is something that one can own. In the way the protocol works, the owner of the domain has the right to say, and the obligation to say on the Semantic Web, what the things in that domain mean. It's not a question of philosophical discussions between third parties. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

The right and the obligation are named in the same sentence, and the obligation is the less obvious half. Owning the name means being the party who must publish what it means, at an address anyone can look up in real time.

Monnin presses the point: a URI is an artefact, so it can be owned, whereas a philosophical name cannot. Berners-Lee accepts the distinction and turns it into a claim about which world is under discussion. Some philosophies treat meaning as a function of use rather than definition. Lawyers work in a world where terms have legal definitions, because otherwise people could be jailed over usage. The Web adds a third model:

So there are models, and now we're adding another one, in which meaning is defined by the owner of a name. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

15.4 The one he wishes he had not conceded

A short exchange settles a question Chapter 2 leaves hanging: why the U in URI stands for Uniform rather than Universal.

Berners-Lee says he originally called them Universal Document Identifiers, that the IETF found “universal” too much hubris, and that he gave way. He now regards this as a mistake:

now I realize that I should have held firm and said “but they are,” as any alternative system of naming you can make out there, I can map it to the character set we use in URIs and I can invent a new scheme for it. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

That is the theorem of Chapter 2 restated seventeen years later as a regret. The argument was sound; it lost on tone.

15.5 Two protocols, and why spam is an architecture problem

The most transferable idea in the interview is that every deployed system has two protocols, and that only one of them is written down.

Berners-Lee works the example of email. The technical protocol is SMTP for sending and IMAP for retrieval. The social protocol is that everyone runs a machine with room to store their friends’ mail in transit, that people write to each other on reasonable topics, and that people read what they receive. Together these form a microscopic system whose interest lies in what emerges at scale.

So when you do the science, the analysis, and the whole rest of the cycle for e-mail, you look at what is happening and notice “Spam has happened, oh dear!” What went wrong? One of our social assumptions was wrong, namely that everybody is friendly and will only send email to another person when the other person wants to read it. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

Spam is thus not a failure of SMTP. It is the falsification of an unwritten assumption in the social protocol, and Berners-Lee notes that nobody has succeeded in redesigning either half to remove it. He ends the answer by naming what is missing: the mathematics connecting the microscopic design to the macroscopic behaviour.

15.6 What he wants from philosophers

The closing request is modest and slightly comic, and it describes the job this book is attempting:

What I would like for philosophers to do is to work diligently and to produce very nice, simple, documents which describe to people like computer scientists how things work in a simple way. What happens when you click on a link? Quite a lot of that is philosophy. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

The purpose is not edification. It is to stop working groups halting. He imagines someone saying that a proposal does not match what they learned from Wittgenstein, and wants a pamphlet to hand them so that work can continue.

What happened since

  • The collection this appeared in was published. Philosophical Engineering: Toward a Philosophy of the Web, edited by Halpin and Monnin, appeared from Wiley-Blackwell in 2014.
  • The law-and-protocol gap remains. Disobeying a protocol is still not straightforwardly unlawful. Where computer-misuse law has reached protocol behaviour, it has done so through statutes about unauthorised access whose boundaries are contested, not through the direct path described here.
  • The obligation to publish meaning was widely ignored. Domain owners rarely serve definitions for the terms they mint, which is the first snag recorded in Chapter 9, restated here as a duty rather than a practice.
  • The two-protocol analysis has been vindicated repeatedly. Every subsequent failure of a social assumption at scale, from spam to coordinated manipulation of platforms, has the shape described here: the technical protocol working exactly as specified while an unwritten social assumption turns out to be false. Chapter 90 is the same argument applied to social networks.

Connections

The ownership of meaning described in section 15.3 is the identity axiom of Chapter 2, and the universality regret in section 15.4 is that chapter’s theorem. The obligation to publish a definition is what Chapters 7 to 9 argue about. The social backing of names is Chapter 6, and the contractual reading of persistence is Chapters 3 and 13. The two-protocol idea is taken up again in Chapter 87 and Chapter 90.

Questions 4

15.1 Berners-Lee says a protocol designer gets to define what words mean. Name a term whose protocol definition departs sharply from ordinary usage, and say what the departure buys.

15.2 The owner of a domain is said to have both a right and an obligation. What happens to the architecture when owners exercise the right and ignore the obligation?

15.3 Write the social protocol of a system you use, in the manner of section 15.5. Then name the assumption in it that would be most damaging if false.

15.4 Berners-Lee regrets conceding “universal” to “uniform”. Would the stronger word have changed any technical outcome, or only the argument?
