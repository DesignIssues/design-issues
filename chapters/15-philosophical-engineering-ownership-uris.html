Chapter 15

Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs

2002

Source Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2002-07-29. read the original

The short version

A protocol’s designer decides what its words mean, and users accept that by joining in. A URI contains a domain name, a domain has an owner, and that owner may and must say what the name means. Every system also runs on unwritten social rules. Email assumed everyone was friendly; when that proved false, spam followed, even though the technical rules worked perfectly.

The question

This chapter closes Volume II with a document unlike any other in the collection. It is not a note but an interview, conducted by Harry Halpin and Alexandre Monnin for a volume of writings from the PhiloWeb workshops, dated March 2013. Berners-Lee is asked what kind of activity Web design is, and the answer explains why a book of architecture notes contains so many arguments about meaning.

It is placed last in the volume on naming because it states, more directly than anywhere else, what the identity axiom of Chapter 2 amounts to: that somebody owns a name and therefore gets to say what it means.

15.1 Where the phrase came from

The origin is an Oxford joke that turned out to be serious. Berners-Lee recalls being amused that the physics degree at Oxford is formally called experimental philosophy: philosophy done by dropping things and seeing whether they continue to drop.

The phrase came up while Web Science was being discussed: the study of what the Web does once millions use it. That, he says, could have been called philosophical engineering, because of what writing a protocol is. A protocol is an agreed set of rules two programs follow when they talk.

effectively when you create a protocol you get the right to “play God” and define what words mean. You can define a philosophy, to define a new world. So when people use your system, when they run the protocol, to a certain extent they have to leave their previous philosophy at the door and they have to join in and agree they will work with your system. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

He adds that this is exciting, and a responsibility.

15.2 Specification as an agreement to stop arguing

Asked whether writing standards is philosophy in progress, Berners-Lee gives an account that any member of a working group will recognise. A working group is the committee that writes a standard. Its members argue about what words mean until the concepts in different heads are aligned enough, and then it is written down in language everyone can live with. What makes it work is a refusal to reopen the question:

You can if you want to philosophically argue that a word is in fact ambiguous, but nobody bothers. Understand when you play the game [of specification-writing], you're not going to argue about that. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

His illustration is a payment someone tries to disown afterwards. Nobody may pay a bill online and then argue that the HTTP headers they sent (the labelled lines that go with every Web request) do not actually mean anything. He recalls a spammer’s defence: the sender line of an email is “just a form field”, so anything can go there. It cannot, he answers, for anyone playing the game. From this he derives a gap:

I think one of the things we're missing is the relationship between the law of the land and protocols. It should be easier to establish that when someone disobeys a protocol that they've broken the law via a straight-forward path. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

15.3 What is special about a URI

Asked how URIs differ from words, Berners-Lee gives the answer this volume has been building towards, and it is about property. (The Semantic Web, mentioned in it, is data published so that programs can look up what terms mean.)

one thing that is nifty about HTTP URIs is that they have domain names in them. So they're hierarchical, and a domain is something that one can own. In the way the protocol works, the owner of the domain has the right to say, and the obligation to say on the Semantic Web, what the things in that domain mean. It's not a question of philosophical discussions between third parties. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

He then gives the mechanism. In a dispute about what a URI stands for, the question goes to the owner of the domain name, who typically delegates to someone else, who has written an ontology (a machine-readable set of definitions) and stored it on a Web server. Anyone can look the URI up in real time and get that answer straight away.

Monnin presses the point: a URI is an artefact, so it can be owned, whereas a philosophical name cannot. Berners-Lee answers that in the world under discussion “names are owned”, and places this among other models of meaning:

ModelWho decidesHow a dispute ends (this book’s gloss)
Meaning as useThe speakersNo one: usage drifts
Legal definitionThe lawA court, because “otherwise people could get put in jail”
Owner’s definitionThe domain ownerLook the URI up
So there are models, and now we're adding another one, in which meaning is defined by the owner of a name. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

15.4 The one he wishes he had not conceded

A short exchange explains why the U in URI stands for Uniform rather than Universal. Berners-Lee says he originally called them Universal Document Identifiers, that the IETF (the body that publishes the Internet’s standards) found “universal” too much hubris, and that he gave way. He now regards this as a mistake:

now I realize that I should have held firm and said “but they are,” as any alternative system of naming you can make out there, I can map it to the character set we use in URIs and I can invent a new scheme for it. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

That is Chapter 2’s claim, that any naming scheme can be written as a URI, restated seventeen years later as a regret.

15.5 Two protocols, and why spam is an architecture problem

The most transferable idea in the interview is that every deployed system has two protocols, only one of them written down.

Berners-Lee works the example of email. The technical protocol is SMTP and IMAP, the rules for sending mail and for collecting it. The social protocol is that everyone runs a machine with room to store their friends’ mail in transit, that people write to each other on reasonable topics, and that people read what they receive. Together these explain one message passing from one person to another in a few hops. What matters is what happens when millions do it.

So when you do the science, the analysis, and the whole rest of the cycle for e-mail, you look at what is happening and notice “Spam has happened, oh dear!” What went wrong? One of our social assumptions was wrong, namely that everybody is friendly and will only send email to another person when the other person wants to read it. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

Spam is thus not a failure of SMTP but an unwritten assumption proving false, and Berners-Lee notes that nobody has succeeded in redesigning either half to remove it. What is missing, he says, is the mathematics connecting the microscopic design to the macroscopic behaviour.

15.6 What he wants from philosophers

He sees two jobs for philosophy. One is legal. Anyone can link to any Web page, but a lawyer may claim the right to charge for linking to theirs; then someone has to explain how a protocol defined over a name, a mere reference, grounds out in concepts lawyers understand. The other is the request that closes the interview:

What I would like for philosophers to do is to work diligently and to produce very nice, simple, documents which describe to people like computer scientists how things work in a simple way. What happens when you click on a link? Quite a lot of that is philosophy. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

The purpose is to stop working groups halting. He imagines someone saying that a proposal does not match what they learned from Wittgenstein (a philosopher who held that meaning is use), and wants a pamphlet to hand them so that work can continue.

What happened since

  • The collection this appeared in was published. Philosophical Engineering: Toward a Philosophy of the Web, edited by Halpin and Monnin, appeared from Wiley-Blackwell in 2014.
  • The law-and-protocol gap remains. Disobeying a protocol is still not straightforwardly unlawful. Where computer-misuse law has reached protocol behaviour, it has done so through statutes about unauthorised access whose boundaries are contested; in Van Buren v. United States (2021) the US Supreme Court narrowed the reach of the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act. This is not the direct path described here.

Connections

The ownership of meaning described in section 15.3 is the identity axiom of Chapter 2, and the universality regret in section 15.4 is that chapter’s claim about naming schemes. The obligation to publish a definition is what Chapters 7 to 9 argue about; Chapter 9 records how often domain owners serve no definitions for the terms they mint. The social backing of names is Chapter 6, and the contractual reading of persistence is Chapters 3 and 13. The two-protocol idea is taken up again in Chapter 87 and Chapter 90.

Questions 4

15.1 Berners-Lee says a protocol designer gets to define what words mean. Name a term whose protocol definition departs sharply from ordinary usage (for example, what a From line or a GET request commits you to), and say what the departure buys.

15.2 The owner of a domain is said to have both a right and an obligation. What happens to the architecture when owners exercise the right and ignore the obligation?

15.3 Write the social protocol of a system you use, in the manner of section 15.5. Then name the assumption in it that would be most damaging if false.

15.4 Berners-Lee says any naming system can be mapped into URIs. Show how, for phone numbers or ISBNs.
