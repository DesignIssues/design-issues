Chapter 15

Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs

2002

Source Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2002-07-29. read the original

The short version

A protocol’s designer decides what its words mean, and users accept that by joining in. A URI contains a domain name, a domain has an owner, and that owner may, and on the Semantic Web must, say what the name means. Every system also runs on unwritten social rules. Email assumed everyone was friendly; when that proved false, spam followed, even though the technical rules were obeyed.

The question

A spammer once defended himself by saying that the sender line of an email is “just a form field, I can put whatever I like there”. Berners-Lee’s reply is that it does matter, for anyone playing the game. Who decides what a field, a header or a Web address means? In this interview his answer is blunt: whoever designed the protocol, and, for a URI, whoever owns the domain.

The source is an interview by Harry Halpin and Alexandre Monnin for a book from the PhiloWeb workshops, published as a Design Issues note in March 2013. It closes Volume II, the volume on naming, because it states most directly what the identity axiom of Chapter 2 amounts to: that somebody owns a name and therefore gets to say what it means.

15.1 Physics as experimental philosophy

Berners-Lee recalls being tickled that Oxford’s physics degree is called experimental philosophy: philosophy done by dropping things and seeing whether they continue to drop.

The phrase came up while Web Science was being discussed: the study of what the Web does once millions use it. That, he says, could have been called philosophical engineering, because of what writing a protocol is. A protocol is an agreed set of rules two programs follow when they talk.

effectively when you create a protocol you get the right to “play God” and define what words mean. You can define a philosophy, to define a new world. So when people use your system, when they run the protocol, to a certain extent they have to leave their previous philosophy at the door and they have to join in and agree they will work with your system. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

He goes on: “So you can build systems, worlds, which have different properties. That's exciting, and a source of responsibility as well.”

15.2 Specification as an agreement to stop arguing

Asked whether writing standards is philosophy in progress, Berners-Lee gives a practitioner’s account. A working group is the committee that writes a standard. Its members argue about what words mean until the concepts in different heads are aligned enough, and then it is written down in language everyone can live with. What makes it work is a refusal to reopen the question:

You can if you want to philosophically argue that a word is in fact ambiguous, but nobody bothers. Understand when you play the game [of specification-writing], you're not going to argue about that. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

His illustration: nobody may pay a bill online and then argue that the HTTP headers they sent (the labelled lines that go with every Web request) do not actually mean anything. The spammer’s “form field” defence fails for the same reason. From this he derives a gap:

I think one of the things we're missing is the relationship between the law of the land and protocols. It should be easier to establish that when someone disobeys a protocol that they've broken the law via a straight-forward path. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

15.3 A name that someone owns

Asked how URIs differ from words, Berners-Lee answers in terms of property, and mentions the Semantic Web: data published so that programs can look up what terms mean.

one thing that is nifty about HTTP URIs is that they have domain names in them. So they're hierarchical, and a domain is something that one can own. In the way the protocol works, the owner of the domain has the right to say, and the obligation to say on the Semantic Web, what the things in that domain mean. It's not a question of philosophical discussions between third parties. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

In a dispute about what a URI stands for, the question goes to the domain owner, who typically delegates to someone else, who has written an ontology (a machine-readable set of definitions) and stored it on a Web server. Anyone can look the URI up in real time and get that answer straight away.

Monnin presses the point: a URI, unlike a philosophical name, is an artefact and can be owned. Berners-Lee answers that in the world under discussion “names are owned”, and places this among other models of meaning:

ModelWho decidesHow a dispute ends (this book’s gloss)
Meaning as useThe speakersNo one: usage drifts
Legal definitionThe lawA court, because “otherwise people could get put in jail”
Owner’s definitionThe domain ownerLook the URI up
So there are models, and now we're adding another one, in which meaning is defined by the owner of a name. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

15.4 The one he wishes he had not conceded

A short exchange explains why the U in URI stands for Uniform rather than Universal. Berners-Lee says he originally called them Universal Document Identifiers, that the IETF (the body that publishes the Internet’s standards) found “universal” too much hubris, and that he gave way. He now regards this as a mistake:

now I realize that I should have held firm and said “but they are,” as any alternative system of naming you can make out there, I can map it to the character set we use in URIs and I can invent a new scheme for it. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

This is Chapter 2’s claim, that any naming scheme can be written as a URI, and his reason for calling “but they are” fact rather than hubris is that it had already been done: “we'd already mapped Gopher and FTP”.

15.5 Two protocols, and why spam is an architecture problem

Berners-Lee’s account of Web Science starts from the claim that the Web means designing two things: a technical protocol, the written rules, and a social protocol, what users are assumed to do. Its engineering “is not like building a mousetrap. You design a microscopic system, but what you're interested in is the macroscopic phenomena that emerge.”

Email is his example. The technical protocol is SMTP and IMAP, the rules for sending mail and for collecting it. The social protocol is that everyone runs a machine with room to store their friends’ mail in transit, that people write to each other on reasonable topics, and that people read what they receive. Together these explain one message passing from one person to another in a few hops; spam is what emerged when millions did it.

So when you do the science, the analysis, and the whole rest of the cycle for e-mail, you look at what is happening and notice “Spam has happened, oh dear!” What went wrong? One of our social assumptions was wrong, namely that everybody is friendly and will only send email to another person when the other person wants to read it. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

Nobody, he notes, has succeeded in redesigning either half to make spam go away, and what is missing is the mathematics connecting the two levels.

15.6 A pamphlet so the meeting can go on

He sees two jobs for philosophy. One is legal: anyone can link to any page, but a lawyer may claim the right to charge for linking to theirs; then someone has to explain how a protocol defined over a name, a mere reference, grounds out in concepts lawyers understand. Here he adds that “Systems where you control names haven't worked so far”. The distinction matters: owning what a name means is not the same as controlling who may use or link to it, and that second kind of control, he says, has not worked. The other job is the request that closes the interview:

What I would like for philosophers to do is to work diligently and to produce very nice, simple, documents which describe to people like computer scientists how things work in a simple way. What happens when you click on a link? Quite a lot of that is philosophy. Philosophical Engineering and Ownership of URIs, 2013

He imagines someone in a working group objecting that a proposal does not match what they learned from Wittgenstein (a philosopher who held that meaning is use), and wants a pamphlet to hand them: enough shared understanding “so that we don't have to stop work for philosophical discussions”.

What happened since

  • The collection this appeared in was published. Philosophical Engineering: Toward a Philosophy of the Web, edited by Halpin and Monnin, appeared from Wiley-Blackwell in 2014.
  • The From line was closed by protocol, not by law. SPF (RFC 7208, 2014, revising a 2006 version), DKIM (RFC 6376, 2011) and DMARC (RFC 7489, 2015) let a receiving server check that mail bearing a domain in its From line was authorised by that domain. From February 2024 Gmail and Yahoo required this of bulk senders. The “form field” defence was answered technically, though spam persists.
  • The law-and-protocol gap remains. Disobeying a protocol is still not straightforwardly unlawful; computer-misuse law reaches it only through contested statutes on unauthorised access, and in Van Buren v. United States (2021) the US Supreme Court narrowed the Computer Fraud and Abuse Act.

Connections

Questions 4

15.2 The owner of a domain is said to have both a right and an obligation. What happens to the architecture when owners exercise the right and ignore the obligation?
