Chapter 78

Net Neutrality: Act Now

2017

Source Net Neutrality: Act Now, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2017. read the original

The short version

Days before the US regulator voted to drop its net neutrality rules, Berners-Lee warned that without them internet providers could “decide which companies succeed online, which voices are heard — and which are silenced”. He needed no one’s permission to launch the Web in 1989. Keeping carriers out of content, he argues, keeps both markets competitive: the one for connections and the one for websites.

The question

This is not a design note but a call to action, dated 10 December 2017, before the US Federal Communications Commission (FCC) voted on 14 December to remove the rules it defends. Should the company that carries a reader’s data be allowed to decide which sites reach the reader, and how fast?

78.1 No fee, no permission

Net neutrality is “the principle that internet service providers (ISPs) treat all traffic equally”: an ISP must not block sites, slow them down, or charge them to go faster. Berners-Lee makes the case from experience:

When I invented the World Wide Web in 1989, I didn't have to pay a fee, or ask anyone for permission to make it available over the internet. All I had to do was write a new app and plug my computer into the net. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

Then the cost:

If US net neutrality rules are repealed, future innovators will have to first negotiate with each ISP to get their new product onto an internet package. That means no more permissionless space for innovation. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

Permissionless (needing no one’s approval to launch) is the operative word: the same decentralisation Chapter 1 sets out as a founding principle (section 1.5). Figure 78.1 follows one newcomer through both worlds.

78.2 Two markets, kept apart

The core argument:

Net neutrality separates the connectivity market from the content market. As separate markets, both have flourished. But if the US allows the internet to become like the old cable TV model — with the same firms controlling the cables and the content — competition in both markets will suffer. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

Connectivity is what a customer pays an ISP for; content is what the customer reaches through it. Under the cable television model one firm controls both. Other countries keep the markets separate, and if the US does not, he warns, “America will decline as the world’s chief digital innovator”.

Three practices were ruled out: “under both Republican and Democratic leadership” the FCC told ISPs they could not engage in “blocking content, throttling site speed, or charging for content to be prioritised”.

78.3 Neutral by accident

The note explains why the rule came late:

In the early years of the internet, ISPs didn't have the technical capacity to discriminate traffic online. Their computers were not fast enough and so net neutrality was a fact of life. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

Neutrality was an accident of slow hardware. Faster machines and more valuable content gave ISPs the ability and the incentive to discriminate “to get a cut of the spoils”. Cases followed: in 2005 Madison River, a small North Carolina carrier, settled with the FCC after blocking Vonage’s internet phone calls, and in 2008 the FCC found that Comcast had interfered with BitTorrent file-sharing traffic (an order a court later overturned for lack of FCC authority).

Figure 78.3 shows how the accident became a rule.

78.4 What he wants

The protections, the note says, uphold free speech and the choice of what to read, which are “American values and fundamental to democracy”. Then the wish-list:

I want an internet where content businesses grow according to their quality, not their ability to pay to ride in the fast lane. I want an internet where ideas spread because they're inspiring, not because they chime with the views of telecoms executives. I want an internet where consumers decide what succeeds online, and where ISPs focus on providing the best connectivity. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

The instruction that follows: “act now. Not tomorrow, not next week. Now.”

78.5 Votes, not protocols

The last section, unusually, is about lobbying. He had been to Washington to meet “the FCC leadership and almost a dozen members of Congress from both parties”:

Some, more than others, understood the importance of preserving net neutrality. But they all understand how many votes they need to stay in office. Act now to save the internet as we know it, 2017

So the note asks readers to press Congress to call on FCC Chairman Ajit Pai to suspend the vote. Chapter 15 argued that every system has a technical protocol and a social one; here the same author works on the social protocol directly.

What happened since

  • The vote went the other way. The FCC voted to repeal the rules on 14 December 2017 (effective June 2018). Several states, including California in 2018, passed laws of their own.
  • Europe held the line. The European Union’s open internet regulation has applied since 2016 and was not repealed, so the separation of the two markets in Figure 78.2 persists there.
  • The rules came back and went again. They were readopted in April 2024 and struck down by the US Court of Appeals for the Sixth Circuit in January 2025, leaving no federal US neutrality rules. The question he asked readers to act on is still open.
