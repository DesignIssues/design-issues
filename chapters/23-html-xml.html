Chapter 23

HTML and XML

2008

Source HTML and XML, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2008-05-19. read the original

The short version

Most web pages are, strictly speaking, broken. Tags are left unclosed, nested wrongly, or misspelled. Browsers display them anyway, and have spent thirty years getting better at guessing what the author meant.

That was a decision, not an accident, and this note works out its consequences by asking a question nobody had asked: what is a page author actually rewarded for? The answer is a graph, and it turns the state of the Web from a mystery into an outcome.

The question

Written in August 2008 and, unusually, not made public until May 2019, this is the longest note in Volume III and the one in which Berners-Lee addresses a split in the Web he could not prevent. The occasion is stated without drama:

Occasionally it seems that some strategic thinking is necessary in order to ensure that the system as a whole will continue to work well and evolve smoothly. This is one of those times. HTML and XML, 2008

The eleven-year delay in publication is part of the document. By 2019 the question had been settled against the position argued here, and the note was released as a record rather than as an intervention.

23.2 Two philosophies of extension

The note is fairest to the branch it disagrees with. The case for centralised extension is stated properly: the modules anyone actually proposes adding to HTML can be counted on the fingers of one hand, so the design authority can simply add them. The list can be published, and every system is expected to know the specification anyway.

The case for the other branch is a requirement rather than a preference:

This is the requirement for a group to be able to define the terms involved in the new technology without having to get an audience with and agreement with central committee. HTML and XML, 2008

Berners-Lee’s examples of centralised extensibility are chosen to make the alternative feel antique: the Dewey decimal system, the Library of Congress cataloguing system, and the international telephone number space.

23.3 The robustness principle, reconsidered

The most valuable section reopens the tolerance principle of Chapter 1 and explains why a rule that worked for protocols failed for pages. The diagnosis has two parts, and neither is about technology.

It has not worked so well on the Web, because the Web page designers in fact paid no heed to being conservative. They were not in general engineers who had read the spec at all, but random people copying each other's Web pages, and seeing what worked when they modified them. HTML and XML, 2008

The second part is about time:

Once a Web page is out there with badly nested tags, it is out there for good. HTML and XML, 2008

The robustness principle assumes short-lived messages and a feedback loop between engineers. The Web has long-lived documents and no loop at all.

23.4 The incentive curve

Berners-Lee then does something rare in this collection: he analyses the system as one containing people with motives, and draws the reward for effort.

The remedy follows from the shape:

What does this mean? It means distinguishing more than the two possible outcomes of success and failure. We need to make a slope, so we need different levels. HTML and XML, 2008

His proposal is a browser toolbar that slides down on a page with errors, rates it out of a hundred, and lets interested users drill in. He anticipates the objection, which is that no browser wanted to be the one giving a worse experience, and answers it with an analogy to environmental cleanup: a browser without such a feature would come to look irresponsible. The word he uses for badly formed markup is pollution.

23.5 Scale-free vocabularies

A short section offers the best available explanation of why one markup language dominates and dozens do not. The Web is composed of communities of different sizes, with scale-free properties: there is no typical number of inbound links to a page, only a power law. Berners-Lee suggests, and marks as an unproved hunch, that this represents a form of optimal arrangement, perhaps the best trade-off between the ungainliness of one universal language and the agility of many small ones.

Applied to vocabularies, this gives a hierarchy rather than a competition. HTML sits at the largest scale, where tags are just tags and everyone is supposed to know them. Below it are languages serving very large communities, and below those the long tail. The argument for namespaces is that the tail needs them and the head does not.

23.6 The grouchy robustness principle

The note ends with a restatement of Postel’s rule, and it is the most quotable thing Berners-Lee wrote about specification practice:

Be conservative what you produce. Be liberal about what you accept but complain about any deviations from the spec in a way to help and to motivate the producers to adhere to it better. HTML and XML, 2008, published 2019

Accept everything, render everything, and say so. The complaint is the slope.

What happened since

  • The fork closed by one branch ending. W3C stopped work on XHTML 2 in 2009, and HTML5 became a Recommendation in October 2014. In 2019 W3C and the WHATWG agreed that the WHATWG Living Standard would be the single version of HTML.
  • The note was published at that moment. Its release in May 2019 coincides with the settlement, and the argument it makes was not adopted: HTML has no namespaces, and extension is centralised.
  • The slope was built, for developers only. Browser developer tools report markup errors in detail, and automated auditing tools score pages out of a hundred. Nothing slides down in front of an ordinary reader, which is the part of the proposal that would have changed authors’ incentives.
  • Decentralised extension arrived by another route. Custom elements require a hyphen in the name rather than a namespace, and structured data in pages uses vocabulary URIs. The requirement of section 23.2 was met without the mechanism this note defends.
