Chapter 79

New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful

2004

Source New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2004-04-01. read the original

The question

Written in April 2004 and, unusually for this collection, formally supported: the note records that the Technical Architecture Group resolved to support it, with one member abstaining and one recusing himself. It is the only chapter here with a recorded vote.

The occasion was proposals for new top-level domains, one of them intended for content for mobile devices. Berners-Lee’s objection to that one is architectural rather than commercial.

79.1 How the namespace stopped being hierarchical

The note begins with a history that explains the whole problem. The tree structure was an improvement over a flat list of machine names because it let new names be allocated in subdomains without a central registry, and because allocation grew rarer as one climbed towards the root, the cost stayed manageable.

Then names moved out of protocols and into speech, and Berners-Lee describes what that did:

It then became valuable to own a short domain name. This turned domain name space into a limited commodity. New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful, 2004

He also names an anomaly in the allocation rules that he treats as the source of much of the instability:

An anomaly was that unclaimed names were deemed to have no owner and no value, and were allocated in a "first come first served" frenzy in which speculators made great profits and held to ransom those who may have been considered the more logical owner of a name. New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful, 2004

79.2 The specific objection

The general costs apply to any new top-level domain. The particular objection to a domain for mobile content is different in kind, and it is the one that made this an architectural note rather than a policy one.

A page is a page. Chapter 10 established that one resource may be served in many forms, chosen by negotiation, and Chapter 2 established that the identifier names the thing rather than the rendering. A separate namespace for the same content in a different form does what the letter in Chapter 67 does: it puts a property of the delivery into the name of the thing.

The consequence is that a link becomes device-specific. A person on a small screen and a person on a large one, reading the same article, hold different addresses and cannot exchange them. That is the universality of Chapter 2 broken along a new axis, and Berners-Lee’s phrase for what it does is that it attacks the universality of the Web.

79.3 Who bears the cost of the root

The note is careful about the structure of ownership, and the description is still the clearest short account of it.

Each node in the tree is a potentially valuable asset, so control of any subset is valuable. Control of the whole is held by a non-profit institution intended to carry the trust of the entire community. Control of the large subtrees is delegated to registries whose nominal business is charging fees.

And then the temptation, recorded as rumour because that is what it was:

There have been temptations for the registry companies to consider themselves owners of unclaimed names. Rumors have abounded about systems which would automatically rent a domain name about which a potential renter was inquiring, or would redirect traffic from an unclaimed Web site to their own Web site, and so on. New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful, 2004

Both rumoured behaviours later happened. A party that operates a namespace and also sells names in it has an interest in what happens when you ask about a name, which is the single-point-of-control problem of Chapter 5 appearing at the one place the Web cannot route around.

79.4 Why a chapter for a proposal that failed

The value of the note is the general form of the argument. Adding a distinction to the top of a namespace does three things: it multiplies defensive work for everyone already in it, it hands value to whoever operates the new division, and it invites people to encode in the name a property that does not belong there.

That argument applies to package registries, to media types, to identifier schemes, and to every proposal to add a new top-level category to something already established. Chapter 4 makes it about schemes; this makes it about names.

What happened since

  • The mobile domain launched and failed. It was approved and delegated in 2005, never achieved wide use, and separate mobile addresses were abandoned in favour of responsive design serving one address to every device, which is the outcome section 79.2 argues for.
  • The namespace expanded enormously. The programme begun in 2012 added over a thousand new top-level domains, multiplying exactly the defensive registration burden described in Figure 79.1.
  • The rumoured behaviour happened. A registry introduced redirection of mistyped names to its own search page in 2003, and withdrew it after objection; the practice recurred at other levels.
  • The adult-content domain launched in 2011 and had the effect the objectors predicted, becoming a defensive registration exercise rather than a category.

Connections

The naming problem is Chapter 3, and the persistence proposal that would need the root’s cooperation is Chapter 13. The single centralised point is named in Chapter 5, and the equivalent argument about schemes is Chapter 4. Putting a delivery property into a name is Chapter 67.

Questions 4

79.1 The hierarchy exists in the specification and not in memory. Name another hierarchy with the same problem.

79.2 A device-specific address cannot be exchanged between devices. Give a modern case where an address is still specific to how it is being read.

79.3 A registry that sells names has an interest in your queries about them. Name the safeguard that prevents this, and say whether it is sufficient.

79.4 Apply the three-part argument of section 79.4 to a namespace you use. What would a new top-level category cost?
