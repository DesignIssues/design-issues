Chapter 79

New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful

2004

Source New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2004-04-01. read the original

The short version

Look up a restaurant on a laptop, bookmark it, and later, with only a phone to hand, open the bookmark to check the evening menu. In 2004 a proposed top-level domain, .mobi, would have given the phone’s menu a different address. Berners-Lee argues this breaks the Web, and that every new top-level domain costs name-holders money. (The title also names .xxx; the specific argument is about .mobi.)

In www.example.com, .com is the top-level domain and example the name rented beneath it. ICANN, a non-profit body, runs the root; fee-charging registries run each top-level domain.

The question

Should a fact about the reader’s device, such as being a phone, be written into a site’s name? Unusually for this collection, it was formally backed: on 14 May 2004 the W3C Technical Architecture Group resolved to support it.

79.1 How the namespace stopped being hierarchical

The note’s Example Manufacturing Company had used example.com “for many years”, then had to buy .net and .org to stop “unscrupulous competition” trading on its reputation, and later .fr and .co.uk. The tree was meant to let anyone create names beneath their own without asking a central registry. But once names appeared in advertisements and on business cards:

It then became valuable to own a short domain name. This turned domain name space into a limited commodity. New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful, 2004

Unclaimed names, he adds, were treated as having no owner and no value, and handed out in a “first come first served” frenzy in which “speculators made great profits”.

The cost of each new top-level domain

Each new top-level domain, the note says, has two effects.

The first effect is a little like printing more money. New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful, 2004

Every existing registration loses value while protecting a brand costs more: trivial for a large company, not for a small business, a charity or a family. The second effect is instability: a rush to reserve names, and a rash of attempts to take valuable ones.

79.2 One menu, two addresses

The Web works by reference: an address is bookmarked, emailed and passed on, then looked up later somewhere else, perhaps on a different device.

Dividing the Web into information destined for different devices, or different classes of user, or different classes of information, breaks the Web in a fundamental way. New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful, 2004

One resource may be served in many forms (Chapter 10); the name is for the thing, not the rendering (Chapter 2).

There was a precedent. Sites once marked “best viewed using screen set to 800x600”, the note observes, “now look terrible on a phone or, for that matter, on a much larger screen”.

Better ways to tell the server about the device

The tools already existed:

HTTP content negotiationThe serverThe phone says “small screen” with each request
CC/PP (Composite Capability/Preference Profiles)The serverThe device sends a fuller description of itself
CSS rules for the ‘handheld’ media typeThe browserOne page, with a different layout on small screens

And “mobile” is a moving target. Profiles such as the “Network PC” and the “Multimedia PC” had been “swept away”; small devices will have high bandwidth, and some phones will be mounted permanently on kitchen walls. A domain name is the worst place to record any of this. If the mobile providers honestly want mobility to be visible to users, the note says, they are “expecting to declare failure”:

It is failure when a communications system, in providing connectivity, becomes foremost in the user’s perceptions. A travel agent should be a travel business, not a “mobile business”. New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful, 2004

79.3 Who profits from an unclaimed name

When a new domain brings little benefit, the note looks for the incentive; given the money that has changed hands over names, “proposals from non-profit organizations would raise less suspicion.” As for the root:

The root of the domain name system is a single public resource, by design. Its control must be for and, indirectly, by the people as a whole. To give away a large chunk of this to a private group would be simply a betrayal of the public trust put in ICANN. New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful, 2004
There have been temptations for the registry companies to consider themselves owners of unclaimed names. Rumors have abounded about systems which would automatically rent a domain name about which a potential renter was inquiring, or would redirect traffic from an unclaimed Web site to their own Web site, and so on. New Top Level Domains .mobi and .xxx Considered Harmful, 2004

The note calls these rumours, but one had already happened. VeriSign’s Site Finder had redirected unregistered .com names in September 2003; in 2008 Network Solutions reserved names that people had searched for. This is the single point of control of Chapter 5: the one weak, central point in an otherwise decentralised system.

79.4 The same mistake, wherever names are shared

Adding a distinction to the top of any namespace multiplies defensive work for everyone already in it, hands value to whoever operates the new division, and invites a property into the name that does not belong there. The note ends plainly: “I urge ICANN not to create the ‘.mobi’ top level domain.” ICANN created it anyway, and the Web then settled on the note’s remedy: one address, with the layout adapted to the device.

What happened since

  • The mobile domain launched and faded. ICANN approved it in 2005. Separate mobile addresses gave way to responsive design (a term coined in 2010): one address for every device.
  • The mistake moved down a level. Many sites put their mobile layout on an m. host, such as en.m.wikipedia.org beside en.wikipedia.org. A link copied on a phone carries the m., so a desktop reader who opens it gets the cramped phone layout: Figure 79.2’s broken bookmark, one level down.
  • The namespace expanded enormously. The programme begun in 2012 added over a thousand top-level domains, multiplying the burden of Figure 79.1.
  • The adult-content domain launched in 2011. Trademark holders filed tens of thousands of paid applications simply to block their names from it.

Questions 4
