Chapter 52

Connecting the Sciences

2004

Source Connecting the Sciences, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2004-01-04. read the original

The short version

Tracing a disease outbreak can lead into weather records, company accounts and flight timetables, as well as biology and medicine. Each keeps its data in its own format, so the trail must be translated at every border, and agreeing one common format takes great time and effort.

The note argues for a third way: leave each field’s data as it is, and publish small machine-readable links where fields overlap. It also argues for open data, because much of the Web’s value came from uses nobody planned. If testing an odd idea takes moments, more odd ideas get tested.

The question

Written in January 2004. Scientific problems increasingly need several fields at once, and scientific information is naturally data (tables, rules, measurements), which makes science an obvious customer for the Semantic Web.

52.1 Fields have fuzzy edges

No scientific discipline, the note says, is an island:

The fields of study to which we give names have fuzzy edges, and overlap one another. They are in fact connected in a loose web which evolves with time, as new fields arise, and we change our perceptions of existing ones. Connecting the Sciences with the Semantic Web, 2004

The chain Berners-Lee offers runs from physics through physical chemistry, organic chemistry, cell biology, proteomics (the study of all the proteins in a cell or organism), genetics, epidemiology (the study of how diseases spread through populations), medicine and pharmacology. Nobody need be expert in all of them, but “one typically has to have a knowledge of neighboring fields”.

The cost of crossing a boundary

The search for cures for AIDS or cancer, or for a new virus such as SARS, traces pathways through many of these fields. But each field models its data its own way, so every idea that crosses fields means negotiating the conversion and transfer of data afresh.

A hypothetical example: two databases record the same protein under different names. Neither changes; someone publishes one machine-readable statement that the names are equivalent, and a program can now follow a question from one into the other. That statement is a mapping. So there are three routes: negotiate each conversion as it arises (a), agree a common format first (b), or leave the data alone and map the overlaps (c).

The note’s answer is route (c), which Berners-Lee calls retrospective and incremental (Figure 52.2). Retrospective: each field’s models and data already exist and stay as they are. Incremental: links are built one interface at a time, and no data model is redesigned at once. The overlap is partial, but worth linking where it exists.

52.2 The argument from serendipity

The catalogue precedent

The second half makes a case for openness that does not depend on altruism. In the Web’s early years companies hesitated to make their catalogues and parts lists public. The hesitation evaporated once it was clear that only companies whose product information was freely available on the Web were likely to do any business at all. Withholding had stopped being viable. Berners-Lee implies the same for scientific data, noting that funders already complained of the original data behind papers disappearing.

Making luck cheaper

The argument proper is about what the human-readable Web turned out to be for:

much of the benefit was serendipitous: information was used to advantage in ways that its publisher could never have imagined Connecting the Sciences with the Semantic Web, 2004

People searching for one answer often found another, sometimes to a different and equally pressing problem.

He then lists discoveries made by accident, from the bath of Archimedes through penicillin to Viagra (sildenafil, which was being trialled as a heart drug when its other effect was noticed), and draws the conclusion about machines:

If we are to make new discoveries using information on a huge scale, we will need to emulate the openness of the minds of these researchers by making scientific data available in a Semantic Web so that crazy hypotheses can be tested in a few moments harnessing data from many diverse fields. Connecting the Sciences with the Semantic Web, 2004

52.3 Science is not an island either

An epidemiological survey often yields results when joined with geographical and economic data, and the outbreak trail from the opening runs into weather, company accounts and flight timetables (Figure 52.3). So, the note insists, the scientific Semantic Web must be one interoperable part of the larger one, not a separate web of its own.

This is Chapter 51’s mixing of vocabularies, seen from the side of the person asking: one question, many vocabularies.

52.4 Getting the snowball rolling

How, the note asks, is one “to get the snowball rolling”? The tools existed: OWL, the Web Ontology Language, for writing vocabularies a machine can process, and RDF for publishing data that uses them. Three steps follow:

  • Basic ontologies for weights and measures, the periodic table, physical constants and simple molecules, useful to schools and to hazard, food and drug information.
  • Demonstrator datasets of general value, such as geophysical, meteorological, drug-interaction (“pharmaceutical incompatibility”) or genome data.
  • Anyone can begin: researchers, funders, journals or academies. Publication could reasonably be a condition of funding.

Openness brings problems, which he lists: anonymising clinical trial data, security-sensitive infrastructure information, and copyright in compilations. The lab notebook is a subtler case. It is “by tradition a write-only medium”: what was done, the conditions at the time, the results. Such detail usually fades, “but occasionally it becomes important after the fact”, and instruments aware of the Semantic Web might make it easier to record.

If its growth were like the early Web’s, he wrote, it would happen wherever one person understood the long-term global benefit and found a way to put in the short-term effort locally.

What happened since

  • The life sciences went furthest. Projects such as Bio2RDF, first published in 2008, republished many public biological databases as linked data, linked across databases.
  • The missing-data complaint became policy. PLOS required authors to share underlying data from 2014; the FAIR principles (Findable, Accessible, Interoperable, Reusable) appeared in 2016; the US National Institutes of Health’s data-sharing policy took effect in January 2023.
