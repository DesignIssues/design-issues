Chapter 51

Linked data is like a Bag of Chips

2010

Source Linked data is like a Bag of Chips, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2010-05-26. read the original

The short version

Suppose you have a table of something useful and you are willing to publish it. How much effort is worth putting in? Every extra step costs you something and the benefit goes to strangers.

This is the answer that persuaded governments: five stars, where the first is awarded simply for putting anything up at all. The second half of the chapter explains, using a packet of crisps, why you should never try to choose the one right vocabulary for your data.

The question

This began as a talk at a government open-data conference in May 2010 and became a Design Issues note much later. It contains the most successful piece of advocacy Berners-Lee ever produced, the five-star scheme, and one of his better metaphors, and the two do different jobs.

He records his own editorial process in the status line: on promoting the talk to a note he went looking for pictures of bags of chips and found recent ones that pretty much match the original.

51.1 Five stars

The scheme answers the question a publisher actually asks, which is not “what is the right way?” but “how far do I have to go?” Its design concedes that the first step is the hardest.

The fourth star is explained in terms of what it lets other people do, which is the only justification a publisher can act on:

That means people can point to it and say that city, actually when they say "Philadelphia" they mean the same thing as all these people mean when they say "Philadelphia". Linked Data is Like a Bag of Chips, 2010

51.2 The bag of chips

The metaphor answers a different question, put to him by developers: given that linked data means using vocabularies, how do you choose one?

The answer is that the question is wrong, because you do not choose one:

The art is to use several different ontologies in the same document, the same message. Linked Data is Like a Bag of Chips, 2010

A typical message carries terms at three scales: something universal, like a title; something shared with a domain, like a blood pressure; and something obscure, like the calibration data of the monitor that took it, understood only by device engineers. Mixing them gets the most total interoperability, because each term is understood by everyone who understands that scale.

51.3 Why the metaphor is doing real work

The rule of ignoring what you do not understand is the old rule of Chapter 16, which Volume III spent eleven chapters showing was inadequate for languages. Here it is exactly right, and the difference is worth naming.

For a document, ignoring an element you do not understand may reverse the meaning, which is why Chapter 18 asks for a way to mark something as mandatory. For a set of independent assertions, ignoring some of them cannot change what the rest say, because they were independent to begin with. That is the axiom of Chapter 27 paying off: a model of independent statements is one you can safely read half of.

The bag of chips works as a metaphor because a label is a set of independent claims. Nothing on it qualifies anything else.

What happened since

  • The five stars became policy. The scheme was adopted by government open data programmes internationally and is still the ordinary way publication quality is described.
  • Most data stopped at three stars. Comma-separated files on portals are the overwhelming majority of public open data, which is the level the scheme deliberately made respectable.
  • Mixing vocabularies became normal. Combining a general vocabulary with domain-specific terms in one message is now ordinary practice in structured data on pages, in scientific metadata and in health records.
  • The advocacy outlived the technology argument. The stars are quoted by people who have never used the data model they were written to promote, which is a fair measure of how well the framing worked.
