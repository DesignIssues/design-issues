Chapter 51

Linked data is like a Bag of Chips

2010

Source Linked data is like a Bag of Chips, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2010-05-26. read the original

The short version

Somebody holds a useful table and is willing to publish it. How much effort is it worth? Every extra step costs the publisher, and the benefit goes to strangers.

Berners-Lee’s answer, later widely adopted, is five stars, the first for putting anything up at all. To the usual objection, that the world will never agree one great taxonomy of everything, he answered with a packet of crisps (American “chips”): one label, several vocabularies.

The question

From a talk at Gov 2.0 Expo, May 2010; promoted to a Design Issues note, abstract added 2023.

51.1 One huge star for just putting it up

One huge star goes “just for getting that data up there at all”, because the hard part is people: the publisher’s own reluctance, and the bureaucracy around them. The scale answers the publisher’s real question, which is not what the right way is but how far to go.

Two stars if a program can pick out the values (a spreadsheet, not a scan of a fax). Three if the format belongs to nobody: CSV, not Excel. Four when each thing, and each property of it, gets a web address, a URI: a city gets one, and so do its population and its area. Then people “can point to it”: anyone who writes “Philadelphia” with that address means the same city as everyone else using it.

So two datasets using the same address for the city can be joined by a program. The fifth star is for doing the work of linking: noting, say, that one’s “population” means what somebody else’s does.

51.2 One packet, many languages

How should a developer pick a vocabulary? The answer began with a packet on a lunch table. Its name, “Crunchy Potato Chips”, was in American English; British English would say crisps. The nutrition facts are a US sub-language, one that “won’t allow you to sell this thing in Europe”. The barcode is “just one number”, but any till “pretty much anywhere in the world” reads it. People with serious allergies check the allergy information. And 110#71400 at the bottom was probably the printer’s batch number.

So the developer should not pick just one. An ontology, or vocabulary, is a published list of terms with agreed meanings, each with its own URI. In his words:

The art is to use several different ontologies in the same document, the same message. Linked Data is Like a Bag of Chips, 2010

A typical message mixes terms at three scales:

ScaleExampleWho understands it
UniversalThe title of a documentAlmost everyone
DomainA blood pressureEveryone in medicine
ObscureThe calibration data of the monitorDevice engineers only

A general program still finds the title; a medical one also finds the blood pressure. Some terms reach almost every system: given latitude and longitude, most can put things on a map.

Cherry-picking vocabularies

His example is a record of people entering buildings. Existing vocabularies cover the dates, people and buildings; only a security incident may need a new term.

Terms nobody else needs, like the printer’s number, the publisher simply makes up. No committee is needed, so someone buried in a large bureaucracy can get useful work done.

51.3 Why the metaphor is doing real work

What should a reader do with the printer’s number 110#71400? Software that checks data against a fixed form, a schema, may reject a field the form does not list, and might stop at it:

Ignoring is not always safe. A reader that drops an unknown “not” around an instruction does the reverse of what was meant, which is why Chapter 18 asks for a way to mark parts as mandatory.

On a label each line is a separate claim: skipping the batch number cannot change the calorie count. Linked data is likewise a set of independent statements, so a reader can safely use the half it understands.

51.4 Publish now, connect later

Finding no machine-readable nutrition terms on the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) website, he would write his own and ask the FDA to publish theirs. If it later did, one added statement, that his Calories Per Serving “means exactly the same thing” as theirs, would let queries in the FDA’s terms find all his existing data.

“So it’s not a top-down system.” Anyone can look a term up and pick a source they respect; in his words:

It is little pieces loosely joined. Linked Data is Like a Bag of Chips, 2010

The talk ends on the sceptic who says nobody will get the whole world “to agree about a great big taxonomy of everything”, and on the reply: “Actually, no, it’s not like that. It’s like a bag of chips!”

What happened since

  • The five stars became policy. The UK government’s Open Data White Paper, Unleashing the Potential (June 2012), used the five-star scale to describe the quality of data it wanted departments to publish.
  • Shared vocabularies arrived. Bing, Google and Yahoo launched schema.org, a common vocabulary for web pages, in June 2011.
