Chapter 51

Linked data is like a Bag of Chips

2010

Source Linked data is like a Bag of Chips, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2010-05-26. read the original

The short version

Somebody holds a table of something useful and is willing to publish it. How much effort is it worth putting in? Every extra step costs the publisher something, and the benefit goes to strangers.

The answer, later widely adopted (see What happened since), is five stars, the first of them awarded simply for putting anything up at all. The second half of the chapter explains, by way of a packet of crisps (American “chips”), why nobody should try to pick the one right vocabulary for a body of data.

The question

This began as a talk at a government open-data conference in May 2010 and was later promoted to a Design Issues note; an abstract was added in 2023. It contains the five-star scheme, widely adopted since, and one of his better metaphors, and the two do different jobs.

51.1 Five stars

The scheme answers the question a publisher actually asks, which is not “what is the right way?” but “how far do I have to go?” Its design concedes that the first step is the hardest.

Some glosses. Machine readable means a program can pick out the values, as in a spreadsheet. A proprietary format belongs to one company, as Excel’s does; CSV is plain text with commas between the values. A URI is a web address. At four stars a city gets one, and so do its population and its area, so that others can refer to them:

That means people can point to it and say that city, actually when they say "Philadelphia" they mean the same thing as all these people mean when they say "Philadelphia". Linked Data is Like a Bag of Chips, 2010

So two datasets using the same address for the city can be joined by a program. The fifth star is for linking outwards to somebody else’s data.

51.2 The bag of chips

The metaphor answers a question developers ask: linked data means using vocabularies, so how is one chosen? It is not:

The art is to use several different ontologies in the same document, the same message. Linked Data is Like a Bag of Chips, 2010

An ontology, or vocabulary, is a published list of terms with agreed meanings, each with its own URI: title, blood pressure, calories per serving. A typical message mixes terms at three scales:

ScaleExampleWho understands it
UniversalThe title of a documentAlmost everyone
DomainA blood pressureEveryone in medicine
ObscureThe calibration data of the monitorDevice engineers only

Using each at its own scale gets the most total interoperability. The packet on his lunch table showed the same: its name was American English (in British English, he noted, it would say potato crisps), and the rest was in other languages again.

Borrowing terms

His working example is a record of people entering buildings. Existing vocabularies already cover dates, people and buildings; only the idea of a security incident may need a new term.

Terms nobody else needs, like the printer’s number, the publisher simply makes up. No committee is needed, which, he says, lets someone buried in a large bureaucracy get useful work done.

51.3 Why the metaphor is doing real work

What should a reader do with the printer’s number 110#71400? Many programs check incoming data against a fixed form, a schema, and reject anything with a field the form does not list. Such software would stop at it:

Ignoring is not always safe. In a document one element can qualify another: a reader that drops an unknown “not” around an instruction carries out the reverse of what was meant, which is why Chapter 18 asks for a way to mark parts as mandatory.

On a label each line is a separate claim. Skipping the batch number cannot change what the calorie count says. Linked data is likewise a set of independent statements, so a reader can safely use the half it understands.

51.4 Publish now, connect later

Finding no machine-readable nutrition terms on the Food and Drug Administration (FDA) website, he would write his own, use them, and ask the FDA to publish theirs.

Because terms are URIs, anyone can look one up and pick a source they respect. His conclusion:

So it's not a top-down system. Linked Data is Like a Bag of Chips, 2010
It is little pieces loosely joined. Linked Data is Like a Bag of Chips, 2010

What happened since

  • The five stars became policy. The UK government’s Open Data White Paper, Unleashing the Potential (June 2012), used the five-star scale to describe the quality of data it wanted departments to publish.
  • Shared vocabularies arrived. Bing, Google and Yahoo launched schema.org, a common vocabulary for web pages, in June 2011. W3C’s Data Catalog Vocabulary (DCAT), a Recommendation since January 2014, borrows Dublin Core terms rather than inventing its own.
  • The note was updated. An abstract was added in 2023, and beside the vocabulary search engines named in the talk, Swoogle and Sindice, it now points to LOV.
