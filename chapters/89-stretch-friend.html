Chapter 89

A "Stretch Friend"

2017

Source A "Stretch Friend", a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2017-01-01. read the original

The short version

Social networks suggest new friends who are friends of existing friends. The suggestions are usually welcome, but they close people into tight groups where everyone knows everyone, and the Web’s power to connect distant people goes unused.

The note proposes the stretch friend: now and then, suggest someone like the user in every way but one, such as race, religion or where they live. It cites unchecked evidence that people with no contact across race were the ones who could not imagine a black president, and suggests simply trying the idea.

The question

Children who can play an online game with anyone logged on anywhere in the world end up playing with the child next door, or even the one at the other end of the sofa. To Berners-Lee this showed that the Web’s most celebrated power was going unused. This note, dated 2011 and left as a first draft, sets out a small fix, which he also presented in talks, including his 2012 WWW conference keynote. Quotations keep the draft’s typing errors.

We celebrate the web as a space which allows you to discover all kinds of new things people and places. We admire it as something which breaks down geographical barriers, allowing me to interact with someone on the other side of the world as easily as with the person next door. But are we really using that aspect, of it? Stretch friends, 2011

89.1 Friends of friends: why suggestions fold inward

Social networking sites suggest people a user might know, usually like this:

The simple way xdoing this is just to pick people who are friend s of our existing friends. We are almost sure to like them, especialy if we have a lot of friends in common. Stretch friends, 2011

The trouble is that it works:

So what happens if we accept these new proposed friends? Out social network becomes a solid mass of mutually connected people. We make cliques. Stretch friends, 2011

A clique is a closed group in which everyone knows everyone else. Each accepted suggestion closes a triangle (Figure 89.1), and the result, in the note’s words, “doesn’t in fact add new social experience”.

The note cites Eli Pariser’s The Filter Bubble (2011): a filter bubble is a feed that shows a person only people and views like their own. Back to the children on the sofa, the note asks:

This is good for the cohesion of the neighborhood, but is it good for the planet? Stretch friends, 2011

89.2 The proposal

Now and then, instead of a friend of a friend, the system would suggest someone almost identical to the user who differs in one respect, such as race or where they live. The note’s own example:

You are a white male liberal geek in New York and you are offered a black male liberal geek in New York or maybe a white male liberal geek in Saudi Arabia. Stretch friends, 2011

Only one boundary is crossed at a time:

Just one at a time. Maybe more later, but for now, let's see how you get on. Can you put the effort in to talk to someone whose life or beliefs or interests differ along one axis? Stretch friends, 2011

The note closes the proposal on its stakes:

If you could, and lots of people had stretch friends, would the world be an incrementaly better place, or maybe after a while a dramatically different place? Stretch friends, 2011

89.3 Who could not imagine a black president

The note’s main evidence is an analysis the author marked “@@check details”, meaning he had not yet verified it. Nate Silver, in a TED talk on the election which first elected Barack Obama, asked who did not shift to the liberal side, and then who those people were (Figure 89.3):

Well, they were by strong correlation the people who answered "no" to the question, "Have you ever lived or worked with someone of a different race". Stretch friends, 2011

He generalises:

So human beings have an ability to mark people outside their normal zone as being strange, or foreign, or enemy. We have evolved the neurology to become tribal at the drop of a hat Stretch friends, 2011

He points to the “rather horrid” experiments in which school children wore badges showing their eye colour (he left the reference blank; probably Jane Elliott’s 1968 classroom exercise). If one difference can switch people into tribal mode, he concludes, breaking such differences down becomes a matter of urgency.

89.4 Or we could just try it

The note ends without a worked-out scheme, linking the idea to twinning projects, in which towns or schools pair up with partners in very different parts of the world, with a wry aside: “(Of course when these are arranged by churches, they don’t always cross religious boundaries!)” The closing line is an argument about method:

Web scientists, people who analyze these things, could do lots of math to try work out what the effect would be. Or we could just try it. Stretch friends, 2011

What happened since

  • The diagnosis was studied at scale. Homophily, connecting with people like oneself, became a central topic; in 2015 Bakshy, Messing and Adamic (Science) measured how far Facebook users’ friends and choices narrowed their exposure to opposing news.
  • The contact hypothesis held up. Gordon Allport’s 1954 idea that contact across a difference reduces prejudice, the mechanism this proposal relies on, was supported by Pettigrew and Tropp’s 2006 review of over 500 studies, with conditions attached.
  • Exposure to difference can backfire. Bail and colleagues (PNAS, 2018) paid Twitter users to follow a bot retweeting the other political side; Republicans in particular became more polarised. Such encounters are adversarial rather than acquainted, a distinction the note does not draw.
  • The cited talk can be checked. The analysis the note flagged “@@check” is Silver’s TED2009 talk, “Does racism affect how you vote?”, which can be watched against the note’s summary.

Questions 4

89.1 List your last ten conversations. Along how many axes did the other person differ from you, and which one axis would you find hardest to cross?

89.2 Why might the note limit the stretch to one axis? Give a reason the note does not state.

89.3 A stretch friend is offered as an acquaintance; the 2018 Twitter bot was an opponent. Design a suggestion that would make the first encounter acquainted rather than adversarial.
