Chapter 21

How to write a Specification

1999

Source How to write a Specification, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 1999-05-24. read the original

The question

Berners-Lee describes this note twice as folklore, and both times slightly apologetically: it is, he says, a little motherhood and apple pie about how a specification should be couched. He credits Peggie Rimmer at CERN and years of work with the IEEE, the IETF and W3C, and assumes others have written books on it.

They have, and this is still worth two pages, because it makes one structural point that longer treatments bury.

21.1 A specification has two halves

A technical specification defines something. The document must specify the thing being defined as well as give its definition: a “left hand side” as well as a “right hand side”. Both must be done quite precisely. The essentials of a specification, 1999

Most specifications spend their length on the right-hand side and leave the left implicit. The note’s contribution is to insist that the thing being defined is itself a thing to be defined, and that it should get a name.

21.2 Meaning, not behaviour

The sharpest rule in the note is about the grammatical mood a specification should be written in. When defining a language, say what a construct means rather than what software should do when it meets one. His example is a cheque:

“x” indicated that the check is void The essentials of a specification, 1999

is better than the version that says the cheque should be rejected with a fatal error. The justification is a claim about what an author is entitled to do:

You can tell people what something means, you can't tell them what to do about it, unless you are defining a protocol. The essentials of a specification, 1999

Typical behaviours may still be described, and Berners-Lee says they can be very useful for explaining intent, provided they are marked non-normative. The distinction survives because meaning outlasts implementations: a specification that says what to do has to be rewritten each time the software changes shape.

Protocols are the exception, and for them he asks for something stricter still: constraints given as a state transition diagram or table, so that they are totally clear.

21.3 Two paragraphs nobody expects

Two short passages lift this note above a style guide.

The first is about contracts. When a message binds human parties, the specification must say so, and Berners-Lee gives the reason without softening it: you could end up in court explaining it. He then writes out a fragment of the kind of prose required, naming a debtor, a creditor and how each is identified. This is Chapter 20’s argument arriving as drafting advice.

The second is a checklist for borrowing. When part of a specification is deliberately made similar to another, the note asks the author to say that the similarity was noticed, to say whether it is exact and where it is not, to make clear which document is definitive and which is a copy for information, to say whether this use will track new versions of the referenced document, and to consider whether such changes could break the system. The last item is the one people skip: if necessary, negotiate constraints with the authority for changes to the referenced specification.

21.4 The four test questions

The note ends with questions to ask of a draft, and they are short enough to use.

QuestionWhat a failure means
Does the spec decide, for any arbitrary object, whether the conformance term applies?The left-hand side is undefined
Could you write a program to test conformance?The definition is not operational
Is conformance alone enough to make systems work as intended?Something necessary is unstated
Can you prove important properties from the state transition tables?The protocol is under-specified

The third is the one most drafts fail. A specification can be perfectly decidable and still describe something that does not work, because the requirement that made it work was left in somebody’s head.

What happened since

  • The vocabulary became universal. The requirement keywords this note points at, from Bradner’s BCP 14 of March 1997, are now cited by nearly every protocol specification, and were refined by RFC 8174 in May 2017 to apply only when written in capitals.
  • Conformance terms became formal. Media types and namespace URIs are now the ordinary way to name what conforms, and the registration processes for both require the definition the left-hand side demands.
  • Testability became the standard. W3C process has required implementation evidence and test suites for advancement since the 2000s, which is the second test question turned into a gate.
  • The meaning-not-behaviour rule lost ground. HTML5 and its successors specify algorithms rather than meanings, deliberately, because interoperability on error handling mattered more than portability of the definition. Chapter 23 is where that choice is argued.

Questions 4

21.1 Take a specification you rely on and find its conformance term. If there is none, write one.

21.2 Rewrite one requirement from that specification so that it states a meaning rather than a behaviour. What is lost?

21.3 The third test question asks whether conformance alone makes a system work. Give a case where a conforming implementation is nonetheless unusable.

21.4 A specification copies a definition from another. Apply the five-point checklist of section 21.3 to a case you know, and say which points were missed.
