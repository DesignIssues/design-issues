Chapter 67

"HTTPS Everywhere" considered harmful

2015

Source "HTTPS Everywhere" considered harmful, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2015-03-28. read the original

The short version

The letter s in https. It means the connection to the site is encrypted, and a generation of readers has been taught to look for it.

This chapter argues that putting that letter in the address was a mistake, and that correcting it by moving the whole Web into a second address space did more damage than anything else in the Web’s history. The author is not arguing against encryption. He is arguing about where the fact belongs.

The question

Chapter 5 recorded a complaint, in 1998, that security should have been negotiated within one namespace rather than forking it. Seventeen years later Berners-Lee returns to the same decision, with the benefit of knowing how it turned out, and makes the strongest claim in the collection about what was at stake.

The title of the note is a deliberate provocation and the argument beneath it is careful. He is not against encryption. He is against putting the letter in the identifier.

67.1 The decision, reconstructed

Two secure versions of the protocol were proposed in the mid-1990s, and one prevailed. Within it there was a further choice: extend the existing protocol, or give the secure version its own identifier prefix.

Berners-Lee reconstructs the arguments as a table, and insists on the frame that makes the trade legible:

When you look at that design choice, you have to remember that the URL is being used to communicate between two people, for example, the person who writes the link containing the href with the link, and the person who later sees the link and clicks ion it. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

67.2 What changed

Three things, and the third is the one that settles it.

People became individually aware and individually under attack, so protecting the follower became the browser’s job rather than the publisher’s. People learned to look not at the letter in the address but at the padlock, and then at the name of the certificate holder. And then the address itself began to disappear:

The 'http:' has in fact (2015) relatively recently been removed from the browser bar altogether for some browsers. While this infuriates those of us who are actually interested in it, it certainly puts another nail in the coffin of the idea that the 's' in the URI is important part of the architecture. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

If the letter is not shown, it cannot be doing the job it was added for.

67.3 The charge

The central claim is made twice and is not hedged:

The problem is of course that moving things from http: space into https space, whether or not you keep the rest of the URI the same, breaks any links to. Put simply, the HTTPS Everywhere campaign taken at face value completely breaks the web. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

And then the comparison that makes it the strongest sentence in Volume VII:

In a way it is arguably a greater threat to the integrity for the web than anything else in its history. The underlying speeds of connection of increased from 300bps to 300Gbps, IPv4 has being moved to IpV6, but none of this breaks the web of links in so doing. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

Every other change to the infrastructure was invisible to the links. This one was not, because the security level was put where the identity lives, which is what Chapter 2’s opacity axiom forbids.

67.4 The single-valued trust mistake

A parenthesis in the middle contains a generalisation worth more than the argument it interrupts:

It has been more any more clear that we and our systems should not just trust things or not trust them, or even to trust them on a scale form 0 to 1. We trust different people for different things. We trust one person for recommendations on food, and another for movies, and to muddle these trusts could be disastrous. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

A boundary between a secure region and an insecure one implements single-valued trust. And Berners-Lee gives the diagnostic symptom that reveals the mistake in any system:

A symptom if that you can never find the perfect place to put that boundary. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

If a boundary keeps needing to be moved, the thing it separates was not two kinds of thing. That test applies far beyond this argument.

67.5 The proposal

The alternative is in the note’s own title. Encrypt everything, at the protocol level, and stop marking it in the identifier. Upgrade in real time, or try the encrypted version first. Show the assurance in the interface, where people already look, and let the prefix be phased out.

Berners-Lee is precise about what users should then be taught to understand: the level of assurance being given, including authentication of the other party, encryption of the communication, and the anonymity, traceability or strong authentication of the user to the other party. Four separate properties, which one letter cannot convey.

What happened since

  • The campaign won completely. The encrypted prefix became the default, browsers began marking the plain one insecure from July 2018, and most of the Web migrated.
  • The breakage was real and was absorbed. Redirects and upgrade headers repaired most links, at the cost of a permanent layer of rewriting, which is the tax this note predicted.
  • The upgrade mechanism the note prefers exists and lost. RFC 2817’s in-band upgrade was available and was not adopted; the industry chose the separate space.
  • The single-valued trust point was vindicated elsewhere. Fine-grained permissions replaced binary trust in nearly every other part of the platform, from device access to storage, which is section 67.4’s argument accepted everywhere except here.

Connections

Questions 4
