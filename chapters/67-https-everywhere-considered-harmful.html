Chapter 67

"HTTPS Everywhere" considered harmful

2015

Source "HTTPS Everywhere" considered harmful, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2015-03-28. read the original

The short version

Web addresses begin http: or https:; the s means the connection is encrypted. Berners-Lee argues that encryption should happen on every connection, invisibly, as faster modems did. Changing the s changes the address, and old links to the other address break.

He is not arguing against encryption. He is arguing that the fact “this connection is secure” does not belong in the name of a page.

The question

Chapter 5 recorded a complaint, in 1998, that security should have been negotiated within one set of names rather than splitting it in two. Seventeen years later Berners-Lee returns to the same decision, knowing how it turned out, and makes the strongest claim in the collection about what was at stake.

The title of the note is a provocation; the argument beneath it is careful.

67.1 The decision, reconstructed

A protocol is the set of rules a browser and a server follow to talk to each other; HTTP is the Web’s. Every address begins with a prefix (also called a scheme), such as http:, naming the protocol used to fetch it. In the mid-1990s two secure versions of HTTP were proposed, S-HTTP and HTTP-S, and HTTP-S prevailed. That left a further choice: extend the existing protocol behind the existing http: prefix, or give the secure version a prefix of its own, https:.

Berners-Lee reconstructs the arguments as a table, and reminds us that a link passes between two people: whoever writes it and whoever later clicks it.

When you look at that design choice, you have to remember that the URL is being used to communicate between two people, for example, the person who writes the link containing the href with the link, and the person who later sees the link and clicks ion it. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

67.2 What changed

Three things, and the third settles it.

  1. Users came under attack individually, so protecting the person who follows a link became the browser’s job rather than the publisher’s.
  2. People learned to look not at the s in the address but at the padlock, and then at the name of the certificate holder shown by the browser.
  3. Browsers stopped showing the prefix at all.
The 'http:' has in fact (2015) relatively recently been removed from the browser bar altogether for some browsers. While this infuriates those of us who are actually interested in it, it certainly puts another nail in the coffin of the idea that the 's' in the URI is important part of the architecture. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

If the letter is not shown, it cannot be doing the job it was added for.

67.3 The charge

The central claim is made twice and is not hedged:

The problem is of course that moving things from http: space into https space, whether or not you keep the rest of the URI the same, breaks any links to. Put simply, the HTTPS Everywhere campaign taken at face value completely breaks the web. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

http://x.org/report and https://x.org/report are different names. A link written years ago keeps pointing at the old one.

Then comes the comparison that makes it the strongest sentence in Volume VII:

In a way it is arguably a greater threat to the integrity for the web than anything else in its history. The underlying speeds of connection of increased from 300bps to 300Gbps, IPv4 has being moved to IpV6, but none of this breaks the web of links in so doing. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

The security level was put where the identity lives. Chapter 2’s opacity axiom says an address is a sealed label: it names a thing, and nothing else should be read from its letters. The s breaks that rule.

67.4 The single-valued trust mistake

Once https: was treated as a safe space, browsers ring-fenced it. Under the Same Origin Policy, everything loaded into an https: page must itself arrive securely, which, the note says, “has led to a class of web apps being broken”. A parenthesis that follows draws the general lesson:

It has been more any more clear that we and our systems should not just trust things or not trust them, or even to trust them on a scale form 0 to 1. We trust different people for different things. We trust one person for recommendations on food, and another for movies, and to muddle these trusts could be disastrous. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

A wall between a secure region and an insecure one implements single-valued trust, and Berners-Lee gives the symptom that reveals the mistake in any system:

A symptom if that you can never find the perfect place to put that boundary. Web Security - TLS Everywhere, not https: URIs, 2015

If a boundary keeps needing to be moved, the thing it separates was not two kinds of thing. That test applies far beyond this argument.

67.5 The proposal

The alternative is in the note’s own title. TLS (Transport Layer Security) is the encryption layer that https: uses. Use it everywhere, inside the protocol, and stop marking it in the address. A connection can be upgraded to TLS as it is made, an in-band upgrade already described in 2000 in an RFC (one of the Internet’s numbered standards documents), or the browser can simply try the encrypted version first. Show the assurance in the browser, where people already look, and let the prefix be phased out.

Users should then be taught to understand the level of assurance being given, which has three parts:

  1. authentication of the other party (is this really the bank?);
  2. encryption of the communication;
  3. how the user appears to the other party, which ranges from anonymous, through traceable, to strongly authenticated.

One letter cannot convey any of these on its own terms, let alone all three.

What happened since

  • The note’s target. A footnote makes clear that it criticises the general drive for https: addresses everywhere, not the EFF’s HTTPS Everywhere browser extension of the same name. The note also names Let’s Encrypt, a project for free, automatic certificates, which began issuing them in late 2015.
  • The campaign won completely. The https: prefix became the default, Chrome 68 began marking plain http: pages “Not secure” in July 2018, and most of the Web migrated.
  • The breakage was real and was absorbed. Redirects, HSTS (RFC 6797, 2012, which tells browsers to rewrite a site’s http: links to https:) and the upgrade-insecure-requests directive repaired most links. They are a permanent layer of rewriting, the breakage the note warned of.
  • The upgrade mechanism the note prefers existed and lost. RFC 2817’s in-band upgrade was available and was not adopted; the industry chose the separate space.
