Chapter 20

The Stack of Specifications

2002

Source The Stack of Specifications, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2002-07-29. read the original

The short version

A forged From line on an email is a lie. An Ethernet packet has a slot saying “what follows is IP”; IP has one saying “what follows is TCP”; and so on up to the From line. Plugging in commits a computer to the whole chain, so each slot has an agreed meaning. Higher up, central lists give way to URIs anyone can create. Quoting lets a sender pass something on without claiming it.

The question

“Bits mean something.” Someone who forges the From line of an email has, Berners-Lee argues, made a misrepresentation he compares to forging a cheque, because by plugging in they agreed to specifications that fix what that line means. The note walks from an Ethernet cable up to a bank statement to show why. It began as his keynote at the World Wide Web conference in Hawai’i in May 2002.

20.1 One commitment, then no more choices

Suppose Alice walks into a meeting room and is handed an Ethernet cable. To use it, her computer must send packets, small blocks of data laid out as the Ethernet specification says. A field in each packet, a slot at a fixed place, names the specification that governs the rest. Committing to the cable commits her computer to everything above it (Figure 20.1). The cable’s packets carry the code 0x0800, meaning “an Internet packet”. Further up, a port number says which service a connection is for, from a list kept by IANA, the Internet Assigned Numbers Authority.

Each specification also sets rules of behaviour, and each can be cheated. When two computers transmit on the cable at once (a collision), each backs off for a random time; one that always picks the shortest wait crowds the others out, “just like people who always seem to be the one talking in a meeting”.

20.2 Why this is an argument about law

At the top of the chain sits the From line. Put a false name there and the message, the note says, “sneaks past their personal email filtering, and also any automated filtering, tricking them into reading a message”, using up “a person’s time, energy, bandwidth and disk space” for the sender’s gain. The note debunks the idea that “its Ok to interpret things this way as more and more people are doing it”:

The Internet specifications, to which any Internet user implicitly agrees in using the Internet at all, define what the fields in an email message mean. To put incorrect information in these fields is to make a misrepresentation, just as it would have been in any other medium. It should be subject to the same penalties as lying or fraud in any other medium. The Stack of Specifications, 2002

The stakes have changed:

When the Internet was young and used by research institutions, its misuse would inconvenience other users and lead to reprobation and the disdain of one's peers. Now that the Internet is such as large force in society, it is possible to make a lot of money and create a lot of damage by protocol abuse. You can compare a lie in an internet message, depending on how it is done, to forging a check, connecting to the electricity supply the other side of the meter, or to poisoning the water supply. Society must therefore be careful to be absolutely clear about the illegality of such misuse. The Stack of Specifications, 2002

20.3 From a Web address to a bank statement

A Web page follows the same chain (Figure 20.2). The http: at the front of an address is its scheme; a public list says it means HTTP. HTTP says to connect to port 80. The reply carries a Content-Type label, such as text/html for a page a person reads, or OFX (Open Financial Exchange, a bank-statement format) for a program.

Neither party can plead ignorance:

So long as the writers of the specification do a good job (and goodness knows they work hard enough at it) then there can be no argument as to what the actual fields in your bank statement mean. The Stack of Specifications, 2002

And the server speaks for whoever owns the address: “The server is the agent of the publisher.”

20.4 The registry that is not a registry

The walk then reaches XML, and a change in kind. A document sent as plain application/xml is read according to the namespace of its outermost element: a URI naming the vocabulary in use. Every list below this point is kept by one authority. A namespace is not: a shop can create, or mint, http://example.org/ns/invoice# tomorrow without asking anyone, and put a page there explaining its terms.

That page may be a specification for people, a schema for machines, or both. A P3P privacy policy, for example, is XML that a browser can scan more easily than a person can read one, and it works only if its terms are well defined. The note flags, without explaining, that “this doesn’t always happen”, naming MathML and XHTML sent as XML.

Berners-Lee calls these “three stages of civilization”: numbers, which need a central registry; URIs, which anyone with a little domain space can mint; and URIs whose definitions can be fetched from the Web. Central lists suited the few early protocols, and strained as protocols multiplied: he cites the “social difficulties of getting an IANA allocation for a MIME type” for an early W3C specification.

In RDF, a W3C language for data, each statement or triple gives a thing, a property and a value (a hat, its colour, blue256), and the property is named by a URI such as http://www.w3.org/2002/05/example#color.

And whoever made up the URI gets to say what the property means, that is, what any triple using that property means. The Stack of Specifications, 2002

Define that property to take Pantone colour names, and an order for a hat in blue256 cannot be read as any other blue.

20.6 When the chain does not connect

Many protocols also have a way to break the chain, passing something on without meaning it:

Understanding the exceptions is as important as understanding the general rule. The Stack of Specifications, 2002

In email it is the attachment, used only as the cover note says: “Look at this stupid program - but whatever you do don't run it!” In Notation3, a text syntax for RDF, curly brackets quote: {my:car srgb:color "000044"} mentions the claim that the car is dark blue without making it. Forwarding a scam email to warn a friend works the same way: its claims travel quoted, unmade.

The fact that you can do this removes the last excuse for anyone claiming not to have meant whatever they did say in the main message! The Stack of Specifications, 2002

What happened since

  • The legal argument was partly taken up. The EU ePrivacy Directive (2002/58/EC, 2002) barred marketing email that hides its sender, and the US CAN-SPAM Act (2003) outlawed misleading header information: narrower, and on different grounds, but aimed at the same lie.
  • A technical answer followed. Sender authentication, told in Chapter 15, made the From field checkable rather than merely defined.
  • The namespace-as-registry idea half worked. Anyone may mint a namespace URI, and as of 2026 many serve nothing at it. HTML, as Chapter 23 describes, dropped namespaces altogether.
