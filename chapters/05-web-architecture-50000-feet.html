Chapter 5

Web Architecture from 50,000 feet

1998

Source

This chapter is about Web Architecture from 50,000 feet, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, dated 1998-09-04, about 7,442 words.

Read the original at https://www.w3.org/DesignIssues/Architecture.html. The Design Issues notes are Tim Berners-Lee's personal notes and are not endorsed by W3C. This book is commentary; where it quotes, it attributes.

The question

This is the survey, and Berners-Lee explains at the start why he resisted writing it for so long:

I have resisted the urge, and requests, to try to write an architecture for a long time: This was from a feeling that a dead and therefore less valuable document must any attempt to select which, of all the living ideas, seem most stable, logically connected and essential. Web Architecture from 50,000 feet, 1998

The sentence is mangled and the meaning is clear: fixing a living thing in one document kills part of it. He wrote it anyway, presented it to the Consortium's Advisory Committee and chairs, and kept revising it into 1999 and beyond. It is the closest the collection comes to a single statement of the whole design, and it is the right chapter to read before the nine volumes that follow, because almost every one of them expands a paragraph of it.

5.1 The plan of the argument

Berners-Lee states his definition of the subject plainly: the Web is a universe of network-accessible information. He then divides its potential in two, and the division organises the rest of the note and much of this book. The Web is first a means of human-to-human communication, and second a space in which software agents can reach enough of what society and science contain to become tools that work alongside us.

He treats the properties of the space itself first, then its use as a human medium, then its use as a medium for machine reasoning. Two commitments are declared to be assumed throughout rather than argued: interoperability, and universality of access irrespective of hardware, software, network, language, culture, location, or physical or mental impairment. He notes that these permeate the work so thoroughly that they cannot be located in any one section.

5.2 Why URIs are the foundation

The most fundamental specification, he says, is also one of the simpler ones. The argument for its primacy is not about naming but about composition:

Great multiplicative power of reuse derives from the facts that all languages use URIs as identifiers: This allows things written in one language to refer to things defined in another language. Web Architecture from 50,000 feet, 1998

A language that adopts URIs inherits, without specifying any of it, whatever the URI specification says about persistence, identity and equivalence. The reference to the URI specification is itself a flexibility point: naming and addressing properties can be defined once and revised separately from every language that uses them.

The guidance that follows is terse and has held up. Respect the generic syntax. Do not introduce new schemes without due cause. Do not introduce a rival scheme presenting itself as a superset of URIs, since that would make a reference require something more than a URI. Give every significant object with persistent identity a URI. Where a reference is needed, use a URI and do not constrain which scheme.

5.3 The centralised part

Having praised decentralisation, Berners-Lee names the exception, and does so without softening it:

The Achilles' heel of the HTTP space is the only centralized part, the ownership and government of the root of the DNS tree. As a feature common and mandatory to the entire HTTP Web, the DNS root is a critical resource whose governance by and for the world as a whole in a fair way is essential. Web Architecture from 50,000 feet, 1998

He adds that the Consortium did not address this concern, except indirectly through involvement with ICANN. The admission is characteristic of the collection: a structural weakness is identified, its owner named, and no solution offered.

On persistence he is equally direct. Improving the persistence of HTTP URIs is a matter of tool maturity, user education and the maturity of Web society, and the moving of resources is strongly discouraged. The existence of research into naming schemes should not be taken to mean that HTTP URIs cannot be made persistent, nor that identifiers should be partitioned into names and addresses, which is the argument of Chapter 3 restated in one sentence.

5.4 GET, universality, and a worked example

The axioms of Chapter 2 reappear here with a sharper illustration of why the second GET rule is about universality rather than tidiness. The example is invented and exact:

Instead of defining a new method CVSSTAT to retrieve the code management status of a document, that status should be given a URI in the server's space, and headers used to point the aware client to it. Otherwise, we end up with a class of document which contains interesting information but cannot be linked to. Web Architecture from 50,000 feet, 1998

A method that returns information creates a second address space reachable only by clients that know the method. Links carry URIs, not methods, so nothing in that space can be linked to. The test is simple and still useful: if an answer is worth retrieving, it is worth an address.

5.5 The https wart

One design decision is singled out for criticism, and the criticism is architectural rather than aesthetic. The existence of an https space separate from http means that a document served securely and the same document served insecurely are different resources with different identifiers. Berners-Lee argues that security should have been a dimension negotiated while dereferencing one identifier, not a fork in the namespace, and warns against the precedent: language, privacy level and other dimensions of negotiation must not each acquire a scheme.

The complaint is worth holding onto, because Volume VII shows the author still making it seventeen years later, by which time the Web had moved decisively the other way.

5.6 Messages and documents are the same thing

The most speculative section proposes collapsing a distinction:

There has been to date an artificial distinction between the transmission standards for “protocols” and “content”. In the ever continuing quest for generalization and simplification, this is a distinction which cannot last. Web Architecture from 50,000 feet, 1998

The gain claimed is compositional. If messages and documents are the same kind of thing, then whatever can be done to one can be done to the other: messages become signable because documents are, documents become binary-encodable because messages are. Berners-Lee notes that the recursion is already present in practice, since email messages contain email messages and business processes are conducted with documents carried by SMTP and HTTP. He records that this nearly happened once, with HTTP messages almost defined in SGML.

He sets out what an evolved HTTP should provide: many concurrent message exchanges, a general data typing and marshalling standard, a schema system able to define any remote-procedure-call interface, extensions for asynchrony and streaming, and a socially aware state propagation protocol built on top in a modular way, allowing a basic design simpler than HTTP 1.1.

What happened since

  • The DNS root question was answered, slowly. The United States relinquished its stewardship of the IANA functions to the global multi-stakeholder community on 1 October 2016. Whether that constitutes governance “by and for the world as a whole in a fair way” is contested; that it was addressed at all took eighteen years.
  • The wish list was granted, in a different currency. Concurrent message exchange arrived with HTTP/2, RFC 7540 of May 2015, and HTTP/3, RFC 9114 of June 2022. Both use binary framing. Neither uses XML or RDF messages. The functional requirements were met and the representational argument was lost.
  • HTTP-NG did not survive. The activity Berners-Lee cites as the vehicle for these changes was closed without producing a successor protocol, and the work was done a decade later by other groups.
  • Web Services went the other way. The XML-based remote-operation stack he anticipated was built, and was then largely displaced through the 2010s by resource-oriented interfaces exchanging JSON, which is closer to his own architectural preference than the stack he expected.
  • The https objection lost. Rather than negotiating security within one namespace, the Web deprecated the insecure one. HTTP Strict Transport Security was published as RFC 6797 in November 2012, and Chrome began marking all plain HTTP pages as not secure in July 2018. The dimension became a scheme permanently. Chapter 67 records the author's response.

Questions

5.1 Berners-Lee calls the DNS root the only centralized part of the HTTP space. Identify two further points of centralisation present in the Web of the 2020s that this note does not anticipate, and say whether either is structural or contingent.

5.2 Restate the CVSSTAT argument as a general rule, then apply it to an interface you know that returns information through a method or verb other than GET.

5.3 The note argues that security should have been negotiated within one namespace. Set out the strongest argument for the separate https scheme, using what is known about mixed content and downgrade attacks.

5.4 HTTP/2 delivered concurrent message exchange with binary framing rather than structured documents. Which of the five goals listed in section 5.6 remain unmet, and does any of them still matter?
