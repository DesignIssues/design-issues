Chapter 28

The meaning of a document - grounding in a global namespace

1999

Source The meaning of a document - grounding in a global namespace, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 1999-01-01. read the original

The question

Written in 1999 when, as Berners-Lee puts it, the namespace argument came around again, this note answers the question Chapter 27 leaves standing. If metadata is a set of assertions in some language, what fixes what those assertions mean?

The answer is given in the abstract in a single formula, and the rest of the note unpacks it.

28.1 Meaning is a product of two things

The meaning of a document is then the product of the text of the document (in some language) and the meaning of the language. The meaning of a document, 1999

Two factors, and on the Web both of them can be named. The document has a URI. The language has a URI. And in each case the party who says what the URI refers to is its owner, which is the identity axiom of Chapter 2 doing the decisive work.

“Language” is given a precise sense: the set of symbols, the syntactic rules constraining their combination, and semantics conveyed by interpretation into other formal languages or into a natural one.

28.2 Two kinds of language

A distinction in the middle of the note explains how the whole edifice bottoms out. Some languages are terminal, defined only in natural language, and programs that process them must be hand coded. Others have machine-readable interpretations into further formal languages, and documents in them can be converted automatically into the first kind.

That is the whole layered structure of Chapter 30 stated in two sentences. At the bottom sits a small number of languages a human had to explain to a programmer. Everything above is reachable by machine.

Berners-Lee draws the contrast with ordinary words sharply:

In practice, the meaning of a word in a natural language is the sum of the associations of that word -- logical or poetic -- in the mind of the reader or writer. The meaning of a document, 1999

He adds that society works on a very strong similarity between the webs of association in different minds, and then states the departure: in the Semantic Web meaning is not vague, and languages must be defined formally and as precisely as possible. Chapter 22 is the later, humbler revision of this confidence.

28.3 Subsets, precisely

Similarity between languages is dismissed as meaning little, and replaced with something checkable: language A is a subset of language B when a document in A can be treated precisely as though it had been in B. This is the sublanguage assertion of Chapter 18, arriving here as a semantic claim rather than a processing hint.

28.4 What a signature does not do

The last third of the note is about digital signature, and it makes an argument that is easy to state and easy to get wrong.

The language defines semantics. On the simple philosophy that one place is enough, It is not the place of a digital signature to define semantics. The meaning of a document, 1999

A signature may give a party reason to use information they would otherwise ignore, and a key’s issuer may constrain what guarantees signing with it carries. But the signature must not change what the document means, because that would open a gap between what the writer intended and what the signed document says.

The conclusion is stated in four words that Chapter 37 repeats:

So, signatures themselves have no meaning. The meaning of a document, 1999

There is a consequence for what can be signed at all. Because a language defines meaning for whole documents, one cannot speak of the meaning of part of a document without first saying how that part reassembles into a second whole document. Signing a fragment requires a rule for making the fragment into a document. This is the same constraint that Chapter 24 reaches from the processing side.

What happened since

  • The formula became the standard account. RDF Semantics, a W3C Recommendation first published in February 2004, fixes meaning exactly this way: an interpretation maps the vocabulary, and the document’s meaning follows from the vocabulary’s.
  • Canonicalisation proved as hard as the note implies. Signing part of a graph requires a rule for making it a document, and RDF Dataset Canonicalization only became a W3C Recommendation in May 2024, twenty-five years after the problem was named here.
  • Terminal languages stayed terminal. The small set of vocabularies defined only in prose and implemented by hand has not shrunk. Most working systems still bottom out in a programmer reading a specification.
  • The confidence was revised. The claim that meaning in the Semantic Web is not vague is softened considerably by the same author in Chapter 22, which argues instead for definitions that are good enough and for a record of which model made them so.

Connections

The ownership half of the formula is Chapter 2 and Chapter 15. The metadata this chapter gives meaning to is Chapter 27, and the layers it implies are Chapter 30. The sublanguage relation is Chapter 18. The treatment of signatures is continued in Chapter 37, and the constraint on signing fragments meets Chapter 24 from the other side.

Questions 4

28.1 Meaning is a product of two factors. Describe what happens to a document when the language’s publisher changes the definition of a term.

28.2 Name a terminal language you use, defined only in prose. What would it take to give it a machine-readable interpretation?

28.3 Signing a fragment requires a rule for making it a whole document. Write such a rule for a format you know, and say what it must preserve.

28.4 If a signature could change meaning, construct the abuse that would follow. Who is harmed, and how would they detect it?
