Chapter 43

Design alternatives considered in Notation3

2002

Source Design alternatives considered in Notation3, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2002-03-01. read the original

The question

This note is unusual in the collection and unusual anywhere: it is a designer working in public on choices that have not yet been made. There is no conclusion, several candidates are left standing, and the reasoning is visible in a way that finished specifications never allow.

It is included at full length because Volume III argued that specifications should record which decisions were arbitrary. This is what that looks like while the decision is still open.

43.1 The problem: saying “one step along”

The need is small and constant. Given an expression for a person, one wants an expression for that person’s email address: some value related to the first by a named property.

Berners-Lee sets the criteria before the candidates, and the last one is the interesting criterion:

“Neat”? Compact, powerful, simple, naturally understandable because of metaphors with existing use of similar syntax. Alternative design choices in Notation3, 2002

Familiarity is treated as a design property rather than a concession. A syntax borrowed from something readers already use costs less to learn, and that is counted as a genuine advantage.

Two directions are named. Forward traversal moves from a thing to the value of one of its properties; backward traversal moves the other way. His instinct is recorded as an instinct: forward traversal, the only thing possible in many systems of linked objects, is the more common need.

43.2 The candidates

That observation is the most transferable thing in the note. A notation that borrows familiarity from a natural language inherits that language’s asymmetries, including the ones its designers did not choose. English makes it easy to go from a person to their email and hard to go from an email to its owner, and any syntax that feels natural in English will do the same.

Berners-Lee notes the alternative available in the model rather than the syntax: backward traversal is forward traversal along the inverse property, so a compact way to write an inverse would serve, provided it is clear when it is merely syntax and when the inverse is genuinely being named.

43.3 Sets, and why containers were the wrong shape

The second half argues for adding sets, and the argument is empirical:

It turns out that in many cases in applications we have seen, containers are in fact logically unordered sets, not ordered lists. Whether it is mail addresses on a mailing list, or rows in a database, or statements in an N3 formula, the order is immaterial, and something can occur in the set once or not at all. Alternative design choices in Notation3, 2002

Three costs of using a list for a set are listed, and the third is the kind of thing only an implementer notices.

CostWhat it means in practice
Two lists with the same members in different orders are not evidently the sameComparison and deduplication become application logic
The fact that it is really a set is communicated out of bandOnly people who know the application know it
Implementations cannot use libraries optimised for setsThe wrong data structure is used throughout

The requirement that follows is the same pair the whole notation is built on: a way of expressing sets in ordinary statements so they pass through general-purpose software unharmed, and a shorthand so people can write them without suffering.

43.4 Why an unfinished note is worth a chapter

Nothing here is decided. Candidates are listed with their borrowings, one column comes out empty, sets are argued for and not specified, and a section of loose material is headed simply as fodder.

Chapter 21 asks a specification to state which decisions are arbitrary and which follow from logic. In finished documents that distinction is invisible, because the arbitrary choices have hardened into the only way things are written. Here they are still soft, and one can see which parts of a notation were forced and which were a matter of taste, personal history, and what looked passable on the day.

What happened since

  • Path syntax arrived in the query language. Property paths became part of SPARQL 1.1, a W3C Recommendation of March 2013, with an inverse operator that writes backward traversal as forward traversal along a reversed property, which is the alternative section 43.2 names.
  • Sets were never added. The model still has ordered collections and no set construct, so the three costs listed here are still paid, and unordered data is still carried in ordered containers.
  • The dot won elsewhere. Dotted path traversal became the universal way to reach into structured data, in query languages, template languages and configuration tools, which is the candidate with the strongest borrowed metaphor.
  • The asymmetry finding generalises. Notations that borrow readability from English inherit its directionality, which is why reverse lookups read badly in nearly every query language in use.

Connections

Questions 4

43.1 Familiarity is treated as a design property. Name a syntax you find natural, identify what it borrows from, and say what that borrowing cost.

43.2 Backward traversal reads badly in English. Find a language where it reads well, and say what about that language makes it work.

43.3 Your system stores an unordered collection in an ordered structure. Which of the three costs are you paying, and which have you noticed?

43.4 Pick a syntax decision in a language you use. Was it forced, or arbitrary? How would you tell from the specification alone?
