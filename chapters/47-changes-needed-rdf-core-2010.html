Chapter 47

Changes needed to the RDF core in 2010

2000

Source Changes needed to the RDF core in 2010, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2000-01-01. read the original

The short version

RDF records facts as three-part statements: subject, property, object (the workshop, began on, 2010-06-26). In 2010 Berners-Lee asked for unused features to be removed, the model to be documented apart from any syntax, a JSON form, and more kinds of thing allowed in each part of a statement. The documentation and JSON arrived in 2014, the removals became advice, and the changes to the statement were not made. Changing the shape of a statement means changing every program and dataset built on the old shape.

The question

On 26 June 2010 a workshop opened on the future of the model. Berners-Lee could not attend, so he wrote down his position instead, and scoped it tightly:

This does not address the many other exciting things which need to be done in the semantic web, but just the RDF model and its common serializations. The Future of RDF, 2010

It is a list, and the interest lies in which items were acted on, which were not, and what the pattern says about how a deployed standard can and cannot change.

47.1 The list

Two terms recur. A serialisation is a way of writing the same data down as text; RDF/XML and Turtle are two. To deprecate a feature is to mark it as not to be used, while leaving it in place for old data. The note also proposes extensions to N3 itself (dates, quoted variables, rule languages); this chapter treats only the dates, in section 47.4. Figure 47.1 glosses each proposal in one line.

47.2 The one that mattered most

Buried without emphasis in the middle of the list is a change to the model itself:

In the RDF model, add the ability of the subject to take any of the forms allowed for the object. Currently, this means including literals The Future of RDF, 2010

A literal is a plain value, such as a date, a number or a piece of text. The model lets a literal be the object of a statement but not its subject. One can say that the workshop began on 2010-06-26, but not that 2010-06-26 was a Saturday. To say anything about a value, one must first invent a node to stand for it, which in Chapter 38 Berners-Lee called frightful. This proposal removes the obstruction in one line, and it was not adopted.

47.3 How hard to push

Two of the removals concern bags and sequences, an early way of writing an unordered or numbered group. RDF’s lists (collections, written ( a b c ) in Turtle) now do the ordered job. One paragraph asks how hard to push them out:

How strong should the deprecation be? One possibility is for them to be just best practice notes. It will have the great advantage of simplifying the documentation. The Future of RDF, 2010

The benefit claimed is not a cleaner model but shorter documentation: a feature nobody uses costs almost nothing at runtime and costs every new reader a page.

He also proposes a migration path rather than a cliff: a parser (the program that reads the text in) could convert sequences to lists as an option. A sequence written rdf:_1 :a; rdf:_2 :b would be read as the list ( :a :b ), so programs that understand only lists could still use old data. That is the evolvability argument of Chapter 16 applied to his own design: give implementations a way to move rather than a date by which to have moved.

47.4 Making dates easy to write

This proposal sits under the note’s heading on extending N3, not under the RDF model. It asks that a date or time be writable bare, in the standard form, such as 2010-06-26 or 2010-06-26T12:04Z, without the datatype label normally attached to it. The reason is plain:

Date-times are a common and important datatype and reading and writing them with an explicit datatype is boring. The Future of RDF, 2010

And then the consequence, which is not about boredom at all:

In practice people end up putting them in data on the web as strings which prevents processing software and user agents providing appropriate functionality The Future of RDF, 2010

47.5 Consistency as a constraint on proposals

One line sets a rule for everything else on the list:

Extensions to the RDF model should be done consistently with N3 to avoid unnecessary incompatibility. The Future of RDF, 2010

Two languages exist side by side: N3 (Chapter 42), the research language, and RDF, the standard. When the standard grows, it should grow towards N3. Without the rule, RDF might add a way of putting a graph inside a statement that is incompatible with N3’s braces, leaving two notations for the same thing. It is the test of independent invention (section 16.5) applied in advance: a design should fit cleanly with one built separately by someone else.

What happened since

  • The documentation proposals were carried out. RDF 1.1, a W3C Recommendation of February 2014, defines the model independently of any syntax, and JSON-LD, a JSON serialisation from the same working group, became a Recommendation the month before: both are on this list.
  • The deprecations were softened to advice. Reification, bags and sequences remain in the specification and are avoided by convention, which is the weakest of the options section 47.3 considers.
  • Literals as subjects are still not allowed. The RDF 1.2 work that W3C began in 2022 kept the restriction, and admits literal subjects only in its non-standard “generalised” form.
  • Graphs in statements arrived partially. RDF-star work from 2021 onward lets a single statement, not a whole graph, be described by another (in RDF 1.2, only as its object), which is the narrower half of the proposal here.
