Chapter 93

Charlie: An AI that works for you

2017

Source Charlie: An AI that works for you, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2017. read the original

The short version

The assistants people talk to are owned by the companies that sell them. They are pleasant, and they are not working for the person in the room.

This chapter is one of two stories in the collection; the other is Chapter 95. It is a short scene in which a man talks to an assistant that does work for him, over breakfast and an exercise bicycle. Its argument is that an assistant which truly works for its user can be given all their data, and that is what makes it useful. It was written in 2017, and parts of it describe things that did not exist until about five years later.

The question

Written in 2017 and used in talks in 2019, the scene exists to make one architectural point vivid.

The premise is stated in the first two sentences:

Bob was fed up with the AIs around him (Alexa, Siri and so on) who all seemed to work for other people, and so he got Charlie. Charlie is an AI and Charlie works for Bob. Charlie works for Bob, 2017

93.1 The scene

In a few minutes Charlie covers a wide range, and the range is the argument.

The recipe is where the fiction does something the rest of the collection cannot:

Well he hadn't published that one, but he has published a dozen books so I read those as a training set and then extrapolated how he would cook the menu you liked. Then compared it with the Linked Open Recipe data, and adjusted it a bit for the way you like things. Charlie works for Bob, 2017

A training set is the examples a program learns from; to extrapolate is to reason beyond them. “Linked Open Recipe data” is the story’s name for shared public recipe data, not a product that exists.

93.2 The loop

After the scene the note names the mechanism, a loop that feeds on trust.

The note walks the loop in four steps. Charlie works for Bob, so Bob trusts him. Because he trusts him, Bob gives Charlie access to all the data in his life. With access across the board, Charlie does a better job, and Bob trusts him more. Joined data gives insights that separate data cannot; Facebook already draws such insights about its members’ likes and habits for itself, and Charlie draws them on Bob’s behalf.

93.3 The answer that is repeated

The scene opens and closes with the same question, “Who do you work for?” The first time Charlie says “I work for you, Bob”. The second time the answer adds a three-part qualification:

Legally, ethically and algorithmically, I work for you, Bob. Charlie works for Bob, 2017

Three separate guarantees. A legal obligation. A duty of the kind Chapter 92 draws from medicine and law. And algorithmically: in how its program actually makes choices. That last one is the hardest to provide and the hardest for anyone outside to check.

93.4 How Bob got his data

The note ends by asking the question the fiction skipped:

How could this happen? How could Bob get to the point where he has access to the data? Charlie works for Bob, 2017

It offers three routes. Anger: a backlash after Cambridge Analytica in which people demand their own data. Law: British banking already had Open Banking, rules letting customers take their banking data to other apps, and European rules, he says, basically call for what Charlie needs. Architecture: the Solid project at MIT, whose apps keep their data “in one or other data store which a user points them at”. The note sums up:

So Bob may end getting his data because he gets mad and demands it, because regulations grant it to him, or because in a new architecture it has always been his. Charlie works for Bob, 2017

The first two move data somebody else holds; the third means nobody else ever held it. Either way the scene ends with Bob empowered: free to share his data with whoever he likes, and to use any app on it, Charlie included.

93.5 Why a story

Chapter 54 describes separating storage from applications; a few exchanges over an exercise bicycle make it concrete. Every detail crosses a boundary between services that today cannot see each other.

What happened since

  • The capability arrived and the arrangement did not. Assistants that can extrapolate a style and reason across documents became ordinary from 2022, hosted by the companies that already hold the data. A 2023 update to the note records Inflection AI’s Pi (May 2023): private conversation, but no access to the user’s personal data.
  • The architecture grew. The same update says Solid had gone from a project at MIT to a movement of new standards for personal data, with large corporations, governments and public-interest organisations starting to roll it out. It adds that Solid lets machine learning run over a set of pods while each individual’s privacy is preserved, the step Chapter 94 takes.
  • The law was already in force. The European rules the note refers to are the General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR), which applied from May 2018 and includes a right to data portability: to receive one’s data in a form that can be taken to another service.
  • Charlie was built. Engineers attempted an implementation in late 2024, which is Chapter 94.

Connections

The duty this assistant owes is Chapter 92, and the storage it reads is Chapter 54; both belong to Volume VI (Linked Data and Solid), which begins at Chapter 49 with joining data for insight. The difficulty of getting wisdom from private data is Chapter 72. The implementation is Chapter 94, and the scenario where the loop runs the other way is Chapter 95.

Questions 4

93.1 List the services that hold the data Charlie uses in the scene. In your own life, how many of them could see each other today?

93.2 Legally, ethically and algorithmically. Which of the three promises could you check yourself, and which would you have to take on trust?

93.3 The loop compounds only if the first step holds. Describe what the same loop produces when the assistant works for someone else.

93.4 Of the three routes to Bob’s data (anger, law, architecture), which seems most likely to work, and why?
