Chapter 4

The Web Model: Information hiding and URI syntax

1998

Source

This chapter is about The Web Model: Information hiding and URI syntax, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, dated 1998-01-29, about 2,510 words.

Read the original at https://www.w3.org/DesignIssues/Model.html. The Design Issues notes are Tim Berners-Lee's personal notes and are not endorsed by W3C. This book is commentary; where it quotes, it attributes.

The question

Berners-Lee numbered this note zero in his axioms sequence, though he wrote it in January 1998, more than a year after the two it precedes. The renumbering is a judgement about order of exposition: the axioms of Chapter 2 describe what URIs are, and this note describes the machine that consumes them. It is the most software-engineering-minded piece in Volume I, and its subject is information hiding.

The question it answers is why the Web's specifications can be revised independently of one another. The answer is that in 1998 somebody drew the module boundaries in the right places, and this note says where they are.

4.1 Three modules

The model divides a browser, or anything else that follows a link, into three parts that know different things.

ModuleKnowsDoes not know
AccessThe scheme, and the URI without its fragmentWhat a fragment identifier means
Presentation objectThe media type, and how to interpret a fragmentThe URI it came from
ApplicationThe absolute URI, for resolving relative onesWhat the fragment means

Access is handed a URI with the fragment removed. It returns two things, a data stream and a media type, which are passed to a factory that constructs an object representing the resource. That object is then given the fragment identifier. Berners-Lee states the arrangement as two axioms:

The access machinery does not need to look at the fragment identifier.

The presentation object does not need to know the URI of the resource.

And derives from them the claim that matters for everyone who writes specifications:

The specifications for access protocols are independent of the specifications for fragment identifiers. The Web Model, 1998

4.2 Why the fragment stays on the client

The immediate payoff is the link that goes nowhere. A link within a document specifies only a fragment, and the object can follow it without consulting the access code at all, because the hash syntax is universal across every scheme.

The consequence is that an author can write a document full of internal links without knowing what URI the document will eventually be served under, and can move it without editing it. Berners-Lee credits Jim Gettys with the observation that makes this more than a convenience: as digitally signed documents become common, editing a document to fix its own internal links would break the signature on it. A document that can be self-consistent independently of its namespace is one that can be signed once.

The second payoff is evolutionary, and he is emphatic about it. Because no access specification refers to fragment semantics, anyone defining a new media type is free to invent whatever notion of a view or a part suits it, knowing that nothing else will break. Equally, anyone designing a new way to name and fetch documents need not care what kinds of document will travel over it.

This has already (1998) proved incredibly enabling to the community, as HTTP has advanced in parallel with many other ways of accessing data, and the number of exciting media types has grown very rapidly. The Web Model, 1998

He notes in passing that this is also how the Semantic Web will be built: not as a new Web, but as a new kind of document, with a fragment convention of its own that names things rather than parts of pages. Webizing a language, he says, means taking what were document-global identifiers and making them globally unique by prefixing the document's URI and a hash. The mechanism of Chapter 6 and of the whole of Volume IV is introduced here in two sentences.

4.3 Why relative resolution is universal

A design decision is recorded here that could have gone the other way. When a relative reference is found in a document, the application could have passed both the relative reference and the base to the access module, letting each scheme define its own resolution algorithm. Instead, one algorithm was made common to all schemes.

The justification is the same shape as the one for fragments, and equally practical. Sets of documents that link to each other, such as the chapters of a book, are moved between file systems, HTTP space and FTP space constantly. Because the resolution rules are universal, the documents need not be modified when they move. Berners-Lee adds the honest caveat: moving half a set to one place and half to another still breaks the links.

The application knows the absolute URI in order to do this resolution. The resource still does not, and the fragment still circulates between the object that understands it and the application that carries it without looking inside.

4.4 The scheme, and why it must not be spent

The last section of the note is a warning, and it is the part that has aged into prophecy.

The access scheme is by definition the highest point of flexibility. The Web Model, 1998

Its value is that it is the escape hatch. If the entire Web developed a problem that could not be solved within existing protocols, a new space could be created, and Berners-Lee argues that there is empirical reason to believe this would work because it already worked for Gopher and FTP:

If you have ported a concept between environments in the past, then there is a better hope that you can in the future. The Web Model, 1998

The danger is that spending the hatch destroys it. If many schemes are introduced, the space of scheme names becomes a namespace with all the properties that make namespaces hard: clashes destroy interoperability, ownership becomes commercially valuable, and fair management becomes both essential and difficult. Worse, software would be needed to look up schemes and fetch handlers for them, which means something like DNS, which means that the top level of the Web's namespace has silently become DNS again. At that point, he observes, there is no top-level extension switch left.

The conclusion is procedural: access schemes should not be open to trivial extension, and should be extended only by new standards with full open review. He suggests that many proposed schemes would be better expressed as extensions within HTTP.

4.5 A note on the word “resource”

The note closes with the etymology, which explains a persistent confusion. The first Web specification said Universal Document Identifier. The IETF discussion changed “document” to “resource” on two grounds: documents did not obviously cover films and sounds, and URIs exist for endpoints such as mailboxes and login ports, which are not documents at all. RDF later adopted “resource” for anything whatever, the class of which every class is a subclass. The Technical Architecture Group then introduced “information resource” for the narrower original sense. Three meanings, one word, and the reader of any given specification must determine which is in force.

What happened since

  • The independence axiom was written into the standard. RFC 3986, of January 2005, specifies that the semantics of a fragment are defined by the media type of the retrieved representation, which is this chapter's first axiom in normative form.
  • The client-side fragment was abused, then replaced. Single-page applications used fragments to encode application state, and Google's AJAX crawling scheme of 2009 made a convention of it before deprecating it in October 2015. The History API's pushState, in HTML5, restored a distinct URI per view, which is what the model asked for.
  • New fragment conventions kept arriving, as predicted. W3C Media Fragments URI 1.0 became a Recommendation in September 2012. Text fragments, addressing a passage by its words, shipped in Chrome in 2020. Neither required any change to HTTP, which is the flexibility this chapter claims.
  • The scheme warning was vindicated by mobile. Applications claimed custom schemes in large numbers through the 2010s, and collided exactly as described: any application could register any scheme, and resolution was undefined. Both major platforms retreated to verified https links, Universal Links on iOS and App Links on Android, both introduced in 2015. The industry spent the flexibility point, discovered the namespace problem, and came back to DNS.

Questions

4.1 State what would have to change in HTTP if fragment identifiers were resolved on the server. Name one capability that would be gained and two that would be lost.

4.2 The note argues that universal relative-resolution rules let document sets move without editing. Content-addressed systems have no relative references at all. What do they trade away, and what do they gain?

4.3 Berners-Lee predicts that a proliferation of schemes would require a lookup system resembling DNS, leaving no further extension point. Describe how custom application schemes on mobile platforms reproduced this, and say whether verified https links solved the problem or deferred it.

4.4 The word “resource” carries three senses. Take one specification you know and determine which sense it uses, giving the sentence that decides it.
