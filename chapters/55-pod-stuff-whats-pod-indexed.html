Chapter 55

Pod Stuff: What's in a Pod, and how is it indexed?

2022

Source Pod Stuff: What's in a Pod, and how is it indexed?, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2022-01-01. read the original

The short version

A pod (a Solid Pod) is a person’s own web storage, of the kind Chapter 54 proposed: folders on a server, each with its own access rules, that any application the person chooses may read and write. Anything in a life can go in one. File it by kind, not by application, so applications can share it. Keep each activity together, so its owner can decide once who may see it.

The question

Thirteen years after Chapter 54, this note from December 2022 asks a question harder than the protocol: given a space of one’s own, how should its contents be arranged?

The answer begins by refusing to narrow the question:

What should be in your Solid Pod? Anything, of course. Any data in your life, across the data spectrum from public to private and everything shared with communities in between. Stuff in your Pod, 2022

55.1 Five ways stuff varies

If anything can go in a pod, one scheme must cope with all of it. The note lists five ways the stuff of a life differs (Figure 55.1).

What never changes can be cached forever and named by its cryptographic hash. Security splits three ways: the risk of being lost, faked, or read by the wrong person.

55.2 The organising decision

The note’s substantive claim is about what determines where something goes:

We do this by principally initially keying data by its RDFS Class, not by the particular app which wrote it. Stuff in your Pod, 2022

An RDFS class is the kind of thing the data describes, such as Event or Person. Filing by application recreates the silo, one company’s walled-off store, inside the space meant to abolish it. Filing by kind lets two applications dealing in events find each other’s events: a pod’s type index lists, for each class, where things of that class are kept. It works because the classes applications use overlap a lot: people, events, places, messages. Classes and apps “are related but not 1:1”, so every application that handles people should use the same shape for contact information.

55.3 Why activities are kept together anyway

The note then allows an exception, for reasons of permission rather than data:

Keeping stuff related to the same activity together in a pod, even though it is of many different types, is valuable, because the same access and trust conditions apply and it is much simpler to get those right. Stuff in your Pod, 2022

Access control, the rules saying who may read or change what, attaches to places. If one matter lives together, a person decides once who may see it. Scattered by type, the decision is made many times, and one will be wrong.

A worked example: running a meeting

The note’s longest example is an application for running a meeting: a poll on when to meet, an agenda, the groups invited, a video call, minutes and action items, often one of a series. A pod has folders (strictly, LDP containers), so the application makes one per series, one per meeting, and subfolders for the parts (Figure 55.2).

The application defines roles, or lets the owner configure them, and maps them onto Solid groups and folder access rules. Things created inside things, such as a chat about one action item, usually inherit their parent’s roles, though they may be narrowed. The interface must make plain who has access.

The two principles pull against each other (Figure 55.3).

55.4 Who configures the layout

The resolution offered is to let applications arrange things:

To be extensible across these dimensions, we must give applications the power to configure this data in the users pod in a way which meets these requirements, and also works well for that particular app. Stuff in your Pod, 2022

The note expects applications to share common patterns for similar data: summary indexes, inboxes, time series in dated folders. An inbox, for example, is a folder others may add to but not read, like Chapter 54’s friend requests. Describing where new data should go is left to Chapter 57.

55.5 Provenance as a first-class concern

One sentence names an obligation the rest of Volume VI assumes:

We track and use its provenance carefully and carefully control is access, to be able to model the way we really trust data, people and bots in this very rich, varied, and valuable system. Stuff in your Pod, 2022

Provenance is a record of who wrote a piece of data and how. It can be tracked by digital signatures from trusted agents, or more lightly by knowing that only one party can write to a resource. Trust is modelled as it really is, not as a permission flag (Chapter 66): automated agents, like people, are trusted according to who wrote what and who may write where.

55.6 Index libraries, not items

As on a desktop, where a photo library hides its insides, the type index works best listing a few large libraries rather than every photo. A small index scales better, and each library indexes its items more richly:
