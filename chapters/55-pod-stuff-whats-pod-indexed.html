Chapter 55

Pod Stuff: What's in a Pod, and how is it indexed?

2022

Source Pod Stuff: What's in a Pod, and how is it indexed?, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2022-01-01. read the original

The short version

A pod (a Solid Pod) is a person’s own web storage, of the kind Chapter 54 proposed: folders on a server, each with its own access rules, that any application the person chooses may read and write. Anything in a life can go in one. File it by kind, not by application, so applications can share it, but keep each activity together, so its owner can decide once who may see it.

The question

A calendar app and a meeting app both deal in events. If each files them in its own folder, neither sees the other’s, and the pod becomes the silo it was meant to replace. If everything is filed by kind, one meeting’s agenda, minutes, chat and guest list are scattered, and deciding who may see that meeting means getting many permissions right. This December 2022 note asks how a space of one’s own should be arranged so that both work:

What should be in your Solid Pod? Anything, of course. Any data in your life, across the data spectrum from public to private and everything shared with communities in between. Stuff in your Pod, 2022

55.1 From one byte to a genome

One scheme must cope with everything, even activities not yet done online: “Maybe sing in a choir together”. The note lists five ways the stuff of a life differs (Figure 55.1).

The smallest item is one byte: “The Doctor is [In]”. What never changes can be cached forever and named by a fingerprint of its contents (a cryptographic hash). Security splits three ways: the risk of being lost, faked, or read by the wrong person. Deeds may go in a box at the bank, unlike “a movie you bought and will watch tonight”.

55.2 The organising decision

We do this by principally initially keying data by its RDFS Class, not by the particular app which wrote it. Stuff in your Pod, 2022

An RDFS class is the kind of thing the data describes, such as Event or Person. Filing by application recreates the silo, one company’s walled-off store, inside the space meant to abolish it, and breaks a Solid mantra: “Whatever you put on your pod, I can bookmark it or like it on my pod, using my favorite app.”

A pod keeps a type index saying where each class is kept: Events in /calendar/, Contacts in /contacts/. A new application reads it and finds the old data. Classes and apps “are related but not 1:1”: in the old Semantic Web Metro diagram, Person is one line running through calendar, customer database and address book, so all should describe a contact the same way.

55.3 Why activities are kept together anyway

The note then allows an exception, for permission:

Keeping stuff related to the same activity together in a pod, even though it is of many different types, is valuable, because the same access and trust conditions apply and it is much simpler to get those right. Stuff in your Pod, 2022

Access control, the rules saying who may read or change what, attaches to places. If one matter lives together, a person decides once who may see it. Scattered by type, the decision is made many times, each a chance to get it wrong.

A worked example: running a meeting

The note’s longest example is an application for running a meeting: a poll, an agenda, invited groups, a video call, minutes and action items, often in a series. The application makes a folder per series, one per meeting, and subfolders for the parts (Figure 55.2).

Roles such as Owner, Invitee and Observer answer “Who can edit the minutes? Who can read the minutes?” and “Who can deem the item done?” The application maps them onto Solid groups and folder access rules; a chat about one action item inherits its parent’s roles unless narrowed. The interface must show who has access; “deviating from how things work in real life, or in existing systems, is a risk”.

Filing by kind solves sharing; keeping an activity together solves permission (Figure 55.3).

55.4 Applications arrange; shared patterns keep them compatible

The note lets each application lay out its data as suits it, and expects them to share common patterns: summary indexes, inboxes, time series in dated folders. An inbox, is a folder others may add to but not read, like Chapter 54’s friend requests. Describing where new data should go is left to Chapter 57.

55.5 Knowing who wrote what

We track and use its provenance carefully and carefully control is access, to be able to model the way we really trust data, people and bots in this very rich, varied, and valuable system. Stuff in your Pod, 2022

Provenance is a record of who wrote a piece of data and how. It can be tracked by digital signatures from trusted agents, or more lightly by knowing that only one party can write to a resource.

55.6 Index libraries, not items

As on a desktop, where a photo library hides its insides, the type index works best listing a few large libraries, not every photo. It scales better, and each library indexes its items more richly:

In the note’s conclusion, folders, folder access control and app roles mapped onto them let a pod serve activities “that we can imagine now, and hopefully those we cannot currently imagine, though others will”.
