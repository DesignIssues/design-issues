Chapter 64

Feeds: Blogs, Podcasts and the Like

2021

Source Feeds: Blogs, Podcasts and the Like, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2021-01-01. read the original

The short version

Subscribing to a feed was standardised long ago, so any reader app can follow any blog or podcast. Reacting (liking, commenting, rating) never was, so it lives inside whichever platform hosts the thing. Platforms give each person one audience, too coarse for people in many groups. The note’s advice: use apps that speak the open protocol, not ones tied to a single company.

The question

Written in 2021 and extended in 2024, this note surveys what had become of subscription on the Web, and asks why reacting to things is owned by the platform that hosts them.

A feed is a file on a website listing its latest posts, in a standard format such as RSS. A feed reader checks it regularly and shows what is new. That is subscribing. The note’s definition is deliberately minimal:

Let's call a feed a sequence of published things such that you can subscribe to, with in some cases a mechanism to inform you when the is more added to it. So a feed by itself is a one-way thing with no feedback. Feeds, 2021

64.2 Reaction as a vocabulary problem

Activity Streams, a W3C vocabulary for recording what people do online, defines more than twenty kinds of activity, from liking to undoing. Berners-Lee extends the idea past social media:

In general, any collaborative system in which the shared state changes form time to time, like for example an issue tracker or action list, then those state-based systems cold well be deemed to create Activity Stream events whenever that state changes. Feeds, 2021

An issue tracker is a feed (compare Chapter 45: a patch is news). The goal concerns Solid (Chapter 54), where each person keeps their data in a personal store that any app may use:

By agreeing on common interoperable representation and workflows for social actions, the Solid world can allow the same smooth user experience no matter what it it they are reacting to --liking, and so on. Feeds, 2021

64.3 The scope problem

With Instagram and Strava each person has one group of followers, so a choice may be not sharing, sharing with followers, or making something completely public. Feeds, 2021

For someone in many communities, Berners-Lee says, giving a reaction the right scope is very important (Chapter 74 treats this in general).

64.4 The warning

A section titled Possible harmful consequences notes that reaction systems can cause harm: unhealthy preoccupation with public reactions, bullying comments, and the online gender-based violence described in a Web Foundation report. Its recommendation:

So any new systems we design should involve investigation and modeling of these things, and where it is possible, explicit design to avoid harm. Feeds, 2021

Take a like-count. Technically it is a number; socially it is read as a measure of a post’s worth. That is the two-protocol analysis of Chapter 15 (a technical protocol and an unwritten social one, which can fail separately).

64.5 Subscriptions are data too

Moving subscriptions between reader apps needs an agreed list format. NetNewsWire exports it as OPML, a small XML file giving each feed’s title and address, which another reader can import. One subscription, abridged from the note: 

<opml version="1.1">
  <body>
    <outline text="ongoing by Tim Bray" type="rss"
        xmlUrl="http://www.tbray.org/ongoing/ongoing.atom"/>
  </body>

The xmlUrl is the feed’s address. The note also points to FreshRSS, which keeps a person’s feeds on their own server.

64.6 Finding feeds

The familiar “wherever you get your podcasts” assumes the big platforms list every show by name. That does not scale if everyone is to have a feed. The note’s alternative is links. Suppose Ann mentions a podcast in an email. The link goes to an episode page, which carries a link to its feed. No directory was needed. Whoever runs the directory decides which shows people find; with links, the people they already trust do.

64.7 Protocols, not apps

Some podcasts ask listeners to install their own app. The note says no:

Use your generic podcast reader, like Overcast, or the default one on your device. Keep the RSS protocol alive! Feeds, 2021

A generic reader also keeps, in one place, the record of what a listener has heard; a separate app per show scatters it. By 2024 the threat was the large players: he had heard that Spotify and Apple Podcasts, while playing anyone’s feed, also carry shows only their apps can play. The note’s policy is not to listen to those.

64.8 One story, two formats

Berners-Lee read Neal Stephenson’s Termination Shock in audio and text at once: text when someone is around, sound when driving. He calls this efficiency and accessibility: switching medium without losing one’s place helps anyone who cannot read or listen at a given moment.

In December 2024 he found that Speechify does this, though it keeps his place in its own cloud rather than in his Solid pod.

What happened since

  • Federated social media grew. Mastodon and other networks on ActivityPub (W3C Recommendation, January 2018) grew sharply after Twitter’s change of ownership in late 2022.
  • Audiences stayed coarse. Mastodon offers four post visibilities (public, unlisted, followers only, direct), still short of the many groups of section 64.3.
  • Feeds were reconsidered. On 25 August 2024 Mark Nottingham, co-author of Atom, published “What RSS Needs”, which the note links.
