Chapter 64

Feeds: Blogs, Podcasts and the Like

2021

Source Feeds: Blogs, Podcasts and the Like, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2021-01-01. read the original

The short version

Subscribing to a feed was standardised long ago; reacting (liking, commenting, rating) never was, so it lives inside whichever platform hosts the thing, with one audience per person. The note’s advice: use apps that speak the open protocol.

The question

A generic podcast app can follow almost any podcast, because subscribing was standardised years ago. Yet in this note’s 2021 survey the podcast row under “Response actions” is empty, while Instagram and Strava offer likes, kudos and comments inside their own platforms. The note asks what it would take for reacting to work everywhere.

A feed is a file on a website listing its latest posts, in a standard format such as RSS; a feed reader checks it and shows what is new. The note, written in 2021 and extended in 2024, defines a feed minimally:

Let's call a feed a sequence of published things such that you can subscribe to, with in some cases a mechanism to inform you when the is more added to it. So a feed by itself is a one-way thing with no feedback. Feeds, 2021

64.2 Reaction as a vocabulary problem

Activity Streams, a W3C vocabulary for recording what people do online, deals in statements such as “John uploaded a new photo” or “12 people liked Sally’s post”. Berners-Lee imagines a GitHub feed of “requested review, started review, completed review, approved”, and goes further:

In general, any collaborative system in which the shared state changes form time to time, like for example an issue tracker or action list, then those state-based systems cold well be deemed to create Activity Stream events whenever that state changes. Feeds, 2021

Each change of state is then news, as a patch is in Chapter 45. The goal concerns Solid (Chapter 54), personal data stores any app may use, whose mantra, “you should be able to [do] anything with anything”, implies a reaction can be recorded to anything:

By agreeing on common interoperable representation and workflows for social actions, the Solid world can allow the same smooth user experience no matter what it it they are reacting to --liking, and so on. Feeds, 2021

64.3 One follower list for every audience

With Instagram and Strava each person has one group of followers, so a choice may be not sharing, sharing with followers, or making something completely public. Feeds, 2021

For someone in many communities, Berners-Lee says, giving a reaction the right scope is very important (Chapter 74 treats this in general).

64.4 When reactions do harm

Reaction systems can cause harm: unhealthy preoccupation with public reactions, bullying comments, and the online gender-based violence described in a Web Foundation report. The note recommends:

So any new systems we design should involve investigation and modeling of these things, and where it is possible, explicit design to avoid harm. Feeds, 2021

The rest is “beyond level of this article”. Chapter 15 offers one lens: a technical protocol and a social one, which fail separately.

64.5 Subscriptions are data too

Moving subscriptions between apps needs an agreed list format. NetNewsWire exports OPML, a small XML file of feed titles and addresses (xmlUrl). One subscription, abridged from the note: 

<opml version="1.1">
  <body>
    <outline text="ongoing by Tim Bray" type="rss"
        xmlUrl="http://www.tbray.org/ongoing/ongoing.atom"/>
  </body>

FreshRSS, which the note also cites, keeps the list on one’s own server.

64.6 Finding feeds

“Wherever you get your podcasts” assumes the platforms list every show, which does not scale if everyone has a feed. The alternative is links: a link in an email leads to an episode page, and from there to its feed. The note wants both serendipity and the ability to “track down” a feed, and says the algorithms that do this in a Solid world “will determine which information people come across. In a present world with much misinformation and much disinformation, they are important.”

64.7 One story, two formats

Berners-Lee read Neal Stephenson’s Termination Shock in audio and text at once: text when someone is around, sound when driving. It is, he writes, about efficiency and about accessibility.

In December 2024 he found Speechify, which tracks his place across devices: “Just what the doctor ordered.” His wish: keep that place in his pod, not the Speechify cloud.

64.8 Protocols, not apps

One podcast tells listeners: “This podcast was generated by Audm. Get the Audm app to follow this”. The note replies:

No, don't get the audm App, or whatever App they have made for each podcast. Use your generic podcast reader, like Overcast, or the default one on your device. Keep the RSS protocol alive! Feeds, 2021

By 2024 the threat was the large players. Spotify “wants desperately to be my podcast player”, but he uses Overcast, “an independent app, for my podcasts, thank you.” Spotify and Apple Podcasts, he gathers, also carry shows only their apps can play; his policy is not to listen to those.

The note was first titled “Feeds and Feedback”, meant to cover protocols such as Linkback and Pingback, which record when someone links to a post from their own blog. “But that will have to wait for another time.”

What happened since

  • Federated social media grew. Mastodon and other networks on ActivityPub (W3C Recommendation, January 2018) grew sharply after Twitter’s change of ownership in late 2022.
  • Audiences stayed coarse. Mastodon offers four post visibilities (public, unlisted, followers only, direct), still short of the many groups of section 64.3.
  • Feeds were reconsidered. On 25 August 2024 Mark Nottingham, co-author of Atom, published “What RSS Needs”, which the note links.
