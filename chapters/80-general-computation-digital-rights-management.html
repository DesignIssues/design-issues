Chapter 80

General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS

2023

Source General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2023-05-29. read the original

The short version

Digital rights management (DRM) is software that lets a film play on a viewer’s machine while stopping the viewer from copying it. Around 2013 the W3C was split over whether HTML should offer a standard slot for it. Berners-Lee separates four things people want and shows that they cannot all hold at once. The 2013 note leaves the choice open. In 2017 the W3C, with Berners-Lee as its Director, published the slot as Encrypted Media Extensions (EME). Looking back in 2023, he finds that he can still install what he likes on his own laptop, but an artist who wants protection must still go through the big platforms.

The question

Should the open Web’s standard include a slot for software the user cannot inspect or control? This note was written in 2013 during one of the most acrimonious arguments in the Consortium’s history, and revised in 2023 to look back at how it turned out. Berners-Lee describes the state of the debate before entering it:

In the fervent discussion, very extreme positions have been taken, which has led to the debate becoming acrimonious, to the extent that much more heat than light tends to be available. Here we tease out some separate issues which have become entangled. General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS, 2013

He does not settle the argument. He separates four wants and shows every design gives up at least one.

80.1 Four rights that cannot all hold

The Electronic Frontier Foundation and the author Cory Doctorow argued that HTML should not allow DRM at all, because it moved computing platforms towards big-company control. A concrete case shows what is at stake. A film streamed to a browser plays on the screen, but the viewer cannot clip a scene to quote it, keep it if the service closes, or read the code of the decoder that unlocks it.

Berners-Lee pulls four wants out of the argument (Figure 80.1). Three belong to the user: the open-source right to have and change the code one runs; the right to have root, meaning full administrator control, over one’s own computer; and the right to copy what one watches. The fourth belongs to the seller.

80.2 The tactical argument

The case for allowing a hook in the standard, a standard slot through which a page hands encrypted video to a closed decoder, is not that the mechanism is good. It is an argument about where people end up otherwise, and Berners-Lee summarises it in his own words:

there are companies who will never put their movies on the net without DRM, so basically if we don't put DRM hooks in HTML they will just stick with flash and force you install an app, or a completely closed platform like a set-top box - in other words use a completely locked down platform. General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS, 2013

Flash was Adobe’s proprietary plug-in, then widely used for video on the Web. The conclusion drawn:

So it isn't as though making DRM more difficult in HTML5 will make DRM go away: it will just force users off the web. General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS, 2013

80.3 Four positions, fairly stated

The note’s most unusual passage sets out what different people believe, without labelling any of them wrong.

PositionWhat it rests on
Copyright itself is wrongArtists should be supported by live gigs and donations
I judge when to copyThe law has been twisted, and protection blocks fair use, backups, and archives that later stop working
Protection is worth it for a thriving industryAccepts losing archives; has no answer for small bands that cannot get onto a DRM platform
I object to hardware I do not controlIndependent of any wish to copy anything

The second position contains the prediction that has aged best: protected files typically fail when the supporting system changes, and an archive becomes unusable. The fourth separates the argument from copyright entirely. Berners-Lee closes the section with a related question:

A related question is, what sort of systems can we build to help people give money to those who e.g. write or perform music, with or without DRM, in an open market, with no 3rd party gatekeeper? General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS, 2013

80.4 A decade on

The 2023 revision is a rare thing: an author auditing his own decision. He records what was standardised and what it does:

This allows the web site to get access to one of a small set of DRM gadget on the device -- gadgets which the user has no control of. General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS, 2023

Streaming happened at enormous scale, on the Web and off, and the platforms still push people from the Web towards their apps for more control of the environment.

What survived

As a developer, I can still develop code on my Apple laptop and still install open source code and random apps written by other people on it. General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS, 2023

This is a check on one machine, not a general verdict, but it is the thing the objectors said was at risk: control of a general-purpose computer.

What did not

As an artist, there is no way for me to make my own DRM platform if a person wants to protect their material. So they have to find a route to the big platforms. General Computation, Digital Rights Management, and FOSS, 2023
What survivedWhat did not
Installing any code on one’s own laptopAny way for an artist to run their own DRM
Publishing one’s own work, unprotected, on one’s own websiteProtected work must go through big platforms, which return little to artists

The question at the end of section 80.3 gets only a partial answer: give work away or sell it unprotected, with patronage, gigs and merchandise as revenue.

What happened since

  • The specification was published. Encrypted Media Extensions became a W3C Recommendation on 18 September 2017, over formal objection, and the Electronic Frontier Foundation resigned from the Consortium in protest.
  • Flash ended. Adobe stopped supporting Flash Player at the end of 2020, so one of the locked alternatives in the tactical argument is gone.
  • The archive prediction came true. In 2019 Microsoft closed the books section of its store, and the protected e-books customers had bought stopped working; Microsoft refunded them. This is the second position’s specific warning.
  • Implementation stayed concentrated. Browsers support a handful of protection systems, chiefly Google’s Widevine, Microsoft’s PlayReady and Apple’s FairPlay, so the barrier in Figure 80.3 remains.

Connections
