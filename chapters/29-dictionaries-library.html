Chapter 29

Dictionaries in the Library?

2000

Source Dictionaries in the Library?, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2000-01-01. read the original

The short version

At a W3C dinner in May 2000, Achilles was caught keeping a dictionary in his section of a library, and the Tortoise threatened to bite his heel unless he took it out. The audience knew the library was the Web and the dictionary an XML namespace: the vocabulary a kind of document uses, named by a URI such as http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml. A debate was raging over whether a namespace should count as a web resource, with something to fetch. This is Berners-Lee’s joke about that dispute, and his argument in it.

The question

This is the only comic item in the collection: a Socratic dialogue (an argument carried on as conversation) performed at the dinner of W3C’s Advisory Committee, with Ian Jacobs as Achilles. Some held that a namespace URI was only a label; Berners-Lee held that anything with a URI may be on the Web, a vocabulary’s definition included.

29.1 A library, a dictionary, a tortoise

Of the library, Achilles says in his first lines: “It was a communal effort. As were the books.” The frame is borrowed from Douglas Hofstadter’s Gödel, Escher, Bach, whose dialogues are spoken by Achilles and the Tortoise, the pair from Zeno’s paradox (the note’s “Xeno’s fable”) in which Achilles never catches the Tortoise. Berners-Lee takes the book’s moral:

you can't try to distinuish wishes from metawishes, or the whole system breaks down. Dictionaries in the Library?, 2000

A metawish is a wish about wishes, such as wishing for more wishes. The Tortoise’s ‘metabook’, a book about books, is the same move, and Hofstadter’s moral is that the system breaks if it tries to keep them apart. Read “library” as “the Web” and “dictionary” as “namespace”, and the dialogue is the debate: may the defining kind of thing live among the things it defines?

29.2 Seven reasons to keep a dictionary out

The comedy is in the escalation (“Irony heaped on Irony!”). Most rungs echo a real position; the last is a joke at everyone’s expense.

The Tortoise saysThe move being made
It was not a goal to register dictionaries in the libraryAbsence of a goal treated as a prohibition
A dictionary is not a book, it is a metabookA new category invented to exclude the case
Achilles wanted it in the form of a book; that was not the intentForm conceded, membership denied
Things get lost, libraries burn downRisk offered as an argument about category
We have plans for a vault, and a white towerAn alternative home, not yet built
Most of us keep a pocket dictionary anywayPresent practice as a reason not to generalise
If you don't take it out, I will bite your heelThreat in place of argument, aimed at Achilles’ one weak spot

The first rung echoes the 1999 Namespaces in XML Recommendation, which said it was not a goal that a namespace name retrieve a schema (a formal description of what a kind of document may contain). The note does not say what the vault and the white tower stand for; the plainest reading is a home for vocabularies outside ordinary web addresses. The pocket dictionary is software with its vocabularies built in.

Achilles replies that he needs to look words up, that his section needs its terms defined, and finally:

But I thought when we designed the library it was so that any sort of book could go in it. That is why we called it the Global Eternal Bibliotech, after all: it is Good for Every Book. Dictionaries in the Library?, 2000

The initials spell GEB, the short name for Hofstadter’s book, and the name is the argument: a library Good for Every Book cannot turn a book away for being a dictionary. The Tortoise simply repeats that a dictionary is not a book, so the joke ends unresolved, as the debate did and as Zeno’s race does.

29.3 The line the Tortoise will not cross

The sharpest exchange is about meaning:

T: Let's not discuss meaning, please Achilles -- you know what happens when we do that! I want to use these dictionaries in order to check that the books are correct. Dictionaries in the Library?, 2000

The Tortoise wants validation and refuses semantics. It gets the better joke, “current linguistic epistemorthosemantisophologic theory” (a made-up word mocking philosophers’ jargon), and the last word:

But Achilles, for the last time, a dictionary is not a book! Dictionaries in the Library?, 2000

Achilles has the argument, and the Web went on to put the dictionaries in.

What happened since

  • Achilles won, partially. Architecture of the World Wide Web, Volume One (W3C Recommendation, 15 December 2004) advises namespace owners to publish material for people and software at the namespace URI. Fetch http://www.w3.org/1999/xhtml today and a page describing the namespace comes back.
  • Later formats took Achilles’ side. JSON-LD (a W3C Recommendation of January 2014) fetches the context defining a document’s terms from an ordinary web URL.

Connections

The Tortoise’s refusal to discuss meaning is answered twelve years later in Chapter 22: one need not settle what meaning is to look something up. What a URI identifies is Chapters 7 and 8.

Questions 4

29.1 The Tortoise invents “metabook” to exclude dictionaries. Name a category invented, in a system you know, to exclude an unanticipated case.

29.2 The Tortoise would allow dictionaries for checking books but not for finding out what they mean. Why might a standards body find the first easier to agree on than the second?

29.3 The Tortoise wants dictionaries for validation, not meaning. Give a case where the two purposes genuinely require different mechanisms.

29.4 The Tortoise says dictionaries “have such different requirements to books”. Name one such requirement, and say whether it justifies a separate system.
