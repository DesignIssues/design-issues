Chapter 36

Using labels to give semantics to tags

2006

Source Using labels to give semantics to tags, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2006-11-01. read the original

The question

Written in November 2006, at the height of enthusiasm for tagging, this note tries to reconcile two things that were then treated as opposites: the ease of typing a word, and the reusability of a well-defined term.

Berners-Lee states the worry about tags without dismissing the enthusiasm:

On the other hand, there is the sinking feeling that tags are headed the same way as keywords of Information Retrieval in the 1980s: initial hope, and then being stranded between the unbearable constraints of a controlled vocabulary and the hopeless ambiguity of uncontrolled user-generated keywords. Using labels to give semantics to tags, 2006

Two failure modes, and the note’s proposal is a way to sit between them without choosing.

36.1 The two worlds

On one side, tagging: quick, personal, and hard to reuse, because the same word means different things when used by another person or on another site. On the other, well-defined data: locations, relationships, authorship, all recorded with global identifiers under the covers, producing data that is extremely reusable. The problem with the second is stated plainly: it is not as quick as tagging with a single word off the top of one’s head.

Berners-Lee also records a live tension in the tagging world, between tags whose meaning is global and tags whose meaning depends on the tagger, and mentions a community considering a way to declare formally that one has committed to use a tag the same way as another person. His comment on that idea describes the whole design goal:

That would be a very interesting feature, as it would allow a useful definition to gain growing acceptance, to progressively move from being a private idea to being a group global standard. Using labels to give semantics to tags, 2006

36.2 The luggage label

The proposal takes its name from a physical object. A luggage label is tied to a bag, is written by whoever owns the bag, and carries information anyone can read without having to know the owner’s filing system.

36.3 The example, which is a complaint about software

The worked case is domestic and is the reason the note persuades. Berners-Lee photographs a local soccer team twice, against two opponents, and wants to find the pictures later and burn a disc for the team.

The first day’s photographs are all from one day, so a rule-based folder suffices. The second day has other photographs mixed in, so he selects the right ones and puts them in a plain folder. Then the wish, which any user of such software will recognise: he wants a folder called Soccer with the two others as subfolders, so that anything in either is in it.

He records that it can be done, as a rule-based folder combining the two, and says exactly what is wrong with that: it is not as intuitive, and it does not give him the hierarchical interface he wants. The gap is not in the data model but in the metaphor the software offers.

The modelling observation follows in one line. The folders are classes, the subfolder relation is subclass, and the local names are tags. Nothing new is needed in the model; what is needed is a way to say what a folder means.

36.4 Where it goes next

The rest of the note works through ways to express such a label: as a rule, in an ontology language, in a purpose-built vocabulary, and a section headed how not to. It then turns to sharing labels between people, and to what Berners-Lee calls smart labels, where a label carries a rule with a single variable so that membership can be computed rather than listed.

The direction of travel is the one described in Chapter 22: a private definition becomes a group’s definition by a social process, and the software should make each step of that process cheap.

What happened since

  • Tagging went the way the note feared. Folksonomies largely did not become interoperable, and general-purpose tag reuse across sites never arrived.
  • The label idea won in a narrower form. Structured annotation attached to collections, rather than chosen per item, is how photograph and media libraries now record places, people and events, with the vocabulary supplied by the software rather than by the user.
  • Machine labelling replaced manual labelling. The gap between a quick tag and a well-defined term was closed from the other side: recognition systems now attach structured terms without the user typing anything, which is the outcome this note does not anticipate.
  • The interface complaint stands. Hierarchies of collections, where membership of a child implies membership of the parent, remain unusual in consumer software twenty years on.

Questions 4
