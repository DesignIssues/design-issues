Chapter 75

Links and Laws - what does a hypertext link imply?

1997

Source Links and Laws - what does a hypertext link imply?, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 1997-04-01. read the original

The short version

A plain link is like a footnote reference: it says nothing about the thing it points to. An embedded image is like a photo pasted into a letter: it is part of what was sent, and the sender answers for it. Frames blur the two.

The question

Volume VIII opens with a note from April 1997 written for a specific purpose, and Berners-Lee says what it is:

Links in hypertext are new in that they can be followed automatically, but the concepts of reference and inclusion of material predate paper. There should not therefore be much confusion about what links imply, but as there have been some strange suggestions recently which would seriously damage the web, I write this note. Links and Law, 1997

The distinction it draws is between two things that are both written as an address in the markup (see the table below) and that mean entirely different things.

75.1 Two kinds of link

Basic HTML had three ways to point at other material. Berners-Lee calls the first two “normal” links, since the reader moves from one document to another, and the third “embedding” links.

MarkupWhat the reader seesKind
AUnderlined words to click; a new page replaces the old one or opens beside itnormal
LINKNothing in the text; a relation such as a stylesheet or the next pagenormal
IMG, OBJECTThe picture or object appears inside the pageembedding

The design intent is stated in one sentence:

The intention in the design of the web was that normal links should simply be references, with no implied meaning. Links and Law, 1997

75.2 Where the meaning actually is

The meaning lives in the words around the link:

So, if one writes "See Fred's web pages (link) which are way cool" that is clearly some kind of endorsement. If one writes "We go into this in more detail on our sales brochure (link)" there is an implication of common authorship. If one writes "Fred's message (link) was written out of malice and is a downright lie" one is denigrating (possibly libellously) the linked document. Links and Law, 1997

75.3 Why including by reference matters

Embedding a remote image, rather than copying it, keeps the reference, and the reference preserves something:

When an embedded image (or sound) is included by reference to its original address (URI) this allows an inquirer to know that address, and hence know the current version of the image. Links and Law, 1997

Including by reference gives three things:

  • anyone can see the current version of the image;
  • the owner can to some extent know, and perhaps control, who has access;
  • in future, Berners-Lee expects, anyone can find the owner and the licence terms.

Copying severs all three. This applies the axioms of universality (section 2.2) to part of a page: once a thing has its own address, anyone can refer to it and ask about it.

75.4 The proposal that was never adopted

One exception is named, with a simple remedy:

Advertising provides an exception to this rule: a case in which the embedded image is not part of the document. At risk of making ittoo easy for users to turn off advertizing, it would be ideal if the distinction were make in the markup between embeeded information which is or is not part of the document. Links and Law, 1997

His proposed markup is a single extra word on the image: 

<IMG src="banner-ad.gif" foreign>

He personally feels this would be an important step forward in the integrity of the Web.

75.5 The frame problem

Frames split one browser window into separate panes. Clicking a link inside one pane could load another site’s page there, while the first site’s logo and menu stayed around it. The closing section identifies this as where the window metaphor breaks:

The parts of the document do appear with the same window, but because within a single frame (subsection of a window) one can follow hypertext links replacing content with a separate document, it is easy to create the impression that the owner of the surrounding frames is in fact responsible for the defining document. Links and Law, 1997

Until markup can express the difference, the note says, content providers should make sure readers are not left with the illusion.

What happened since

  • The foreign flag never arrived. In markup, an advertisement is still indistinguishable from the page that embeds it.
  • Frames were replaced and the problem persisted. Inline frames (HTML 4.0, 1997) and embedded players reproduce the confusion section 75.5 names; HTML5’s sandbox attribute limits what they can do rather than saying whose they are.
