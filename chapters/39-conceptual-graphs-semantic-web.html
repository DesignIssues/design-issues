Chapter 39

Conceptual Graphs and the semantic Web

2001

Source Conceptual Graphs and the semantic Web, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2001-01-01. read the original

The short version

Conceptual Graphs are a logic drawn as boxes and arrows, older than the Semantic Web. To put them on the Web, their names are given URIs. What has to go is not the logic but the assumption of one closed, central knowledge base.

The question

Volume IV closes with a comparison, and with an admission of why it was overdue. More than once, people (Mary Keeler, fresh from a CG conference, among them) had told Berners-Lee that the Semantic Web was really Conceptual Graphs, or the reverse. He explains, in January 2001, why the message had not landed:

I think the tendency of the CG examples to relate to natural language rather than hard logic made it more difficult for someone of my own leanings toward rigid logical processing for the Sweb to understand what the CG folks were driving at. Conceptual Graphs and the Semantic Web, 2001

Then he read the specification and found that the two were closer than the examples had suggested. The chapter applies the webizing of Chapter 26, turning a system’s local names into URIs, to a rival.

39.1 The verdict

To put it in a nutshell, Conceptual Graphs (CGs) are a logic language used for describing closed worlds of logic. They have traditionally had a strong emphasis on two-dimensional graphical representations, but there are conventional serializations Conceptual Graphs and the Semantic Web, 2001

The operative phrase is closed worlds: everything the system knows lives in one knowledge base, so whatever is not there is treated as absent. On the Web, the open world, a missing fact means only “not known here”. The next sentence says CGs are basically as expressive as KIF (the Knowledge Interchange Format, a standard text format for logic), and so only have to be webized to serve as a basis for the Semantic Web.

That is a considerable concession. The rival is not wrong; it is unwebized.

39.2 Webizing, applied to a rival

The procedure is Chapter 26’s. Modify the syntax so that a concept or a relation may carry a URI, which means allowing the characters of a URI in identifiers. Then map local identifiers to global ones in the usual way: the document’s URI, a hash, and the local name. A concept Bus in a file at http://example.org/travel becomes http://example.org/travel#Bus.

He then separates two kinds of obstacle.

39.3 The one real difference

In CGs, concepts and relations are disjoint: two worlds that never overlap, so a relation can never be the subject of another relation. RDF can say, for instance, that livesIn is the opposite of hasResident: a statement about a relation. CGs forbid that; relations are only ever used, never described. This is deliberate, not a defect. A true two-way mapping would require CGs to reify an arbitrary RDF graph (turn each statement into a thing so it can be talked about), and Berners-Lee does not think that would be useful.

The principle he states in passing is the one worth keeping:

The semantic web logic language has to be universal in that it must allow expression of any other language; but it certainly does not force every language to be universal itself. Conceptual Graphs and the Semantic Web, 2001

Universality is a property required of the base language, not of everything built on it.

39.4 Where the shapes do not match

Two arguments or many

Properties in the Semantic Web take exactly two arguments, a subject and an object. Relations in CGs may take any number. A three-way relation such as Leeds lies between York and Manchester must be broken up, which is possible and is not free.

Who may say what type a thing is

In CGs a concept has a single type, usually its creator’s. On the Web, Berners-Lee notes, a third party may equally well assert a type for something, and that is ordinary. A thing’s type is not the property of whoever first named it.

39.5 What the comparison shows

Chapter 31 argued that earlier knowledge representation failed through centralisation; here the claim is checked against one real system, and holds.

39.6 Striking similarities

The best evidence is one sentence written twice: John Sowa’s CG form for John goes to Boston by bus, followed by Berners-Lee’s N3, the compact notation for RDF he was then developing: 

[Go]-
   (Agnt)->[Person: John]
   (Dest)->[City: Boston]
   (Inst)->[Bus].
Conceptual Graphs and the Semantic Web, 2001 
@prefix : <#>.
[a :Go]
   >- :agent -> [a :Person; = <#John>];
   >- :dest -> [ a :City; = <#Boston>];
   >- :inst -> [ a :Bus].
Conceptual Graphs and the Semantic Web, 2001

In the N3, [a :Go] means “something that is a Go”; each >- p -> line attaches a relation p from it to another thing. The likeness is “remarkable down to the final period”. Drawn out, the two show the one structural difference.

It extends to contexts. Both systems quote a whole formula to talk about it, as in the note’s example, loosely “Tom believes that Mary wants to marry a sailor”: CGs nest a proposition inside a box, and N3 wraps it in braces. Berners-Lee says the concept occurs “very equivalently” in the two. One small mismatch survives at the level of names: CGIF identifiers are not case sensitive, while URIs are.

The comparison also sets up Volume V: if the Semantic Web needs a logic, what should it look like and how should it be written down?

What happened since

  • Both were standardised, separately. Conceptual Graphs became part of the ISO Common Logic standard, published in 2007, while the Web stack went through RDF, RDF Schema, OWL and SPARQL. Common Logic, which absorbed the CG interchange format, provides for naming and importing content on the Web.
  • The n-ary problem was met by convention. Relations of more than two arguments are represented by introducing an intermediate node, a pattern the Semantic Web Best Practices group documented in April 2006. It is the same kind of middle node that Chapter 38 used to attach a unit to a number.
  • Several types, from anyone. The RDF Recommendations of February 2004 let a resource carry any number of rdf:type statements, from any source, as section 39.4 expects.
