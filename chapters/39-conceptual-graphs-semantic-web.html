Chapter 39

Conceptual Graphs and the semantic Web

2001

Source Conceptual Graphs and the semantic Web, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2001-01-01. read the original

The question

Volume IV closes with a comparison, and with an admission of why it was overdue. People had been telling Berners-Lee for years that the Semantic Web was really Conceptual Graphs, or the reverse. He explains, in January 2001, why the message had not landed:

I think the tendency of the CG examples to relate to natural language rather than hard logic made it more difficult for someone of my own leanings toward rigid logical processing for the Sweb to understand what the CG folks were driving at. Conceptual Graphs and the Semantic Web, 2001

Then he read the specification and found that the two were closer than the examples had suggested. The chapter is worth reading as a demonstration of the method of Chapter 26 applied to a rival system, and as the fairest treatment of a competitor in the collection.

39.1 The verdict

To put it in a nutshell, Conceptual Graphs (CGs) are a logic language used for describing closed worlds of logic. They have traditionally had a strong emphasis on two-dimensional graphical representations, but there are conventional serializations Conceptual Graphs and the Semantic Web, 2001

The operative phrase is closed worlds, and the operative consequence is in the next sentence: they are basically as expressive as the older interchange format, and so in a way only have to be webized to serve as a basis for the Semantic Web.

That is a considerable concession. The rival is not wrong; it is unwebized.

39.2 Webizing, applied to a rival

The procedure is exactly the one set out in Chapter 26, and takes two paragraphs. Modify the syntax so that a concept or a relation may carry a URI, which means allowing the characters of a URI in identifiers. Then map local identifiers to global ones in the usual way: the document’s URI, a hash, and the local name.

Berners-Lee then separates two kinds of obstacle, and the separation is the most useful idea in the chapter.

39.3 The one real difference

The disjointness of concepts and relations is treated as a deliberate choice to be respected rather than a defect. Berners-Lee observes that a true bidirectional mapping would require the rival to describe an arbitrary graph at a meta level, and says he does not think that would be useful.

The principle he states in passing is the one worth carrying out of the chapter, and it applies to every language that has ever been asked to be more general than its designers wanted:

The semantic web logic language has to be universal in that it must allow expression of any other language; but it certainly does not force every language to be universal itself. Conceptual Graphs and the Semantic Web, 2001

Universality is a property required of the substrate, not of everything built on it. Chapter 2’s third axiom says the same thing about identifier spaces.

39.4 Where the shapes do not match

One structural difference is recorded without a resolution. Properties in the Semantic Web model take exactly two arguments; relations in Conceptual Graphs need not. Relations of more than two arguments must therefore be broken up, which is possible and is not free.

A second difference is more interesting because it is about authority. In the rival system a concept is associated with a single type, usually its creator’s. Berners-Lee notes that on the Web a third party may equally well assert a type for something, and that such statements are ordinary rather than exceptional. Anyone may say anything about anything, so a thing’s type is not the property of whoever first named it.

39.5 Why this chapter closes the volume

Volume IV has been about giving meaning to data, and this is where the question is asked of a system that had been doing so for years before the Web existed. The answer, that the logic was sound and the assumptions were parochial, is the same answer Chapter 31 gives about knowledge representation in general, arrived at here by examination rather than by argument.

It also establishes the shape of Volume V. If the rival is a logic and the Semantic Web needs one, the next question is what that logic should look like and how it should be written down.

What happened since

  • Both were standardised, separately. Conceptual Graphs became part of the ISO Common Logic standard, published in 2007, while the Web stack went through RDF, RDF Schema, OWL and SPARQL. The webizing described here was never carried out on the rival.
  • The n-ary problem was met by convention. Relations of more than two arguments are represented by introducing an intermediate node, a pattern the Semantic Web Best Practices group documented in April 2006. It is the same extra node that Chapter 38 found frightful in another context.
  • The single-type assumption did not survive contact. Multiple types asserted by different parties are now routine, which is the open-world behaviour section 39.4 predicts.
  • The two-dimensional tradition outlived its notation. Drawing logic as circles and arrows, which the rival emphasised, is how graph data is universally presented, including in this book.
