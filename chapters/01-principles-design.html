Chapter 1

Principles of Design

1998

Source Principles of Design, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 1998-09-01. read the original

The short version

Before anyone builds something that other people will build on in turn, the same handful of choices arrive. Simple, or capable? Strict about what is accepted, or forgiving? Control kept in one place, or given away?

Berners-Lee had answered these questions many times in arguments and had never written the answers down. This is the note where he does: six rules, borrowed from three different traditions, that the rest of this book keeps applying.

The question

By 1998 Berners-Lee had been arguing about Web design for seven years, and had noticed that the same appeals to good practice kept recurring in arguments without ever being written down. This note exists to give those appeals an address. Its opening sentence is unusually candid about the motive:

Again and again we fall back on the folklore of the principles of good design. Sometimes I need a URI for them so this is started as collection of them. Principles of Design, 1998

That is a working note, not a manifesto, and the author says so: he calls the content “largely motherhood and apple pie” while insisting it still needs a home. The chapter is placed first because everything after it is an application of one of these six principles to a particular problem.

1.1 Three pairs, three traditions

The six principles are not a flat list. Berners-Lee groups them by where they come from, and the grouping is the most useful thing in the note. Simplicity and modularity, he says, are “the stuff of software engineering”. Decentralization and tolerance are “the life and breath of Internet”, and he credits Brian Carpenter’s RFC 1958, Architectural Principles of the Internet, of June 1996. The remaining pair, the test of independent invention and the principle of least power, he claims as the ones he found he needed for the Web specifically.

Reading the six as three inherited layers explains why they sometimes conflict. The software-engineering pair is about the system one is building. The Internet pair is about the network one is building on. The Web pair is about the systems other people will build later, which do not exist yet and cannot be consulted.

1.2 Simplicity, and what it is not

The definition offered is narrow and worth keeping: “A language which uses fewer basic elements to achieve the same power is simpler.” Berners-Lee then separates this from familiarity. A two-line recursive solution is simpler than a ten-line iterative one even where readers find the longer version easier to follow.

The worked example is XML processing instructions, the constructs beginning with a question mark. His objection is not that they are large but that they are contagious: they add a third kind of thing to a design that had only elements and attributes, and so every specification that refers to XML processing must afterwards say what it does about them. Simplicity here is measured in other people’s specifications, not in one’s own.

1.3 Modularity, and being a module

The first half is conventional: group features so that bindings within a group are tight and bindings between groups are loose, and the groups become the units of independent change. Berners-Lee adds the standard warning that a design in which each module must know the insides of the others is not modular but merely partitioned.

The second half is the part that is rarely met, and he flags it as such:

It is also necessary to realize that your own system, no matter how big and wonderful it seems now, should always be designed to be a part of another larger system. This is often much more difficult than modularity. Principles of Design, 1998

This is modularity turned inside out, and it is the bridge to the fifth principle. A system designed to be a component of something unspecified must do one thing, must leave the rest alone, and must not claim to be the centre.

1.4 Tolerance, and its cost

The rule quoted is Postel’s, restated: be liberal in what is required, and conservative in what is done. Write strict, accept loose.

Berners-Lee records the objection as well as the rule, which is why the section survives re-reading. Lax browsers make the system work better in the short term and encourage lax authors, which makes the system worse in the long term. He draws the line carefully: tolerance in an implementation is never a licence for vagueness in a specification, and never an excuse for a product that contravenes one. A specification must still draw a precise boundary between conformance and non-conformance, whatever implementations then choose to accept across it.

1.5 Decentralization, including of concepts

The network argument is familiar: a common point involved in every operation limits scaling and concentrates failure. The extension is the part worth carrying forward. Berners-Lee applies decentralization to vocabulary as well as to infrastructure. A knowledge representation system that requires every user of the concept of an automobile to use one particular identifier for it has created a conceptual bottleneck, and restricts the system to those for whom that one formulation works.

This sentence is the seed of the argument that runs through Volumes IV and VI: that the Semantic Web must let anyone mint a term, and must solve agreement by mapping between vocabularies rather than by mandating one.

1.6 The test of independent invention

The test is a single question: if someone else had already invented this system, would theirs work with ours? It is a thought experiment about arbitrary decisions. Where two independent designers would have chosen differently, the design must not assume its own choice is the only one.

Chapter 2 shows the test applied to the Web’s own foundation, where Berners-Lee argues that URI space passes it because nothing prevents a rival universal space from existing and enveloping URI space as a subset.

1.7 The principle of least power

This is the longest section of the note and the one with the clearest argument. Berners-Lee sets out a scale, from the plainly descriptive through limited logics and declarative languages to the unashamedly procedural, and observes that the profession spent the 1960s to the 1980s trying to move up it. His claim is that for data on the Web the incentive runs the other way:

The reason for this is that the less powerful the language, the more you can do with the data stored in that language. If you write it in a simple declarative from, anyone can write a program to analyze it in many ways. Principles of Design, 1998

The comparison offered is weather data. Published as a table of values with a declarative description, it can be averaged, plotted, combined with other sources, and queried in ways its publisher never considered. Published through a Java applet, it may look better and can be analysed by nobody: the only way to discover what an applet means is to run it in front of a person.

He then gives the design decision this principle was extracted from:

I chose HTML not to be a programming language because I wanted different programs to do different things with it: present it differently, extract tables of contents, index it, and so on. Principles of Design, 1998

A follow-up added later in the life of the page credits Meredith Patterson’s talk The Science of Insecurity with the observation that least power also governs security: an interface exposed to attack is safer when the language it accepts is weak enough to be decided.

What happened since

  • Least power was promoted. It left this personal note and became a W3C Technical Architecture Group finding, The Rule of Least Power, edited by Berners-Lee and Noah Mendelsohn, published in February 2006.
  • The security reading became a field. The language-theoretic security work that Patterson’s talk introduced, dating from around 2011, made the argument that parser expressiveness is itself the attack surface.
  • Tolerance was reconsidered. The Internet Architecture Board published RFC 9413, Maintaining Robust Protocols, in June 2023, arguing that liberal acceptance degrades interoperability over time because divergent implementations are never corrected. The 1998 objection recorded in this note is the same objection, sustained.
  • The tolerance argument was settled differently on the Web. HTML5, whose first W3C Recommendation came in October 2014, specified error handling exactly, so that lax input produces one defined result rather than many. That is neither strictness nor tolerance but a third answer the 1998 note does not anticipate.
  • Least power lost the data argument in practice. The weather-applet comparison recurred with JavaScript-rendered pages, which by the 2010s made a large share of published data readable only by running it.

Where these principles are used

Decentralization and independent invention are applied to URI space in Chapter 2. Modularity in the inside-out sense becomes the independence axioms of Chapter 4. Simplicity and the cost of extra constructs return in Volume III, in particular in Chapter 16 on evolvability and Chapter 19 on modularity. Least power is the argument beneath the whole of Volumes IV and V, and is stated again at the opening of Chapter 5.

Questions 4

1.1 Berners-Lee objects to XML processing instructions on grounds of simplicity, not size. Restate his objection as a claim about the number of documents that must change when a construct is added to a widely referenced specification.

1.2 Tolerance and simplicity can conflict: accepting malformed input requires rules for handling it, and those rules are additional basic elements. Give an example from HTML where this conflict was resolved in favour of tolerance, and say what it cost.

1.3 Apply the test of independent invention to the Domain Name System. If a second, independently designed global naming system had existed in 1985, could the two have interoperated? What in the design decides the answer?

1.4 The principle of least power claims that weaker languages make data more reusable. State the strongest counter-argument, and identify which of the other five principles it appeals to.
