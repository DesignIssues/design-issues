Chapter 22

Working despite Ambiguity

2012

Source Working despite Ambiguity, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2012-10-15. read the original

The question

In October 2012 an argument broke out on the Technical Architecture Group’s mailing list, not for the first time, over whether a symbol can ever have an unambiguous meaning. Berners-Lee’s reply, lightly edited into this note, does not try to win it. He concedes the point in the first paragraph and then asks how work is supposed to continue.

His own framing of what he is doing is worth keeping:

I have not studied much of the history of philosophy but I do find one needs to be prepared to jump in in order to keep the course of what I otherwise regard as engineering still on track Working despite Ambiguity, 2012

The original message was titled “Working without being ambushed by Ambiguity”, and ambush is the note’s real subject: not whether the pedants are right, but what to do when they are right at an inconvenient moment.

22.1 The billiard ball

The argument turns on an analogy that is unusually well chosen, because it concedes everything and gives up nothing.

He states the parallel exactly:

So it is with denotation by names. We need to keep the models of ambiguity in our back pocket and bring them out when we need them, but not use them to ambush any discussion in the classical form. Working despite Ambiguity, 2012

And notes the one thing the analogy is missing. In physics there is mathematics showing that the quantum model gives rise, to a high degree of approximation, to the classical one. For meaning there is no such derivation, which is what the rest of the note tries to supply by other means.

22.2 Five ways of pinning a word down

The central move is to stop treating disambiguation as a philosophical problem and start treating it as a catalogue of working systems. Berners-Lee lists five, in rough order of how much machinery each requires.

The standards-committee model gets the most careful description, and one parenthesis in it defines what a specification is actually for. A committee works until it has terms tight enough to make a standard people will use without undesirable consequence through misunderstanding, and then he adds: not a standard which everyone will understand unambiguously in exactly the same way.

22.3 The observation that changes the subject

What makes this note more than a survey is a claim about the status of the five models:

The initial philosophers seemed to treat language as a natural or god-made thing to be investigated not engineered invented things, but in fact dictionaries and court procedure and standards bodies are all engineered systems. So we can design the ones we need. Working despite Ambiguity, 2012

Disambiguation stops being something that happens to language and becomes something one builds. Berners-Lee adds a reason for bothering that is more serious than the context requires: given how much violence in the world one might imagine stems from misunderstanding of some sort, it may behove us to improve on these systems.

The common property of all five is stated plainly:

A common facet of all these models is that they do not give complete unambiguity at all, just a good enough definition. Working despite Ambiguity, 2012

22.4 What the Semantic Web adds

The design is then described as a meta-model rather than a sixth model. It does not say how meaning is fixed. It says where to look, and it lets each vocabulary fix its meaning however it likes.

Three parts: the Domain Name System and the spaces beneath it let a social entity own a term; HTTP lets a machine look that term up; and what comes back gives elucidation in two forms, natural language and logic. Crucially, what comes back also indicates which of the five models is in force.

A sender then chooses terms whose owners curate them under whichever models make the disambiguation, in Berners-Lee’s phrase, good enough for the situation, for the sending and receiving agent. He notes wryly that people also still share understandings over beers in bars, as they always have, and claims two advantages for the machine version: it provides a way to look up meaning without needing to believe meaning is unambiguous, and it produces logic that programs can use.

The note ends with Pat Hayes, quoted at length, on what happens to English prose when meanings really must be pinned down. Hayes observes that in legal contracts, diplomatic communications and patent applications hardly any word is simply used: almost all are given explicit definitions, often with guard language that denies the ordinary reading. His conclusion is the practical one this whole chapter rests on:

This is not normal language: to even use it requires specialized training, and it is dangerous for non-experts to attempt, exactly because it has hard unambiguous meanings which are independent of context. Pat Hayes, quoted in Working despite Ambiguity, 2012

What happened since

  • The ambush kept happening. Arguments about denotation recurred in Web standards work after 2012, and this note is now the thing people link to in order to end them, which is precisely the use its author asks for.
  • Vocabularies did not declare their model. The design requires that what a term dereferences to indicate how its meaning is fixed. In practice most vocabularies supply a label and a comment, and the model is left to be inferred.
  • The engineering claim spread. Treating shared meaning as an institution to be designed rather than a fact to be discovered is now ordinary in data governance and in the operation of large shared schemas, whether or not anyone reached it through this argument.
  • The tooling described in section 22.4 largely did not arrive. The buttons Berners-Lee wants, for inviting comment on a new term or forming a group to curate one, exist in specialist ontology editors and nowhere a form designer would meet them.

Questions 4

22.1 The physics analogy is missing the mathematics connecting the two scales. What would the equivalent derivation for meaning have to show?

22.2 Take a term you use daily at work. Which of the five models fixes its meaning, and what happens when two of your colleagues disagree about it?

22.3 The design requires a vocabulary to indicate which model it uses. Write such an indication for a vocabulary you know, and say what a consumer could do with it.

22.4 Hayes argues that precise legal language is dangerous for non-experts. Does the same danger apply to specification prose? Give an example.
