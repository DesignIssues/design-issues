Chapter 88

Cultures and boundaries

2007

Source Cultures and boundaries, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2007. read the original

The short version

Groups invent words to talk better among themselves. Every such word also makes the group harder to understand from outside. The cure is the hard work of figuring out what the other side means. The Semantic Web was designed so that many small vocabularies can be mixed, rather than forcing one giant one.

The question

Written in July 2007 with the editing status “stable”, which in this collection is rare. It describes a mechanism that everyone has experienced and almost nobody has named, and it states it as a law. Why does the thing that helps a group talk make it harder for anyone else to follow?

88.1 The mechanism

A group that talks among itself develops its own language. Berners-Lee describes three ways this happens: borrowing a term one party already uses and settling its meaning together, adjusting an existing meaning to cover something new the group has found, or deliberately coining a word chosen to be unlike any other.

The first effect is the intended one: a shared vocabulary broadens what can be discussed without misunderstanding. The second effect is, in most cases, not intended by anyone:

88.2 What it looks like in practice

An example from his own working life:

So it is, then, that a working group will, given a free rein, work in relative isolation for several months, and when they have finished have great difficulty explaining the specification documents to their peers outside the group. Often this will be a surprise to the members of the group. Cultures and boundaries, 2007

The surprise is the telling detail: from inside, the wall going up looked like progress. Each side then explains the gap in its own way. The group finds outsiders “rather slow to understand”, and the outsiders see the group as using, or misusing, jargon. From there it can escalate:

It may be worse: those on one side of the boundary may see those on the other as being stupid, malicious, or even heretical. Cultures and boundaries, 2007

88.3 The remedy, and what it costs

The solution offered is not a technique, and the note admits it is incomplete:

An incomplete but essential solution to the problem is for those involved to think about what those on the other side of the boundary are thinking. This is hard work. Cultures and boundaries, 2007

In a technical setting it means carefully studying the words in the other party’s seemingly senseless protestations, to work out how those words must be related in the other’s mind: not deciding they are wrong, but asking what would have to be true of their vocabulary for what they said to make sense.

An illustration (ours, not the note’s): sales and billing disagree on how many customers the firm has. Listening reveals that each uses the word differently, and both numbers are right:

TeamWhat customer means
Salesanyone with a signed contract
Billinganyone who paid an invoice this year

This is what makes standards slow. When a small group’s specification goes out for wide agreement, its terms are re-examined and often changed, as the wider group repeats what the small group already went through. Hence:

There is constant tension between the need to get things done quickly with less effort, by working within a small group, and the need to get this wider understanding which takes so much more time. Cultures and boundaries, 2007

88.4 Why the technology was shaped by this

From nested to tangled

In practice, the note says, life is a “fractal tangle of overlapping communities”. “Fractal” here means the same pattern of overlapping groups at every scale, from a family to the whole world. So there is always a little language shared by very many people, a great deal of language local to individuals, and everything in between.

In earlier centuries geography constrained who could form a group, so communities were nested: town inside region inside country. The Internet and the Web remove that constraint. A person can also belong, worldwide, to the specialists in a field, to people with a particular medical condition, or to people concerned about a global issue.

Then comes the connection that places this chapter at the centre of the book:

The Semantic Web is a technology engineered specifically for this situation. Cultures and boundaries, 2007

The design in five sentences

Five sentences follow that compress the whole design. An ontology is a vocabulary: a set of related terms with agreed meanings.

  1. Terms are defined in ontologies.
  2. Ontologies are defined by communities.
  3. A person is involved in many communities.
  4. A message mixes terms from many ontologies.
  5. An operation needs consistency only between the parts of ontologies it actually uses.

The note then names the two failures this is built to avoid:

We know that a single huge ontology of everything cannot be done, as it the effort of getting consensus on it becomes unimaginable. We know that stovepipe systems with only local ontologies leave us with communication, and especially the re-use of data, which just does not happen, to our great detriment. Cultures and boundaries, 2007

A stovepipe is a system that only talks to itself, like a pipe with no side openings. These are the two failures that the scale-free requirement of Chapter 87 (designs must work for groups of every size) rules out: one vocabulary too big to agree on, or many too small to share.

The conclusion follows in a single clause:

And so we engineer specifically for a fractal topology. Cultures and boundaries, 2007

What happened since

  • Linked Data (from 2006) and schema.org (launched 2011) published data that mixes terms from several vocabularies. Schema.org itself, though, is one large shared vocabulary, closer to the first arrangement in Figure 88.3.
