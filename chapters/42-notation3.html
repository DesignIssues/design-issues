Chapter 42

Notation3

2000

Source Notation3, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2000-10-01. read the original

The short version

Notation3 (N3) is RDF plus braces, which quote a group of statements without asserting them. That makes rules possible, and statements about what a document says. To stay safe on an open Web it allows only one kind of not: this named document does not contain that fact. The data part became Turtle; the logic stayed experimental.

The question

This is the specification of the notation Berners-Lee designed to write the logic of Chapter 40 down. It is the most technical note in the collection and the only one that reads as a paper, with formal syntax, a definition of entailment (what follows from what), and a catalogue of built-in operators. The catalogue dates it to 2000; the copy online is dated August 2005 and calls itself an early draft of the semantics.

The design question it answers is not what logic is true, but what logic survives being published.

42.1 Written for an open world

Whereas a closed system may be built based on a single knowledge base of believed facts, an open web-based system exists in an unbounded sea of interconnected information resources. This requires that an agent be aware of the provenance of information, and responsible for its disposition. Notation 3 Logic, 2000

From which the first requirement follows immediately. To say what a document said, one must be able to quote a set of statements and match it against a pattern. Quotation is therefore not an advanced feature but a starting condition, which is why Chapter 30 puts it in the base layer.

In Figure 42.1, a prefix such as foaf: abbreviates the start of a long URI, and a semicolon means the same subject again.

A rule, worked through

The note’s first example puts the braces to work. A rule is a statement whose subject and object are quoted formulas, joined by the built-in property log:implies, written => for short.

42.2 The trap that shaped the design

Quotation plus reference brings self-reference, and self-reference brings paradox: a sentence such as this statement is false, which is true only if it is false. Berners-Lee’s response is unusually direct about the cost. (First-order logic, below, is the ordinary logic of all, some and not.)

This quotation and reference, with its inevitable possibility of direct or indirect self-reference, if added directly to first order logic presents problems such as paradox traps. To avoid this, N3 logic has deliberately been kept to limited expressive power: it currently contains no general first order negation. Notation 3 Logic, 2000

A language built to express anything is deliberately made unable to say a general not, because the alternative is a system that can tie itself in knots. Negated forms of particular built-in operations are provided instead.

42.3 Monotonicity as a publishing requirement

The second design goal is stated as a claim about other people rather than about logic:

Further, we require that other users of N3 in the web can express new knowledge without affecting systems we have already built. Notation 3 Logic, 2000

Consider a booking system that concludes a guest has no allergy because none is on file. Someone records an allergy elsewhere, and the conclusion silently flips. A logic is monotonic when adding facts never withdraws a conclusion. New information may still contradict old, but the conflict must be resolved openly, not by quietly changing what the old information meant.

The culprit, Berners-Lee says, is negation as failure: treating a sentence as false when it cannot be derived from the “current knowledge base”, everything the system holds at the moment. That is a changing quantity, referred to only implicitly.

42.4 Scoped negation as failure

The solution is to keep the useful behaviour and remove the implicit reference. Negation is permitted only about a specific named document or formula: whether that document holds, or allows derivation of, a given fact. The built-in log:notIncludes does this, and the note calls it completely monotonic, since no other knowledge gained can change its answer.

42.5 The Web as an operator

Two built-in properties do something no logic textbook contains: they make retrieval part of inference. log:semantics relates a document to what it means, and log:says to what it says. Berners-Lee defines the semantics of a document as the result of retrieving a representation, parsing it, and assimilating it into the local framework.

The note’s second example looks inside what people wrote, using log:includes to believe weather reports only from residents.

A reviewer was aghast at defining semantics as retrieval, and the footnote answering the objection is the wittiest passage in the collection:

I suspect however that the meaning of the paper to the reviewer could be considered quite equivalently the result of the process of retrieval of a representation of the paper, its parsing by the review, and its assimilation in terms of the reviewer's local logical framework: a similar though perhaps imperfect process. Notation 3 Logic, 2000

42.6 What the separation bought

The conclusion records an outcome that the note’s own structure made possible:

The separation between the Notation3 extensions to RDF and the logic properties has allowed N3 by itself to be used in many other applications directly, and to be used with other properties to provide other functionality such as the expression of patches (updates) Notation 3 Logic, 2000

The notation and the logic are separable, so the notation can be used by people who want none of the logic. That is the modularity of Chapter 19, applied by the same author to his own design, and it is why the readable half of this work outlived the rest.

What happened since

  • The notation won; the logic did not. Turtle, which is the data half of this notation with the logic removed, became a W3C Recommendation in February 2014 and is now the ordinary way people read and write graph data. The rules, formulae and built-ins remained a research language.
  • Scoped negation stayed largely within N3 reasoners. Most rule systems in everyday use test absence against whatever store they run on, the unscoped form of Figure 42.3.
  • Quoting graphs was standardised twice over. Named graphs arrived with SPARQL in 2008 and became datasets in RDF 1.1 (February 2014); RDF-star work from 2021 onward added statements about statements.
  • Retrieval as inference stayed in research reasoners. Reasoners such as cwm and EYE implement fetching a document during a rule, but production systems load data first and reason afterwards.

Questions 4
