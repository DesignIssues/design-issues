Chapter 42

Notation3

2000

Source Notation3, a Design Issues note by Tim Berners-Lee, 2000-10-01. read the original

The short version

Notation3 (N3) is RDF plus braces, which quote a group of statements without asserting them. That makes rules possible, and statements about what a document says. To stay safe on an open Web it allows only one kind of not: this named document does not contain that fact. The data part became Turtle; the logic stayed experimental.

The question

Bob lives in Boston and writes that Boston is sunny. Alice lives in Adelaide and writes that Boston is cold. A program reading both must hold the two claims without believing either, then choose by who said it. Notation3, the notation for the logic of Chapter 40, adds to RDF a way to quote. Because others keep publishing, nothing they add may quietly overturn a conclusion (section 42.3). The design question is not what logic is true, but what logic survives being published.

42.1 Written for an open world

Whereas a closed system may be built based on a single knowledge base of believed facts, an open web-based system exists in an unbounded sea of interconnected information resources. This requires that an agent be aware of the provenance of information, and responsible for its disposition. Notation 3 Logic, 2000–2005

Provenance means where information came from. To say what a document said, one must quote a set of statements and match it against a pattern, so quotation is a starting condition, not an advanced feature, which is why the plan of Chapter 30 builds it in from the bottom.

A rule, worked through

The note’s first example puts the braces to work: a rule is a statement whose subject and object are quoted formulas, joined by the built-in property log:implies, written => for short.

42.2 The trap that shaped the design

Quotation plus reference brings self-reference, and self-reference brings paradox: a sentence such as this statement is false, which is true only if it is false. Berners-Lee is direct about the cost in first-order logic, the ordinary logic of all, some and not:

This quotation and reference, with its inevitable possibility of direct or indirect self-reference, if added directly to first order logic presents problems such as paradox traps. To avoid this, N3 logic has deliberately been kept to limited expressive power: it currently contains no general first order negation. Notation 3 Logic, 2000–2005

A language built to express anything is deliberately barred from a general not, so that it cannot tie itself in knots. Negated forms of many built-ins are provided instead, such as log:notIncludes (section 42.4).

42.3 Others must be able to publish without breaking what is built

The next goal concerns other people, not logic:

This means that one person should be able to express knowledge in N3 for a certain purpose, and later independently someone else reuse that knowledge for a different unforeseen purpose. Notation 3 Logic, 2000–2005

With the later use unknown, no one may assume a closed set of knowledge, and newcomers must be able to publish without affecting systems already built.

For example, take a booking system that concludes a guest has no allergy because none is on file. Someone records an allergy elsewhere, and the conclusion silently flips. A logic is monotonic when adding facts never withdraws a conclusion. New information may contradict old, but the conflict must be resolved openly, never by silently changing the old meaning.

The culprit, Berners-Lee says, is negation as failure: treating a sentence as false when it cannot be derived from the “current knowledge base”, a changing quantity referred to only implicitly.

42.4 A negation that stays safe: this document does not say it

The fix keeps the useful behaviour and removes the implicit reference. The only not allowed is about a named document or formula: it does not contain a given fact, and nothing in it leads to that fact. log:notIncludes does this, and the note calls it completely monotonic: no knowledge gained elsewhere can change its answer.

The positive twin, log:includes, checks a quoted formula for a statement. The opening’s weather rule uses it to look inside what each person wrote and believe only residents.

42.5 Fetching a document as a step of reasoning

In N3, fetching a document can be a step in reasoning. Berners-Lee defines the semantics of a document as the result of three plain steps: fetch it, read its statements, and add them to what the program already holds. log:semantics relates a document to that meaning.

A footnote defends it: “Philosophers may be distracted here into worrying about the meaning of meaning. At least we didn't call this function ‘meaning’!” To a reviewer aghast at defining semantics as retrieval, it replies:

I suspect however that the meaning of the paper to the reviewer could be considered quite equivalently the result of the process of retrieval of a representation of the paper, its parsing by the review, and its assimilation in terms of the reviewer's local logical framework: a similar though perhaps imperfect process. Notation 3 Logic, 2000–2005

It ends by apologising for the name log:semantics “for any heartache it may cause”.

42.6 What the separation bought

The conclusion records a practical result:

The separation between the Notation3 extensions to RDF and the logic properties has allowed N3 by itself to be used in many other applications directly, and to be used with other properties to provide other functionality such as the expression of patches (updates) Notation 3 Logic, 2000–2005

Notation and logic are separable, so the notation serves people who want none of the logic: the modularity of Chapter 19, applied by its author to his own design, which let the two halves go separate ways (see below). The note’s last design claim returns to the penguin of Chapter 41:

The use of log:notIncludes to allow default reasoning without non-monotonic behavior achieves a design goal for distributed rule systems. Notation 3 Logic, 2000–2005

What happened since

  • The notation won; the logic did not. Turtle, the data half of this notation without the logic, became a W3C Recommendation in February 2014 and is now the usual way to read and write graph data. The rules, formulae and built-ins remained a research language.
  • Absence tests went mainstream, unscoped by default. SPARQL 1.1 (March 2013) added FILTER NOT EXISTS, which tests absence against whatever dataset is being queried.
  • Quoting graphs was standardised twice over. Named graphs arrived with SPARQL in 2008 and became datasets in RDF 1.1 (February 2014); RDF-star work from 2021 onward added statements about statements.
  • Retrieval as inference stayed in research reasoners. Reasoners such as cwm (from 2000) and, later, EYE fetch a document during a rule, but most deployed triple stores load data first and reason afterwards.

The note reads more like a paper than an essay; the catalogue and its log: namespace date it to 2000, while the text is an August 2005 draft of the semantics, so quotations are cited 2000–2005.
